# Chapter 4: Ontological Frameworks for Anomaly Detection and Metric Emission

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter establishes the normative data governance framework for identifying, classifying, and managing outlier claims within enterprise information systems. The content herein defines the axiomatic structure required to map descriptive information content entities (ICE) to the processes that emit and trigger them. It provides a rigorous specification for the relational instantiation of these axioms, ensuring that audit trails for anomaly detection are both semantically valid and structurally sound. Organizations implementing these controls must ensure that all data instances conform to the logical constraints derived from the underlying ontology, specifically regarding the circular dependency between outlier claims and the alerts they generate.

## 4.2 Basic Outlier Claims

### 4.2.1 Conceptual Definition

The foundational unit of analysis in any anomaly detection framework is the Basic Outlier Claim. Under the defined ontology, this concept is formalized as a subclass of the `cco:DescriptiveICE` (Descriptive Information Content Entity). A DescriptiveICE is defined as an entity that is about some other entity; it is a generically dependent continuant that stands in relation to its independent continuant. In the context of this framework, the Basic Outlier Claim represents the raw assertion of an anomaly without the requisite attributes or process linkages that would necessitate immediate action. It serves as the passive data record, the "fact" of the anomaly, distinct from the "event" of its processing.

### 4.2.2 Regulatory Context and Implementation

From a compliance perspective, the Basic Outlier Claim fulfills the requirement for immutable logging. It captures the state of a system variable or a transactional property that deviates from the established baseline. However, because this class is defined solely by its descriptive nature and lacks the `sdg:hasAttributeSet` or `sdg:triggeredByMetric` properties, it acts as a staging entity. Claims in this state are considered "unverified" or "pre-alert." They must not be used to drive automated mitigation workflows until they are promoted to a more complex class that includes attribute validation.

The relational projection of this axiom is straightforward. The primary key is the unique identifier of the claim itself, while the `x` column represents the instance of the class (the claim). The absence of foreign keys in this schema highlights its isolation; it is a node in the graph that has not yet been connected to the process topology.

## 4.3 Attribute-Structured Outlier Claims

### 4.3.1 Conceptual Definition

The `outlier_claim_min_one_attribute` axiom introduces a critical constraint to the descriptive entity: the possession of attributes. Formally, this class is a subclass of `cco:DescriptiveICE` with the restriction `sdg:hasAttributeSet min 1 {Y:Class}`. This implies that for an outlier claim to be valid within this operational tier, it must be associated with at least one attribute. In the context of the provided schema, this attribute is modeled as a reference to an alert entity. This structural requirement ensures that an anomaly is not merely noted but is contextualized by the specific alert conditions or rule sets that define it.

### 4.3.2 Relational Topology and Constraints

The schema for `t_outlier_claim_min_one_attribute` enforces a mandatory relationship between the claim (`x`) and the attribute (`y`). The foreign key constraint `FOREIGN KEY (y) REFERENCES t_alert_triggered_by(id)` creates a directed edge from the claim to the alert. This dependency signifies that the claim is substantiated by the alert. In practice, this means that the data integrity of the outlier claim is contingent upon the existence of the triggering alert record. If an alert is purged or archived, the associated attribute-structured claim must be reviewed or resolved to maintain referential integrity.

This structure supports complex audit queries where investigators must trace an anomaly back to the specific trigger mechanism. By enforcing a "minimum 1" cardinality, the framework prevents the creation of "orphan" anomalies—claims that exist without a defined cause or attribute set.

## 4.4 Alert Triggering Mechanisms

### 4.4.1 Conceptual Definition

The `alert_triggered_by` axiom defines the processual aspect of the framework. Here, the class `{X}` is defined as a subclass of `bfo:0000015` (a Process), which is `sdg:triggeredByMetric some {Y:Class}`. This establishes a causal chain: a process (the alert generation) occurs only when triggered by a specific metric. In this ontology, the metric that triggers the alert is the Outlier Claim itself. This creates a feedback loop where the descriptive entity (the claim) acts as the catalyst for the processual entity (the alert).

### 4.4.2 The Metric-Process Cycle

The verbalization "{X} is something that triggered by metric {Y}" is fundamental to the event-driven architecture of the monitoring system. The `t_alert_triggered_by` table captures the execution of the alerting logic. The `x` column denotes the specific alert process instance, while the `y` column denotes the metric (the outlier claim) that initiated it.

Crucially, the schema defines a circular dependency: `t_outlier_claim_min_one_attribute` references `t_alert_triggered_by`, and `t_alert_triggered_by` references `t_outlier_claim_min_one_attribute`. This circularity is not a design flaw but a representation of a tight coupling in the operational logic: the claim is defined by the alert (its attribute), and the alert is defined by the claim (its trigger). In a relational database, this requires careful insertion logic, typically involving the deferral of constraint checking or the use of temporary staging tables. For the auditor, this cycle represents a closed loop of evidence: the claim proves the alert's necessity, and the alert proves the claim's validity.

## 4.5 Metric Emission Subclass

### 4.5.1 Conceptual Definition

The `metric_emission_subclass` axiom describes the origin of the metrics within the system. It posits that a class `{X}` (a Process) is a subclass of `bfo:0000015` that `sdg:emitsMetric some {Y:Class}`. This addresses the "upstream" generation of data. While the alert is triggered by a metric, that metric must be emitted by some observable process or system event. This axiom traces the anomaly back to its source, ensuring that every outlier claim is ultimately grounded in a real-world process occurrence (e.g., a database transaction, a network packet receipt, or a user login).

### 4.5.2 Source Traceability and Audit

The schema `t_metric_emission_subclass` links the emitting process (`x`) to the metric it emits (`y`). The foreign key constraint `FOREIGN KEY (y) REFERENCES t_outlier_claim_min_one_attribute(id)` ensures that the only metrics recorded are those that have been formally identified as attribute-structured outlier claims. This prevents the system from being cluttered with metrics that do not result in claims.

From a governance standpoint, this table is essential for root cause analysis. When an anomaly is detected, the analyst can query the metric ID to find the emitting process. This distinguishes between "false positives" generated by sensor noise (where the emitting process might be faulty) and "true positives" generated by actual business logic events (where the emitting process is legitimate but the behavior is anomalous).

## 4.6 Data Aggregation

### 4.6.1 Conceptual Definition

The final concept, `aggregation_basic`, returns to the category of `cco:DescriptiveICE`. While basic outlier claims represent atomic events, aggregations represent summaries, roll-ups, or statistical derivations of those events. An aggregation is a descriptive entity that is about other data entities. It does not necessarily trigger an alert on its own, nor does it have the same tight coupling to the process loop. Instead, it serves a reporting function, providing high-level visibility into the volume, frequency, or magnitude of anomalies.

### 4.6.2 Reporting and Compliance Metrics

The `t_aggregation_basic` table is structurally simple, consisting only of an ID and the instance reference. Its simplicity belies its importance in regulatory reporting. Compliance frameworks often require evidence of "ongoing monitoring" and "trend analysis." While the individual tables (Claims, Alerts, Emissions) provide the granular evidence of specific incidents, the Aggregation table provides the macro-level proof that the monitoring system is active and effective over time.

Aggregations are typically generated by batch processes that run against the `t_outlier_claim_basic` or `t_outlier_claim_min_one_attribute` tables. They might calculate the total number of high-value transaction outliers per day or the average latency of alerts. Because they are DescriptiveICEs, they are part of the permanent record and must be retained according to the organization's data retention policies.
