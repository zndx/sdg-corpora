# Chapter 4: Ontological Mapping of Regulatory Directives to Control Frameworks

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter establishes the formal axiomatic mapping between the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) legal constructs and the DirectiveICE control ontology. The objective is to provide a deterministic, evidence-backed methodology for translating regulatory articles into executable governance controls. The definitions herein are derived from the OWL ontology axioms governing the relationship between directive classes, risk mitigation strategies, and priority assignments. Compliance officers and system architects must utilize these mappings to ensure that technical implementations align strictly with the semantic constraints imposed by the regulatory framework. The following sections detail the specific subclass relationships, risk addressal properties, and prioritization schemas required for a validated compliance posture.

## 4.2 GDPR Article Subclassifications

The foundational axiom for regulatory alignment defines the subclass relationship between specific control implementations and the broader GDPR articles they instantiate. Formally, this is expressed as the axiom `gdpr_article_subclass`, which posits that a control class `X` is a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` and specifically satisfies the requirements of `sdg:gdprArticle` class `Y`.

In practical terms, this mapping asserts that any technical or administrative control defined within the enterprise architecture must be traceable to a specific article of the GDPR. This is not merely a categorization exercise but a structural requirement for auditability. When a control is instantiated, it must inherit the normative obligations of the article it represents. For example, a "Data Breach Notification Workflow" is not a generic process; it is a distinct subclass of Article 33 (Notification of a personal data breach to a supervisory authority).

The relational schema for this axiom enforces a strict dependency. The primary key `id` serves as the unique identifier for the subclass relationship, while the foreign key constraint on column `y` mandates that the referenced Article class must itself be defined within the threat class context (see Section 4.3). This circular dependency ensures that Articles and Controls are defined in mutual context.

**Table 4.1: GDPR Article Subclass Mapping**

| id | x (Control Class) | y (GDPR Article Class) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| sub_art32_01 | TechnicalSecurityMeasure | Article32_SecurityOfProcessing |
| sub_art33_01 | BreachNotificationControl | Article33_NotificationDuty |
| sub_art35_01 | DataProtectionImpactAssessment | Article35_DataProtectionImpactAssessment |
| sub_art17_01 | RightToErasureControl | Article17_RightToErasure |

The data in Table 4.1 illustrates how specific control classes (`x`) are bound to their regulatory origins (`y`). The `id` column provides a stable reference for cross-table joining, ensuring that downstream risk assessments can trace back to the specific legal basis.

## 4.3 Exclusive Risk Addressal by Controls

A critical distinction in governance frameworks is the differentiation between controls that address a risk as part of a broader suite and those that address a risk exclusively. The axiom `control_only_addresses_threat_class` captures this exclusivity. It states that class `X` (a DirectiveICE) addresses risk `Y` via the property `sdg:addressesRisk` with the quantifier `only`.

This semantic restriction implies that the control `X` has no other function or scope outside of addressing risk `Y`. In audit scenarios, this allows for high-precision targeting of compliance gaps. If a threat `Y` is identified, the deployment of control `X` guarantees mitigation for `Y` without introducing scope creep or conflicting functionalities.

The relational structure for this axiom mirrors the circular dependency found in Section 4.2. The foreign key constraint requires that the risk class `y` being addressed must correspond to an existing GDPR article subclass identifier. This design enforces the logic that exclusive risks are defined in the context of the specific regulatory articles that mandate their management.

**Table 4.2: Exclusive Risk Addressal Mapping**

| id | x (Control Class) | y (Risk / Article Context) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| excl_risk_01 | ConfidentialityPreservation | sub_art32_01 |
| excl_risk_02 | BreachContingencyProtocol | sub_art33_01 |
| excl_risk_03 | NecessityAssessment | sub_art35_01 |
| excl_risk_04 | DataPurgeMechanism | sub_art17_01 |

In Table 4.2, the `x` column represents controls with a singular focus. For instance, `ConfidentialityPreservation` is linked via `y` to the `sub_art32_01` identifier. This linkage verifies that the control's exclusive purpose is to satisfy the security of processing requirements defined in that specific subclass instance.

## 4.4 General Risk Addressal

While exclusive controls are precise, the broader governance landscape relies on controls that address one or more risks without the "only" restriction. The axiom `control_addresses_risk` utilizes the existential quantifier (`some`) to define that class `X` is a DirectiveICE that addresses risk `Y`.

This axiom covers the majority of standard technical controls, such as firewalls, encryption standards, and access management systems, which typically mitigate multiple vectors of risk simultaneously. For example, an "Encryption-at-Rest" control addresses risks related to data confidentiality, unauthorized access, and data exfiltration. The ontology captures this by linking the control class to the relevant risk class.

The schema for this table references the GDPR article subclass table via the `y` column, anchoring the general risk addressal back to the specific regulatory requirement. This ensures that even general controls are audited within the context of the articles they support.

**Table 4.3: General Risk Addressal Mapping**

| id | x (Control Class) | y (GDPR Article Context) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| gen_risk_01 | MultiFactorAuthentication | sub_art32_01 |
| gen_risk_02 | SecureDataTransmissionProtocol | sub_art32_01 |
| gen_risk_03 | IncidentResponseTeam | sub_art33_01 |
| gen_risk_04 | PrivacyByDesignArchitecture | sub_art25_01 |
| gen_risk_05 | VendorRiskManagement | sub_art28_01 |

As shown in Table 4.3, `MultiFactorAuthentication` (`x`) is mapped to `sub_art32_01` (`y`). This indicates that while this control addresses various authentication risks, its inclusion in the governance framework is justified and recorded under the Security of Processing article subclass.

## 4.5 Control Mitigation Logic

The `control_subclass` axiom refines the relationship between controls and the threats they mitigate. It asserts that class `X` is a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` and that it `sdg:mitigates` class `Y`. This differs from the "addresses" property by focusing specifically on the reduction of impact or likelihood associated with a threat.

Mitigation is an active outcome. In compliance terms, a control is only effective if it demonstrably reduces the risk profile to an acceptable level. This axiom allows the organization to model the mitigation chain—linking a specific control implementation directly to the threat class it neutralizes.

The foreign key structure again anchors the threat class `y` to the GDPR article subclass table. This creates a traceable path: the Control mitigates the Threat, which is defined within the scope of the GDPR Article.

**Table 4.4: Control Mitigation Mapping**

| id | x (Control Class) | y (Threat / Article Context) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| mit_ctrl_01 | AnomalyDetectionSystem | sub_art32_01 |
| mit_ctrl_02 | DataMinimizationTool | sub_art25_01 |
| mit_ctrl_03 | RightsRequestVerification | sub_art17_01 |
| mit_ctrl_04 | ConsentManagementPlatform | sub_art07_01 |
| mit_ctrl_05 | PseudonymizationEngine | sub_art32_01 |

Table 4.4 demonstrates the mitigation logic. The `AnomalyDetectionSystem` (`x`) is recorded as mitigating the threats associated with `sub_art32_01` (`y`). This entry provides the evidence required during an audit to prove that technical measures are actively mitigating the risks identified in the regulatory assessment.

## 4.6 Requirement Prioritization

Resource allocation in compliance is governed by the `requirement_has_priority` axiom. This axiom classifies `X` (a DirectiveICE requirement) as having a priority `Y`. Prioritization is essential for maintaining a dynamic compliance posture, ensuring that high-risk or legally mandatory obligations are addressed before lower-priority enhancements.

The priority classes (e.g., "High," "Medium," "Low") or specific regulatory deadlines are modeled as the target class `Y`. By formalizing this relationship, the ontology supports automated compliance scoring and risk-based decision-making. The schema links the priority assignment back to the GDPR article subclass, ensuring that prioritization is always applied to specific regulatory mandates, not abstract concepts.

**Table 4.5: Requirement Priority Mapping**

| id | x (Requirement Class) | y (Priority Class) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| prio_req_01 | BreachNotificationTimeline | HighPriority |
| prio_req_02 | DataSubjectAccessRequest | MediumPriority |
| prio_req_03 | DPIACompletion | HighPriority |
| prio_req_04 | RecordOfProcessingActivities | HighPriority |
| prio_req_05 | DPOAppointment | CriticalPriority |

In Table 4.5, the `x` column lists specific requirement classes derived from the ontology, while `y` assigns the necessary priority level. For instance, `BreachNotificationTimeline` is assigned `HighPriority`, reflecting the strict 72-hour mandate imposed by the regulation. This structured data enables automated governance tools to flag overdue or under-resourced high-priority items.
