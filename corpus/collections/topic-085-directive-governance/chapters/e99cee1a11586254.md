# Governance Frameworks: Mapping Regulatory Directives to Control Ontologies

## 1.0 Preamble and Scope

This chapter delineates the formal mapping between General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) articles and the internal control ontology defined under the DirectiveICE framework. The objective is to establish a rigorous, auditable lineage between regulatory requirements and technical or administrative controls. The following sections provide a detailed analysis of the axiomatized relationships governing these mappings, specifically focusing on how specific classes of controls address identified risks, mitigate threats, and prioritize enforcement actions. The data tables embedded herein serve as the evidentiary basis for these assertions, illustrating the deterministic derivation of relational schemas from the underlying ontology.

## 1.1 GDPR Article Subclassifications

The foundational element of the governance framework is the classification of regulatory mandates into distinct subclasses. In the context of the DirectiveICE ontology, a specific class of requirements is defined by its adherence to a particular GDPR article. This relationship is not merely associative but structural; the requirement class *is* a manifestation of the article's stipulations within the operational environment.

Formally, we define the axiom such that a Class {X} is a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` and satisfies the condition `sdg:gdprArticle some {Y}`. In natural language, this asserts that {X} is a requirement class that is substantively defined by GDPR Article {Y}. This classification allows auditors to trace a specific control or requirement back to its legal basis without ambiguity.

To illustrate this, consider the classification of security processing measures. These are not generic security controls but are specifically instantiated as requirements because they fall under the purview of Article 32 of the GDPR. The relational schema for this axiom captures the unique identifier of the requirement class, the class name itself, and the specific article identifier that governs it. It is critical to note that the foreign key structure in this schema links the article reference to a specific control class, reinforcing the interdependence of regulatory text and operational implementation.

The reference {Y} serves as a foreign key linking to the control definitions, ensuring that every regulatory requirement is anchored to a tangible control mechanism.*

## 1.2 Control Constraints and Threat Addressal

Moving from the definition of requirements to the specificity of controls, we must examine the constraints placed on control classes regarding the risks they address. The ontology distinguishes between controls that address a risk as part of a broader suite and those that address a risk *only* (exclusively). This distinction is vital for risk assessment accuracy; a control that claims exclusivity in addressing a threat implies that failure of that control results in the total absence of mitigation for that specific threat.

The axiom for this concept is defined as: Class {X} SubClassOf `cco:DirectiveICE`, `sdg:addressesRisk only {Y}`. This verbalizes to: {X} is a control that addresses risk {Y} and no other risk within the current scope. This "only" restriction (`only`) in the Manchester syntax is a logical universal quantification, implying that if an instance of {X} addresses a risk, that risk must be an instance of {Y}.

In practice, this is applied to highly specialized technical controls. For example, a specific cryptographic standard might be defined as addressing the risk of "Unauthorized Disclosure" *only*. It does not, by itself, address "Data Loss" or "System Availability." The relational schema for this axiom enforces a strict relationship where the risk addressed by the control is validated against the originating article subclass, creating a closed loop of verification.

**Table 1-2: Exclusive Control-to-Risk Mappings**

| Control ID | Control Class (X) | Risk Addressed (Y) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| CTRL-ENC-001 | EncryptionStandard | REQ-SEC-001 |
| CTRL-AUTH-002 | BiometricAuthentication | REQ-ACC-002 |
| CTRL-HASH-003 | ChecksumValidation | REQ-INT-003 |
| CTRL-RED-004 | RedundantPowerSupply | REQ-AV-004 |

*Table 1-2 details the `control_only_addresses_threat_class` axiom. The Control Class {X} is linked to a specific Risk {Y}. The foreign key constraint ensures that the Risk {Y} corresponds to an entry in the GDPR Article Subclass table, maintaining the traceability from the specific control back to the regulatory driver.*

## 1.3 General Risk Addressal by Controls

In contrast to the exclusive constraints defined in Section 1.2, the governance framework must also accommodate controls that address risks as a subset of their functionality (existential quantification). These are broader controls that mitigate one or more risks but are not defined solely by a single threat vector.

The relevant axiom is: Class {X} SubClassOf `cco:DirectiveICE`, `sdg:addressesRisk some {Y}`. This asserts that {X} is a control that addresses risk {Y}, among potentially others. The `some` operator indicates that the relationship is necessary but not necessarily exclusive.

This categorization is essential for complex controls such as "Intrusion Detection Systems" or "Security Awareness Training." An IDS, for instance, addresses the risk of "Unauthorized Access" (`some`), but it may also facilitate "Incident Response." Therefore, it cannot be classified under the strict `only` constraint. The relational schema for this axiom maintains the link to the governing article subclass, ensuring that even broad controls are traceable to a specific compliance mandate.

| CTRL-POL-103 | DataRetentionPolicy | REQ-SEC-001 |
| CTRL-VPN-104 | VirtualPrivateNetwork | REQ-ACC-002 |

*Table 1-3 illustrates the `control_addresses_risk` axiom. Here, the Control Class {X} addresses the Risk {Y} in a general capacity. The linkage to the Article Subclass (via the Risk ID) confirms that these controls are deployed in satisfaction of specific regulatory requirements, such as those found in Article 32.*

## 1.4 Control Subclass and Mitigation Logic

The ultimate purpose of a control within the DirectiveICE framework is mitigation. While "addressing" a risk implies a defensive posture, "mitigating" a threat implies an active reduction in the likelihood or impact of that threat. This section defines the subclass of controls specifically designated for mitigation activities.

The axiom is formalized as: Class {X} SubClassOf `cco:DirectiveICE`, `sdg:mitigates some {Y}`. We interpret this as: {X} is a control that mitigates {Y}. The object {Y} in this context refers to the threat or vulnerability class defined within the scope of the GDPR article.

This distinction allows the governance framework to differentiate between passive controls (which merely address or acknowledge a risk) and active mitigations (which reduce the attack surface or impact). For instance, while a "Logging Policy" addresses the risk of non-repudiation, an "Automated Alerting System" mitigates the threat of delayed incident response. The relational structure ensures that every mitigation claim is anchored to a valid requirement class derived from the primary regulation.

**Table 1-4: Control Mitigation Mappings**

| Mitigation ID | Control Class (X) | Threat Mitigated (Y) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| MIT-AES-201 | AES256Encryption | REQ-SEC-001 |
| MIT-RBAC-202 | RoleBasedAccessControl | REQ-ACC-002 |
| MIT-WORM-203 | WORMStorage | REQ-INT-003 |
| MIT-CLUSTER-204 | ServerClustering | REQ-AV-004 |

*Table 1-4 presents the `control_subclass` axiom. The Control Class {X} is explicitly defined as mitigating the Threat {Y}. The foreign key constraint linking {Y} to the GDPR Article Subclass table ensures that the mitigation effort is directly contributing to the compliance objectives outlined in the relevant article.*

## 1.5 Requirement Prioritization

Finally, the governance framework must incorporate a mechanism for prioritizing requirements and their associated controls. Not all controls carry equal weight in terms of compliance urgency or criticality to operations. The ontology defines a priority hierarchy to guide resource allocation and audit sequencing.

The axiom for this concept is: Class {X} SubClassOf `cco:DirectiveICE`, `sdg:hasPriority some {Y}`. This verbalizes to: {X} is a requirement that has priority {Y}. The priority class {Y} can range from "Statutory/Mandatory" to "Advisory/Low."

This formalization allows the organization to dynamically adjust its compliance posture. For example, requirements derived from Articles regarding the rights of data subjects (e.g., access requests) might be assigned a higher priority class than those regarding record-keeping. The relational schema links the priority assignment back to the original requirement class, ensuring that prioritization is not an arbitrary exercise but a documented attribute of the compliance artifact itself.
