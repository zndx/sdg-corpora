# Chapter 4: Ontological Mapping of Regulatory Directives to Control Frameworks

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the formal ontological structures required to map technical governance controls to statutory regulatory frameworks, specifically focusing on the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). The objective is to establish a rigorous, evidence-based methodology for defining directives, where every control is axiomatically linked to specific legal articles and risk categories. By utilizing the Web Ontology Language (OWL) and the Common Control Ontology (CCO), organizations can derive deterministic relational schemas that enforce data integrity and ensure that compliance artifacts are auditable and logically consistent. The following sections detail five core axioms that govern these relationships, supported by relational data tables that instantiate these theoretical constructs within a practical governance environment.

## 4.2 GDPR Article Subclassification

The foundational step in regulatory compliance is the explicit binding of internal directives to specific legal statutes. In the ontology, this is governed by the axiom `gdpr_article_subclass`, which asserts that a Directive Class (`X`) is a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` and specifically satisfies a GDPR Article (`Y`). This relationship is not merely associative but hierarchical; the directive inherits the normative obligations of the article.

Formally, the Manchester syntax for this axiom is expressed as `Class: {X:Class} SubClassOf: cco:DirectiveICE, sdg:gdprArticle some {Y:Class}`. This verbalizes to: "{X} is something that gdpr article {Y}". In practice, this means that the existence of the directive within the governance framework is contingent upon its alignment with the referenced article. This axiom prevents "orphan" controls that do not map to legal requirements and ensures that the scope of the directive is strictly bounded by the text of the law.

The relational projection of this axiom, `t_gdpr_article_subclass`, serves as a junction table where the primary key identifies the specific directive instance, and the foreign key (`y`) enforces the link to the threat class context defined by the article. This structure ensures that every directive is anchored to a regulatory artifact, facilitating automated gap analysis during audits.

## 4.3 Universal Risk Addressing (Restriction)

While the previous axiom establishes the legal basis for a directive, it does not define the operational boundaries of the controls that execute it. The axiom `control_only_addresses_threat_class` introduces a universal restriction, denoted by the `only` (`only`) construct in OWL. This axiom asserts that a Control Class (`X`) is a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` and addresses Risk `Y` exclusively.

The Manchester syntax is `Class: {X:Class} SubClassOf: cco:DirectiveICE, sdg:addressesRisk only {Y:Class}`. Verbalized, this means: "{X} is something that addresses risk {Y}". The critical distinction here is the logical implication of the universal restriction: if an instance of this control addresses any risk, that risk *must* be a member of class `Y`. This is a powerful constraint for scope management. It prohibits "function creep," where a control designed for encryption might inadvertently be applied to access logging, thereby violating its defined scope.

In the relational schema `t_control_only_addresses_threat_class`, the foreign key constraint creates a bidirectional validation loop with the GDPR article table. This circular reference (`y` in this table references the `id` in the article table, and vice versa) enforces a tight coupling between the legal article and the specific threat context. It ensures that the threat being addressed is exactly the one defined by the regulatory article, creating a closed logical loop that is robust against semantic drift.

## 4.4 Existential Risk Addressing

In contrast to the universal restriction, the governance framework must accommodate controls that are broader in scope or that address a subset of risks without excluding the possibility of addressing others. The axiom `control_addresses_risk` utilizes an existential restriction (`some`), which asserts that a Control Class (`X`) addresses *at least one* instance of Risk Class (`Y`).

The axiom is defined as `Class: {X:Class} SubClassOf: cco:DirectiveICE, sdg:addressesRisk some {Y:Class}`. The verbalization is: "{X} is something that addresses risk {Y}". This is a weaker logical constraint than the universal restriction; it does not preclude the control from addressing other risks not explicitly classified under `Y`. This distinction is vital for general-purpose controls, such as a "Firewall," which may address risks defined in Article 32 (security of processing) but also risks associated with Article 33 (integrity of breach reporting logs).

The relational schema `t_control_addresses_risk` links specific control implementations to the directive articles. By referencing the `t_gdpr_article_subclass` table, it ensures that while the control may have broad applicability, its compliance relevance is formally recorded against specific articles. This allows auditors to query which controls contribute to the compliance posture for a given article, even if those controls are not exclusively limited to that article.

## 4.5 Control Mitigation Subclassification

The ultimate purpose of a control is to mitigate a threat. The ontology captures this through the `control_subclass` axiom, which classifies a Control (`X`) based on its capacity to mitigate a specific Threat or Risk (`Y`). This relationship moves beyond simple "addressing" (which may imply monitoring or detection) to "mitigating," which implies a reduction in impact or likelihood.

The Manchester syntax is `Class: {X:Class} SubClassOf: cco:DirectiveICE, sdg:mitigates some {Y:Class}`. Verbalized: "{X} is something that mitigates {Y}". This axiom is essential for risk scoring methodologies. By classifying controls based on what they mitigate, the framework can automatically calculate residual risk levels by subtracting the efficacy of the mitigating controls from the inherent risk profile.

The `t_control_subclass` table provides the data structure for this calculation. Each entry represents a specific mitigation activity tied to a regulatory directive. The foreign key constraint ensures that the mitigation is relevant to the specific threat context defined in the GDPR article table. This allows for granular reporting, such as "List all mitigating controls relevant to Article 32," providing immediate evidence of compliance posture to external auditors.

## 4.6 Requirement Prioritization

Not all compliance requirements carry equal weight or urgency. The `requirement_has_priority` axiom introduces a mechanism to stratify directives based on priority levels. This axiom asserts that a Requirement Class (`X`) has a Priority (`Y`). This is critical for resource allocation, ensuring that high-priority legal obligations (e.g., those preventing fundamental rights violations) are addressed before lower-priority procedural requirements.

The axiom is expressed as `Class: {X:Class} SubClassOf: cco:DirectiveICE, sdg:hasPriority some {Y:Class}`. Verbalized: "{X} is something that has priority {Y}". In the context of the ontology, `Y` would typically be a class such as `HighPriority`, `MediumPriority`, or `LowPriority`, potentially derived from the risk impact assessment.

The `t_requirement_has_priority` table operationalizes this stratification. By linking the requirement to the directive via the foreign key, the organization can generate a prioritized compliance roadmap. For instance, filtering this table for "High Priority" allows the Chief Information Security Officer (CISO) to immediately identify which directives require immediate budget and attention, ensuring that the most critical legal liabilities are managed first.
