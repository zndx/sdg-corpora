# Governance Artifacts and Directive Structures in Compliance Frameworks

## 1. Scope

This chapter defines the formal ontological structures underlying compliance artifacts, requirements, and directives within a regulated information system. It establishes the necessary conditions for an entity to be classified as a descriptive information content entity, an artifact, or a directive. The provisions herein detail the relational dependencies between these classes, specifically how artifacts are composed of requirement parts, and how requirements function as directives that specify targets and are, in turn, authored by other directives. These definitions are binding for the construction of audit trails and the verification of governance metadata.

## 1. Descriptive Information Content Entities

The foundational unit of information within the governance framework is the Descriptive Information Content Entity (DescriptiveICE). This class encompasses any entity that exists primarily to convey data, represent a state of affairs, or document a process, as opposed to physical objects or agents. In the context of compliance auditing, a DescriptiveICE is not merely a string of characters but a semantically typed resource that anchors the evidentiary chain.

Formally, a class is designated as a DescriptiveICE if it is a subclass of the general Information Content Entity (ICE) category. This classification ensures that the entity is recognized by the system as a carrier of information capable of being referenced, archived, and audited. Examples of such entities include audit logs, system configuration baselines, and incident reports. These entities do not possess agency; rather, they serve as the passive substrate upon which governance actions are recorded.

To illustrate the classification of these entities, the following registry lists distinct classes that have been axiomatically defined as subclasses of DescriptiveICE. Each entry represents a category of documentation utilized within the compliance framework.

**Table 1.1: Registry of Descriptive Information Content Entities**

| id | x |
| :--- | :--- |
| desc_001 | AuditLogRecord |
| desc_002 | SystemConfigurationBaseline |
| desc_003 | SecurityIncidentReport |
| desc_004 | PolicyInterpretationMemo |

The table above demonstrates the variety of formats that descriptive entities may take. While the physical medium (e.g., a PDF file or a database row) may differ, the ontological classification remains consistent. This consistency allows downstream processes to treat `AuditLogRecord` and `SecurityIncidentReport` as valid targets for descriptive queries without needing to parse their internal structure.

## 2. Artifacts and Constituent Requirements

In the physical and digital realm, governance materials are frequently aggregated into composite structures known as Artifacts. An Artifact is defined as a discrete, identifiable object—either physical or digital—that persists over time. Within the ontology, an Artifact is not merely a container; it is structurally defined by the relationship it holds with its constituent parts. Specifically, an Artifact is equivalent to the intersection of the class `cco:Artifact` and the object property `bfo:0000051` (has part).

This definition implies that for an entity to be recognized as a valid Artifact in this system, it must possess at least one part that is formally classified as a Requirement. This creates a strict dependency: an empty Artifact, or an Artifact composed of non-requirement parts (such as loose paper or unstructured binary data), falls outside the scope of this governance definition. The Artifact serves as the delivery mechanism or the binding unit for the Requirements that enforce compliance.

Consider a "Governance Binder" or a "Compliance Dossier." These are Artifacts because they are physical or digital aggregates containing specific, actionable Requirements. The relationship is compositional; the Artifact ceases to be a complete governance object if the Requirements are removed. The following table details instances of Artifacts and their associated Requirement parts, validating the axiom that an Artifact is defined by its possession of these specific components.

| :--- | :--- | :--- |

## 3. Requirements as Specification Directives

A Requirement represents a specific obligation or constraint within the governance framework. It is not merely a suggestion; it is a type of Directive Information Content Entity (DirectiveICE) that carries the force of a rule. The ontology defines a Requirement through a complex equivalence: it is a DirectiveICE that *specifies* a target and *applies to* a context.

The specification relationship (`sdg:specifies`) indicates the content of the Requirement—what is actually being mandated. The application relationship (`sdg:appliesTo`) indicates the scope of the Requirement—where or to whom the mandate is relevant. This dual structure allows the system to distinguish between the *rule* and the *subject* of the rule.

Crucially, the schema establishes that the target specified by a Requirement (`y`) must be another Directive. This creates a layered governance structure where a high-level Requirement may specify a more granular Directive or operational procedure. For instance, a Requirement regarding "Access Control" might specify a "Password Policy Directive." The Requirement acts as the abstract mandate, while the Directive provides the specific mechanism.

The following table enumerates specific Requirements, identifying the Directive they specify and the operational context to which they apply.

**Table 3.1: Requirement Specification and Application Matrix**

| id | x | y | z |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| req_001 | PhysicalAccessControlRequirement | dir_001 | ServerRoom |
| req_002 | DataRetentionRequirement | dir_002 | FinancialDatabase |
| req_003 | ChangeManagementRequirement | dir_003 | ProductionEnvironment |
| req_004 | EncryptionStandardRequirement | dir_004 | TransmissionChannel |

In this matrix, `req_001` (PhysicalAccessControlRequirement) serves as the governing mandate. It specifies `dir_001` (presumably a specific directive detailing how access is managed) and applies to the `ServerRoom`. This separation allows the Requirement to remain stable even if the specific Directive (`dir_001`) is updated or revised. The Requirement enforces the *need* for a directive, while the Directive provides the *form* of that directive.

## 4. Directives: Authorship and Application

The Directive class represents the actionable unit of governance. While a Requirement defines the obligation, the Directive defines the execution. A Directive is formally defined as a DirectiveICE that is *authored by* an entity and *applies to* a context.

The authorship relationship (`sdg:authoredBy`) is critical for establishing provenance and authority. In this ontology, the author of a Directive is not necessarily a human agent but can be another governance entity, specifically a Requirement. This reflects a top-down derivation model where a Requirement acts as the authorizing source for a Directive. The Requirement "writes" the Directive by necessitating its existence and defining its scope.

Consequently, the system enforces a recursive dependency: a Directive is authored by a Requirement, and that Requirement specifies a Directive. This loop ensures that all Directives are traceable back to a higher-level Requirement, and all Requirements are grounded in specific Directives that implement them. This structure eliminates orphaned directives and ensures a complete chain of custody for all governance logic.
