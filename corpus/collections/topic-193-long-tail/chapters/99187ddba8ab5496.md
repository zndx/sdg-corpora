# Chapter 7: Ontological Formalization of Governance Directives and Controls

This chapter establishes the formal ontological framework for defining, governing, and enforcing information security policies within a regulated enterprise architecture. It delineates the structural relationships between directive information content entities, descriptive claims, and the artifacts they govern. The definitions provided herein align with the Basic Formal Ontology (BFO) and are designed to ensure semantic interoperability across compliance, audit, and governance domains.

## 7.1 Basic Directive Information Content Entities

The foundational unit of governance is the Directive Information Content Entity (ICE). A Directive ICE is defined as a generically dependent continuant that conveys an instruction, prescription, or obligation. Unlike descriptive information, which asserts a state of affairs, a directive imposes a modality of necessity—it dictates what *must* be the case. In a compliance context, these entities manifest as policies, standards, procedures, and guidelines.

The axiom `policy_basic` posits that any entity classified as a Directive ICE inherently carries the obligation of governance. This classification is not merely taxonomic but functional; it separates prescriptive content (rules) from descriptive content (facts). For instance, a document titled "Acceptable Use Policy" is a Directive ICE because it prescribes behavior, whereas a "System Audit Log" is a Descriptive ICE because it records historical events.

## 7.2 Ownership and Accountability in Controls

While directives define the rules of governance, controls are the specific mechanisms implemented to enforce those rules. A critical aspect of control governance is the assignment of ownership. The axiom `control_min_one_owner` asserts that any Directive ICE functioning as a control must be associated with a minimum of one `cco:Person` (the owner).

This requirement ensures that there is no "orphan" control within the governance framework. Every technical, administrative, or physical control must have a designated individual accountable for its implementation, maintenance, and effectiveness. This linkage transforms a static document into an operational responsibility. The owner is not necessarily the executor of the control but is the party with the authority and answerability for its existence.

## 7.3 Artifact-Centric Governance Scopes

Directives often vary in terms of the breadth of their application. The axiom `policy_only_governs_artifacts` defines a specific subclass of directives whose scope is strictly limited to `cco:Artifact` entities. An Artifact, in this ontology, refers to a physical object or digital information entity that is the product of a process.

This distinction is vital for separating "process policies" (which govern workflows and human behaviors) from "artifact policies" (which govern the state, integrity, or handling of objects). For example, a "Source Code Retention Policy" governs artifacts (the code files), whereas a "Code Review Procedure" governs a process. By constraining the scope to artifacts, the ontology ensures that validation mechanisms check the properties of objects rather than the actions of agents.

## 7.4 Evidentiary Support for Descriptive Claims

In the governance lifecycle, the efficacy of a directive is often assessed through descriptive claims—assertions about the current state of the system. The axiom `claim_min_one_evidence` formalizes this by stating that a Descriptive ICE (a claim) must be supported by a minimum of one evidence item.

In this relational model, the evidence (`y`) required to support a claim (`x`) is structurally linked to the enforcement mechanism defined in the subsequent section. This creates a rigorous chain of custody: a claim cannot exist in isolation; it must be anchored to evidence. Furthermore, the schema dictates that this evidence pointer (`y`) must reference a valid enforcement record (a `policy_min_one_enforcer` entry). This implies that evidence is not merely raw data but is recognized as such only within the context of an enforcement framework.

## 7.5 Enforcement Mechanisms for Directives

The final component of the governance framework is the enforcement of the directive itself. The axiom `policy_min_one_enforcer` stipulates that a Directive ICE (`x`) must be enforced by a minimum of one entity (`y`). This entity acts as the active agent or mechanism that compels compliance with the directive.

The relational structure reveals a recursive validation loop: the enforcer (`y`) of a policy is, in this schema, identified by a Claim ID. This implies that the "enforcer" is effectively the assertion that the policy is being followed (the claim). Thus, the enforcement of the policy is realized through the generation of a claim, which is in turn supported by the policy's own existence. This circular dependency captures the continuous nature of compliance: a policy is enforced by the monitoring claims (e.g., "System is Compliant") that verify its adherence, and those claims gain their validity from the policy they enforce.
