# Chapter 4: Ontological Constraints for Auditable Kernel Entities

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the formal ontological requirements for classifying and managing system calls within high-assurance computing environments. Specifically, it addresses the intersection of kernel-level observability and compliance frameworks, defining how specific information content entities must be structured to satisfy rigorous audit mandates. The discussion herein is grounded in the Basic Formal Ontology (BFO) and the Common Core Ontologies (CCO), providing a deterministic mapping between abstract ontological axioms and concrete relational data structures used in security auditing. The objective is to establish a reproducible standard for identifying "Designative Information Content Entities" that are subject to process-based auditing, ensuring that evidence collection mechanisms adhere to the structural integrity required by regulatory bodies.

## 4.2 Designative Information Content Entities (ICE)

In the context of kernel auditing, a fundamental distinction must be drawn between raw data artifacts and entities that possess semantic designation. A **Designative Information Content Entity** (cco:DesignativeICE) is defined as an information-bearing entity that stands for, or refers to, another entity within the system. Unlike generic data blocks, a designative entity functions as a signifier, intended to convey specific meaning to an observer or an automated validation process.

When applied to system calls, the concept of designation implies that the call record is not merely a log entry but a deliberate token representing a specific operational state or transition. For a system call to be classified as auditable within this framework, it must satisfy the axiom that it is a subclass of `cco:DesignativeICE`. This classification imposes strict constraints on the metadata and structure of the entity; it must contain sufficient semantic content to be identified unambiguously as a proxy for the underlying kernel event.

### 4.2.1 The Axiom of Auditability

The core ontological constraint governing these entities is expressed through the axiom `syscall_audited_by`. This axiom asserts a necessary relationship between the system call entity (the designative ICE) and an independent process responsible for its validation. Formally, the axiom is verbalized as: *{X} is a designative information content entity audited by some process.*

This relationship is not merely associative but constitutive. The entity {X} derives its status as an auditable record specifically through its connection to a `bfo:0000015` (Process). In BFO terms, a process is an entity that unfolds in time and possesses temporal parts. Therefore, the auditability of a system call is contingent upon the existence of an active, temporal process—such as an eBPF tracing program or a kernel audit daemon—that observes, records, and verifies the entity.

## 4.3 The Audit Process and Temporal Verification

The second component of the axiom involves `bfo:0000015`, which denotes a **Process**. In the context of compliance and governance, the audit process is the mechanism by which the fidelity and existence of the Designative ICE are verified. It is insufficient to simply store the entity; the system must provide evidence that a process has actively engaged with the entity to validate its properties.

This process-oriented approach aligns with regulatory frameworks such as NIST SP 800-53 and ISO/IEC 27001, which require not only the retention of records but also the demonstration of controls over those records. The "audited by" relationship implies that the process has the authority and capability to inspect the entity. In modern kernel architectures, this is typically achieved through extended Berkeley Packet Filter (eBPF) programs, which hook into specific tracepoints to observe system calls in real-time.

### 4.3.1 Process-Entity Coupling

The coupling between the Designative ICE and the Audit Process must be tight enough to prevent tampering yet loose enough to allow for scalability. The ontological definition allows for "some" process, meaning the specific implementation of the auditor may vary (e.g., a logging daemon, a security monitor, or a compliance filter), provided it meets the criteria of a BFO Process.

However, for the evidence to be admissible in a forensic or compliance context, the process must be verifiable. This often requires the process itself to generate secondary artifacts, such as checksums or digital signatures, that attest to its interaction with the Designative ICE. The relational schema provided in Section 4.2.2 implicitly supports this by isolating the entity (`x`), allowing foreign key relationships to be established with process logs in a broader schema architecture.

## 4.4 Governance and Evidence Retention

The formalization of system calls as Designative Information Content Entities audited by a process establishes a robust foundation for digital forensics and continuous monitoring. By adhering to the ontological structure defined in Axiom 1, organizations ensure that their audit logs are semantically rich and structurally sound. This facilitates automated reasoning over the audit data, enabling compliance officers to query not just for "events," but for "entities that are designated as specific system calls and are verified by a process."

Furthermore, the separation of the entity (`x`) from its unique identifier (`id`) in the relational schema allows for versioning and lineage tracking. As the kernel evolves or the audit process changes, the designation of the entity may remain constant while the specific instances (records) accumulate. This persistence is essential for long-term retention strategies mandated by regulations such as GDPR or SOX.

In summary, the transition from viewing system logs as ephemeral data streams to viewing them as instances of ontologically defined entities provides a layer of abstraction that enhances both security and compliance. It ensures that every record captured in the `t_syscall_audited_by` table is a verified, designative token of system state, anchored in reality by the temporal process that observed it.
