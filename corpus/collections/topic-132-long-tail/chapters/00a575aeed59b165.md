# Chapter 7: Governance Frameworks for Directive Compliance and Evidence Management

This chapter delineates the structural and procedural requirements for establishing a robust governance framework within regulated technical environments. It addresses the formalization of compliance directives, the evidentiary basis of attestations, and the authorization chains necessary for audit validity. The content herein is intended for systems architects, compliance officers, and data governors who require a rigorous, axiomatically defined approach to managing organizational controls. The principles described are aligned with the Ontology for Governance and Compliance (OGC) and are designed to facilitate automated reasoning over compliance states.

## 7.1 Attestation with Supporting Evidence

In the context of formal governance, an attestation is defined not merely as a declaration of compliance, but as a process that is intrinsically dependent on the existence of corroborating artifacts. The relationship between the attestation process and its evidence is axiomatic; without evidence, the attestation is logically null. This dependency ensures that every compliance claim is traceable to a specific, verifiable data source, whether that source is a system log, a signed document, or a real-time monitoring output.

The formal axiom governing this relationship posits that an attestation (Class X) is a process that possesses the property of `withSupportingEvidence` some Evidence (Class Y). This structure mandates that for any instance of an attestation to be valid within the governance framework, it must be explicitly linked to an instance of an evidence class. This linkage prevents "orphan" attestations—claims made without substantiation—which are a primary source of liability in audit scenarios.

Implementation of this axiom requires the maintenance of a rigorous mapping between the assertion of control and the artifact proving it. For example, if an organization attests to the enforcement of access controls, the supporting evidence must be a specific log entry or configuration snapshot demonstrating that enforcement at the relevant point in time.

This strict dependency is fundamental to maintaining the "evidence-anchored" nature of the compliance framework.

## 7.2 Requirement Specification and Targeting

Directives within a governance framework must be precise and unambiguous. A requirement is formally defined as a directive that is equivalent to the intersection of three conditions: it is a Directive, it specifies a particular control or action, and it applies to a specific target entity. This tripartite structure ensures that requirements are actionable and bounded. Vague directives, such as "improve security," are excluded from this formal class because they fail to specify a concrete action (Y) or a defined target (Z).

The axiom `requirement_equiv_specifies_target` establishes that a Requirement (Class X) is equivalent to a Directive that `specifies` some Specification (Class Y) and `appliesTo` some Target (Class Z). This formalism allows for the automated validation of requirement completeness. A requirement entry lacking a specified target or a defined specification is logically invalid and cannot be instantiated as a member of the Requirement class.

From a documentation standpoint, this necessitates a granular approach to policy writing. Policies must be decomposed into discrete requirement units, each of which references a specific technical standard or procedure (the specification) and a distinct asset or system group (the target).

## 7.3 Attestation Periodicity

Compliance is not a static state but a dynamic process that must be reaffirmed at defined intervals. The concept of attestation periodicity introduces the dimension of time into the governance ontology. An attestation is a process that occurs within a specific `AttestationPeriod`. This temporal binding ensures that compliance claims are current and relevant to the operational timeframe being assessed.

The axiom `attestation_at_period` asserts that an Attestation (Class X) is a process that has the property `atAttestationPeriod` some Period (Class Y). This formalization prevents the misuse of historical attestations to validate current states. For instance, an attestation regarding system vulnerability conducted in Q1 cannot serve as evidence of compliance in Q3 unless the attestation period explicitly covers the entire year or the attestation is re-executed.

In practice, this requires the governance framework to support temporal versioning of compliance states. When an auditor or automated system queries the compliance status of a control, the framework must return the attestation relevant to the current period or flag the absence of a valid attestation for the present timeframe.

This temporal rigor is critical for maintaining continuous compliance visibility.

## 7.4 Attestation Authorization

The validity of an attestation is contingent upon the authority of the entity asserting it. An attestation process must be authorized by a responsible agent, specifically a `Person`. The axiom `attestation_signed_by` formalizes this by stating that an Attestation (Class X) is a process that is `signedBy` some Person. This requirement ensures non-repudiation and assigns accountability for the accuracy of the attestation.

In the governance framework, the "signature" may be digital (e.g., a cryptographic signature using a PIV card) or procedural (e.g., a formal approval in a ticketing system), but it must be irrevocably linked to a verified identity record within the `cco:Person` class. This linkage prevents anonymous attestations and establishes a clear chain of custody for compliance decisions.

When an attestation is signed, the signer is implicitly validating the supporting evidence referenced in Section 7.1. Therefore, the signer acts as the human bridge between the raw technical data (evidence) and the formal compliance claim (attestation).

## 7.5 Audit Execution

Distinct from attestation, which is a declaration of state, an audit is an investigative process conducted by an agent to verify that state. The axiom `audit_conducted_by` defines an Audit (Class X) as a process that is `conductedBy` some Person. This distinguishes the audit as a human-driven (or human-supervised) activity, separate from automated scanning, although an audit may rely on automated tools.

The critical distinction here is the role of the conductor. While a system can generate a compliance report automatically, an audit implies a level of professional judgment, sampling, and verification performed by a qualified individual. The ontology captures this by requiring the `conductedBy` property to point to a `Person`.

This axiom supports the segregation of duties often required in compliance frameworks (e.g., the person who configures a system should not be the person who audits it). By explicitly modeling the conductor of the audit, the framework can perform conflict checks against the configuration change logs to ensure independence.
