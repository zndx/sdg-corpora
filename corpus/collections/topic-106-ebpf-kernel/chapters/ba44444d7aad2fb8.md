# Chapter 4: Correlation of Regulatory Directives and Control Descriptors

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the formal mapping between organizational governance directives and specific compliance requirements within the context of information security management. It establishes a structured approach to identifying how high-level policy directives—categorized as Directive Information Content Entities (DirectiveICE)—enforce or satisfy granular control objectives defined by the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) and ISO/IEC 27001. The text provides a rigorous examination of the ontological relationships between these directives and their corresponding descriptive entities, offering a methodology for auditors to verify the alignment of internal controls with external regulatory frameworks. The discussion is supported by relational data matrices that instantiate these axioms, demonstrating the practical application of theoretical mappings in a compliance audit environment.

## 4.2 Descriptive Information Content Entities as Measurement Baselines

### 4.2.1 Definition and Ontological Status

In the context of compliance auditing, a fundamental unit of analysis is the Descriptive Information Content Entity (DescriptiveICE). This entity represents the atomic level of a control objective or a measurement artifact. It is not the directive itself (the "shall" statement), but rather the descriptive definition of what is being measured, observed, or verified. Formally, a DescriptiveICE is a class of entities that encapsulate the semantic content of a requirement without the prescriptive force of a directive. It serves as the object upon which compliance activities are performed.

For instance, the concept of "Firewall Configuration Review" is a descriptive entity. It defines the scope of activity. The directive "The organization must perform a Firewall Configuration Review" imposes the obligation. In the ontology provided, this is captured by the `measurement_basic` axiom, which asserts that the entity is a subclass of `cco:DescriptiveICE`. These entities form the vocabulary of the compliance domain; they are the nouns to the directives' verbs.

### 4.2.2 Role in Audit Evidence

When conducting an audit, evidence is collected against these descriptive entities. An auditor does not merely check a box labeled "Compliance"; they validate that the specific state described by the `measurement_basic` entity exists. If the entity describes "Log Data Retention," the auditor examines the backup media and retention schedules to confirm that the description matches reality. Therefore, the precise definition of these entities is critical to the integrity of the audit. Ambiguity in the descriptive entity leads to ambiguity in the evidence collection.

## 4.3 PCI DSS Requirement Directives

### 4.3.1 The Directive-Requirement Relationship

The PCI DSS framework imposes specific obligations on entities handling cardholder data. In the ontology, this relationship is modeled by the axiom `pci_dss_requirement`, which asserts that a DirectiveICE (the organizational policy or procedure) is a subclass of directives that necessitate a specific PCI DSS requirement. The structure of this axiom is: `{X} is something that pci dss requirement {Y}`.

Here, `{X}` represents the internal governance artifact—such as a "Network Security Policy" or a "System Configuration Standard." `{Y}` represents the specific PCI DSS requirement drawn from the `measurement_basic` registry. This relationship is directional and mandatory; the existence of the directive `{X}` implies the existence of the requirement `{Y}` within the compliance scope.

### 4.3.2 Implementation and Verification

To verify this relationship in an audit, the reviewer must locate the internal directive `{X}` and trace its implementation to the specific control `{Y}`. For example, if the organization has issued a directive titled "Quarterly Firewall Review," the auditor must map this to the PCI DSS requirement regarding "Review of firewall and router rule sets." The strength of the compliance posture depends on the specificity of this mapping. A generic directive is insufficient; it must explicitly address the descriptive entity defined in the standard.

The auditor must verify that the policy text contains sufficient language to satisfy both the review frequency and the configuration standards.

## 4.4 ISO 27001 Annex A Subclass Integration

### 4.4.1 Harmonizing Directives with ISO Controls

The ISO/IEC 27001 standard provides a specification for an Information Security Management System (ISMS). Annex A of the standard lists a comprehensive set of control objectives and controls. The ontology models the integration of organizational directives with these controls via the `iso27001_annexa_subclass` axiom. This axiom asserts that a DirectiveICE `{X}` is a subclass of directives that relate to a specific ISO 27001 Annex A control `{Y}`.

The verbalization `{X} is something that iso27001 annex a {Y}` indicates that the directive is the mechanism by which the ISO control is implemented. Unlike the PCI DSS mapping, which is often prescriptive and binary, ISO 27001 allows for flexibility in how controls are implemented. The directive `{X}` represents the organization's specific choice of implementation for the generic control `{Y}`.

### 4.4.2 Audit Trail for Annex A Controls

Auditors assessing ISO 27001 compliance must trace the Statement of Applicability (SoA) back to the actual policies and procedures. The `iso27001_annexa_subclass` table provides this traceability. It confirms that for every selected control in Annex A, there is a corresponding directive (or set of directives) within the organization's corpus of governance documents.

For example, the ISO control "Annex A.12.3.1 - Information backup" is a generic requirement. The organization implements this via a directive titled "Data Backup and Restoration Procedure." The mapping of this directive to the control `m_b_004` is the evidence that the ISMS is operational and effective.
