# Chapter 7: Ontological Formalization of Audit Evidence and Attestation Protocols

## 7.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the formal ontological structures required to rigorously define audit evidence, attestation processes, and verification mechanisms within a compliant governance framework. The content herein is grounded in the Basic Formal Ontology (BFO) and the Common Core Ontologies (CCO), providing a deterministic mapping between abstract axioms and the relational data schemas necessary for high-fidelity audit trails. The objective is to establish a semantically stable environment where every assertion of compliance is traceable to a specific, designative information content entity, thereby ensuring the integrity of regulatory reporting and internal control evaluations.

## 7.2 Designative Subclass Basic

In the architecture of audit informatics, the foundational unit of data is the Designative Information Content Entity (ICE). As defined by Axiom 2 (`designative_subclass_basic`), this class encompasses any information entity that serves to designate or refer to a specific entity within the system. Unlike descriptive entities, which elaborate on qualities or occurrences, a designative entity functions primarily as a referential anchor. Within a compliance context, these are the distinct labels, codes, or reference numbers that allow auditors to point to specific artifacts without ambiguity.

The ontological assertion here is that `{X}` is a subclass of `cco:DesignativeICE`. This classification is critical for data governance; it ensures that the entity is recognized by the system not merely as a string of text, but as a controlled referent with specific semantic properties. This distinction prevents the conflation of data labels with descriptive prose, a common source of error in automated compliance checking.

## 7.3 Attestation with Supporting Evidence

The concept of attestation is central to regulatory compliance. An attestation is not a static state but a process—an occurrence that happens over time. According to Axiom 1 (`attestation_with_supporting_evidence`), an attestation is defined as a process (`bfo:0000015`) that necessarily possesses supporting evidence. The formalization asserts that `{X}` (the attestation process) is a process that `sdg:withSupportingEvidence` some `{Y}` (the designative entity).

This relationship mandates that for any attestation to be valid within the system, it must be inextricably linked to a specific, pre-existing designative entity. This ontological constraint enforces the principle of "evidence-based compliance." An auditor cannot simply assert that a control is active; they must perform an attestation process that is explicitly bound to the evidence supporting that assertion.

| `attest_01` | `QuarterlyAccessReview` | `desig_001` |

| `attest_03` | `PrivilegeAccessCertification` | `desig_003` |

In the relational schema, `t_attestation_with_supporting_evidence` links the process (`x`) to the evidence (`y`). For instance, the `QuarterlyAccessReview` (`attest_01`) is formally supported by the `AccessLogEntry_Ref` (`desig_001`). This structure allows automated governance tools to verify that every attestation in the log has a corresponding evidentiary anchor. It prevents "orphaned attestations"—claims of compliance that lack a basis in recorded data—and provides a clear audit trail for regulatory inspectors.

## 7.4 Verification Produces Evidence

While attestation consumes evidence to validate a claim, the verification process is generative. Axiom 3 (`verification_produces_evidence`) posits that `{X}` is a process that `sdg:producesEvidence` some `{Y}`. Here, the verification activity (such as a system scan or a hash calculation) results in the creation of a new designative information content entity.

This distinction is vital for maintaining the chain of custody in digital forensics and continuous monitoring. The evidence produced by verification is often a system-generated artifact—a checksum, a timestamped log, or a status flag—that did not exist prior to the execution of the verification process. Ontologically, this establishes a directional dependency: the existence of the evidence `Y` is dependent on the occurrence of the verification process `X`.

For example, the `FileIntegrityCheck` (`ver_01`) is the process that produces the `SystemConfiguration_Hash` (`desig_004`). By modeling this relationship, the framework captures the provenance of critical data points. It answers the question: "Why does this evidence exist?" The answer is found in the verification process recorded in the `t_verification_produces_evidence` table. This is essential for reconstructing the sequence of events during a security incident or a compliance investigation.

## 7.5 Subclass to Designative ICE

To ensure granular control over the types of information utilized in governance, Axiom 4 (`subclass_to_designative_ice`) provides a mechanism for defining specific subclasses of designative entities. While Axiom 2 establishes the basic classification, Axiom 4 allows for the extension of this class to include specialized designative entities tailored to specific regulatory domains or technical environments.

The axiom asserts that `{X}` is a subclass of `cco:DesignativeICE`. In practice, this is used to segregate entities by their function or origin—for instance, distinguishing between "Internal Reference Codes" and "External Regulatory Identifiers." Although the relational structure mirrors that of the basic designative table, the semantic distinction is maintained through the specific instances populated and the namespace of the `x` value. This separation allows the ontology to scale without losing semantic precision.

## 7.6 Identifier Unique

The final structural component ensures the uniqueness and singularity of references. Axiom 5 (`identifier_unique`) asserts that `{X}` is a designative information content entity that `sdg:identifies` max 1 `{Y}`. This is a cardinality constraint that enforces a one-to-one relationship between an identifier and the entity it identifies—in this case, an attestation.

This axiom is crucial for preventing ambiguity in audit reporting. If a single identifier could point to multiple attestations, the integrity of the audit trail would be compromised. Conversely, if an attestation could be identified by multiple primary identifiers, aggregation and reporting would become error-prone. The constraint `max 1` ensures that the identifier is a unique, singular key for the attestation process.
