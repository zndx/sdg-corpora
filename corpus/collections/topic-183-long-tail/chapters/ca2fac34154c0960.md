# Chapter 4: Regulatory Compliance Frameworks and Control Mapping

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter provides a comprehensive overview of the mechanics of regulatory alignment within information security governance. The session is designed to cover the structural mapping between internal governance directives and external compliance mandates, specifically focusing on the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS), the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), and the ISO/IEC 27001 framework. The objective is to discuss the formalization of control objectives through ontological structures, ensuring that Information Compliance Elements (ICE) are rigorously defined and auditable. By the end of this module, the reader should be capable of constructing relational mappings that demonstrate how specific organizational controls satisfy high-level regulatory requirements.

## 4.2 PCI DSS Requirement Directives

The PCI DSS framework establishes a baseline of technical and operational requirements intended to protect cardholder data. Within our ontology, we model these requirements as specific subclasses of the `cco:DirectiveICE` class. A PCI DSS Requirement Directive is defined axiomatically as an entity that enforces a specific PCI DSS requirement category. This relationship allows governance professionals to trace a specific internal policy or control mechanism back to the explicit mandate of the PCI DSS standard.

The mapping is critical for audit readiness. When an auditor queries the system for evidence of "Requirement 1: Install and maintain a firewall configuration," the ontology must return all directives classified as satisfying that requirement. This is not merely a tagging exercise but a structural assertion that the directive (`X`) is, by definition, an implementation of the requirement (`Y`).

The primary key `id` serves as the unique identifier for the mapping assertion, while `x` and `y` represent the control and the requirement category, respectively.

| id | x | y |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| pci_req_001 | NetworkFirewallConfigurationDirective | Requirement1 |
| pci_req_002 | SecureRouterConfigurationDirective | Requirement1 |

### 4.2.1 Implementation Guidance
Organizations must ensure that instances of `NetworkFirewallConfigurationDirective` are not merely theoretical documents but active configurations. The audit evidence for this mapping typically includes firewall rule sets, change tickets approving rule modifications, and quarterly review reviews. The ontological linkage ensures that any update to `Requirement1` automatically triggers a review of all associated `x` entities.

## 4.3 HIPAA Technical Safeguards

The HIPAA Security Rule defines "Technical Safeguards" as the technology and the policy and procedures for its use that protect electronic protected health information (ePHI) and control access to it. In our formal model, a Technical Safeguard Directive is a specific type of `cco:DirectiveICE` that implements a technical safeguard defined by HIPAA. This axiom captures the relationship between a concrete technical control—such as an encryption mechanism or an access control protocol—and the abstract regulatory requirement.

The distinction here is the specificity of the implementation. Unlike administrative measures, technical safeguards involve automated logic and hardware/software constraints. The axiom `{X} SubClassOf cco:DirectiveICE, sdg:hipaaSafeguard some {Y}` ensures that the entity `X` is categorized strictly within the technical domain of the safeguard `Y`.

**Table 4.2: HIPAA Technical Safeguard Mapping**

This table embodies Axiom 2 (`hipaa_safeguard_technical`), linking a specific technical control class (`X`) to the HIPAA technical safeguard category it fulfills (`Y`). The primary key `id` uniquely identifies the control instance, ensuring referential integrity within the compliance database.

| id | x | y |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| hip_tech_001 | UniqueUserIdentificationDirective | AccessControl |
| hip_tech_002 | EmergencyAccessProcedureDirective | AccessControl |
| hip_tech_003 | AutomaticLogoffDirective | AccessControl |
| hip_tech_004 | EncryptionAndDecryptionDirective | TransmissionSecurity |
| hip_tech_005 | IntegrityControlDirective | TransmissionSecurity |

### 4.2.2 Audit Verification
Verification of these mappings requires technical testing rather than document review. For instance, to validate `UniqueUserIdentificationDirective`, auditors will examine system logs to confirm that every action attributable to a specific user is traceable to a unique ID. The ontology supports this by classifying the directive under `AccessControl`, allowing the audit engine to filter all technical controls related to user access.

## 4.4 HIPAA Physical Safeguards

Physical safeguards are physical measures to protect electronic information systems and related buildings and equipment from natural and environmental hazards, and unauthorized intrusion. A Physical Safeguard Directive is modeled as a `cco:DirectiveICE` that enforces a specific physical safeguard requirement. The ontological structure here separates physical policies—such as facility access plans or workstation usage policies—from their technical counterparts.

This classification is essential for facility managers and security personnel who operate outside the traditional IT domain but are nonetheless critical to compliance. The axiom asserts that `X` (e.g., a policy regarding workstation placement) is a physical safeguard of type `Y` (e.g., Workstation Security).

**Table 4.3: HIPAA Physical Safeguard Mapping**

This table embodies Axiom 3 (`hipaa_safeguard_physical`), defining the relationship between a physical security directive (`X`) and the HIPAA physical safeguard category (`Y`). The `id` column provides a stable reference for the directive, while `y` anchors it to the regulatory text.

| id | x | y |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| hip_phys_001 | FacilityAccessControlDirective | FacilityAccessControls |
| hip_phys_002 | ContingencyOperationsDirective | FacilityAccessControls |
| hip_phys_003 | WorkstationSecurityDirective | WorkstationSecurity |
| hip_phys_004 | DeviceAndMediaControlDirective | DeviceAndMediaControls |
| hip_phys_005 | DisposalDirective | DeviceAndMediaControls |

### 4.4.1 Evidence Collection
Compliance evidence for physical safeguards often involves visual inspections and facility access logs. For `FacilityAccessControlDirective`, the auditor will verify that the facility uses key cards or biometric scanners and that access logs are retained. The ontological mapping ensures that these physical assets are included in the scope of the information security audit, preventing a siloed approach where physical security is overlooked.

## 4.5 HIPAA Administrative Safeguards

Administrative safeguards are administrative actions, policies, and procedures to manage the selection, development, implementation, and maintenance of security measures. These are often the most complex and voluminous set of requirements. An Administrative Safeguard Directive is a `cco:DirectiveICE` that manifests an administrative safeguard.

This axiom covers the governance layer of security: risk analysis, training, and contingency planning. The mapping of `X` to `Y` here often represents high-level organizational policies rather than specific configurations. For example, a `SecurityAwarenessTrainingDirective` is an administrative implementation of the `SecurityAwarenessAndTraining` safeguard.

**Table 4.4: HIPAA Administrative Safeguard Mapping**

This table embodies Axiom 4 (`hipaa_safeguard_admin`), connecting an administrative policy or procedure class (`X`) to its corresponding HIPAA administrative safeguard (`Y`). The schema ensures that every administrative action is traceable to a specific compliance requirement.

| id | x | y |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| hip_admin_001 | RiskAnalysisProcessDirective | SecurityManagementProcess |
| hip_admin_002 | RiskManagementStrategyDirective | SecurityManagementProcess |
| hip_admin_003 | SanctionPolicyDirective | SecurityManagementProcess |
| hip_admin_004 | InformationAccessManagementDirective | SecurityManagementProcess |
| hip_admin_005 | WorkforceSecurityDirective | SecurityManagementProcess |

### 4.5.1 Policy Governance
The primary challenge with administrative safeguards is maintaining version control and ensuring that the workforce has actually read and understood the policies. The ontology aids this by linking `SanctionPolicyDirective` directly to the `SecurityManagementProcess`. If a violation occurs, the system can immediately identify the relevant policy (`X`) and the governing requirement (`Y`) to determine the appropriate remediation steps.

## 4.6 ISO/IEC 27001 Annex A Controls

ISO/IEC 27001 is the international standard for Information Security Management Systems (ISMS). Annex A provides a list of information security control objectives and a catalogue of controls. An Annex A Subclass Directive is a `cco:DirectiveICE` that implements a specific control or objective listed in Annex A.

The structure of Annex A is hierarchical (e.g., A.9 Access Control, A.9.1 Business Requirements for Access Control). The axiom `{X} is something that iso27001 annex a {Y}` allows us to map specific organizational controls to these specific clauses. This is vital for organizations seeking certification, as the certification audit relies heavily on demonstrating that the management system has effectively implemented the relevant Annex A controls.

**Table 4.5: ISO 27001 Annex A Mapping**

This table embodies Axiom 5 (`iso27001_annexa_subclass`), mapping a specific control directive (`X`) to the ISO 27001 Annex A control objective or control (`Y`). The primary key `id` ensures that each implementation of a control is distinct and auditable.

### 4.6.1 Certification Scoping
During an ISO 27001 audit, the scope of certification is defined by the applicability of Annex A controls. The ontological mapping in Table 4.5 allows the organization to generate a Statement of Applicability (SoA) automatically. By querying for all directives where `y` is an Annex A control, the organization can demonstrate which controls are implemented, which are excluded, and the justification for any exclusions.
