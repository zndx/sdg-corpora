# Chapter 4: Regulatory Control Mapping and Compliance Directives

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter establishes the formal framework for mapping internal governance directives to external regulatory obligations. It details the structural relationships between organizational control mechanisms—specifically Information Control Elements (ICE)—and the requisite requirements defined by the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA). The objective is to provide a deterministic schema for auditing compliance, ensuring that every internal directive is traceable to a specific legal or contractual mandate. The following sections delineate the axiomatic structure of these mappings, supported by relational data tables that instantiate the logical connections between control classes and regulatory standards.

## 4.2 PCI DSS Requirement Mapping

The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) prescribes a robust set of requirements intended to ensure the secure handling of cardholder data. Within the ontology, a specific class of directives is identified as a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE`, which functions as the governing artifact for internal policy. The axiom `pci_dss_requirement` formalizes the relationship where a directive (Class X) is the instrument that satisfies a specific PCI DSS requirement (Class Y).

This relationship is critical for audit readiness. It asserts that the existence and enforcement of Directive X are necessary conditions for meeting Requirement Y. For instance, a "Firewall Configuration Directive" serves as the internal implementation of "PCI DSS Requirement 1." The mapping is not merely associative but structural; the directive *is* the embodiment of the requirement within the enterprise's governance framework.

The directive must contain procedural language, technical specifications, and validation steps that align verbatim with the regulatory text. Any deviation in the directive's scope from the requirement's intent constitutes a finding of non-compliance. The relational mapping provides the evidence trail, linking the governance artifact to the external obligation.

## 4.3 HIPAA Safeguards: Physical Controls

The HIPAA Security Rule categorizes safeguards into three distinct domains: Physical, Technical, and Administrative. The Physical Safeguards domain focuses on the physical access to electronic protected health information (ePHI) regardless of its storage location. The axiom `hipaa_safeguard_physical` defines a class of directives (X) that serves as the implementation mechanism for a specific physical safeguard (Y).

In this context, `cco:DirectiveICE` represents the policies governing physical access, such as "Facility Access Plans" or "Workstation Use Policies." These directives must articulate the physical measures—locks, security cameras, badge readers, and placement restrictions—that enforce the safeguard. The axiom asserts that the directive *is something that* HIPAA safeguards the physical environment through the specified measure.

### 4.3.1 Facility Access and Workstation Security

Physical security is often the first line of defense against unauthorized access. A directive in this category must address the contingency operations and the facility security plan. It is insufficient to merely possess a lock; the directive must define the procedures for issuing keys, the protocol for revoking access upon termination, and the visitor logging requirements.

This schema enforces the relationship between the policy document and the physical security standard.

### 4.3.2 Compliance Validation

Validation of physical safeguards involves site inspections and a review of the `FacilityAccessControlDirective`. The auditor will verify that the directive mandates the use of badges or biometric tokens and that physical access logs correspond to the policy's retention requirements. The mapping confirms that the administrative intent (the directive) is effectively translated into physical reality (the safeguard).

## 4.4 HIPAA Safeguards: Technical Controls

While physical safeguards address the tangible environment, Technical Safeguards involve the technology and the policies and procedures for its use that protect electronic health information and control access to it. The axiom `hipaa_safeguard_technical` maps a directive class (X) to a technical safeguard class (Y). This relationship ensures that automated mechanisms, such as encryption and authentication, are governed by formal directives.

Directives falling under this axiom typically include "Access Control Policies," "Audit Control Procedures," and "Transmission Security Protocols." These directives must specify the technical configurations, such as the required encryption strength (e.g., AES-256) or the complexity requirements for authentication credentials. The directive acts as the configuration standard for the technical infrastructure.

### 4.4.1 Access Control and Audit Mechanisms

A critical component of technical compliance is the ability to uniquely identify users and track their actions. The `UniqueUserAccessDirective` would map to the "Access Control" safeguard, stipulating that user accounts must be unique and traceable to a specific identity. Similarly, an `AuditControlMechanism` directive would map to the "Audit Controls" safeguard, requiring the implementation of hardware, software, and/or procedural mechanisms that record and examine activity in information systems that contain or use ePHI.

This structure links the governance documentation to the technical implementation.

| ID | Directive Class (X) | HIPAA Technical Safeguard (Y) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |

| tech_map_002 | EmergencyAccessProcedure | EmergencyAccessProcedure |

### 4.4.2 System Configuration Review

During a technical audit, the system configurations are compared against the `TransmissionEncryptionProtocol`. If the directive specifies TLS 1.2 or higher, but the system is configured to accept TLS 1.0, the mapping is violated. The axiom ensures that the directive is not merely a document but a functional standard that dictates the state of the technical infrastructure.

## 4.5 HIPAA Safeguards: Administrative Controls

Administrative Safeguards are the administrative actions, policies, and procedures to manage the selection, development, implementation, and maintenance of security measures to protect electronic protected health information. These safeguards are often the most comprehensive, covering the entire organization. The axiom `hipaa_safeguard_admin` defines the relationship where a directive class (X) implements an administrative safeguard (Y).

Directives in this category include "Risk Analysis Procedures," "Security Awareness Training," and "Contingency Planning." These are high-level governance documents that establish the management framework for compliance. The axiom asserts that the directive *is something that* HIPAA safeguards the administrative operations through the specified policy or procedure.

### 4.5.1 Risk Management and Training

The foundation of HIPAA compliance is the Security Management Process. A `RiskAnalysisProcedure` directive maps to the "Risk Analysis" safeguard, mandating a periodic and thorough assessment of the risks and vulnerabilities to the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of ePHI. Furthermore, a `WorkforceTrainingProgram` directive maps to "Security Awareness and Training," ensuring that all members of the workforce receive appropriate education regarding the handling of PHI.

This relational schema ensures that management policies are directly traceable to the administrative requirements of the Security Rule.

### 4.5.2 Policy Governance Review

Reviewing administrative safeguards involves examining the lifecycle of the directives. The `RiskAssessmentMethodology` must be a documented, repeatable process. The auditor verifies that the directive exists, that it has been approved by management, and that it is being reviewed and updated periodically. The mapping confirms that the organization's governance structure is designed to satisfy the administrative mandates of HIPAA.
