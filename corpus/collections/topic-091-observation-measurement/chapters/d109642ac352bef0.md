# Chapter 4: Regulatory Compliance Mapping and Directive Implementation

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the formal ontological structures required to map internal governance directives to external regulatory frameworks, specifically focusing on the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Security Rule. The content herein defines the axiomatic relationships between Information Control Elements (ICE) and specific compliance requirements or safeguards, providing a deterministic schema for audit readiness. It is intended for compliance officers, data governance architects, and information security managers who must demonstrate precise alignment between organizational controls and statutory obligations. The following sections present the relational schemas and data instances necessary to validate that a given directive satisfies the stipulations of PCI DSS requirements or HIPAA safeguard specifications.

## 1. PCI DSS Requirement Mapping

### 1.1 Definition and Axiomatic Structure

The alignment of organizational controls with the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) is governed by the axiom `pci_dss_requirement`. In this context, a specific internal class of directives (X) is defined as a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE`, which asserts that the directive necessitates compliance with a specific PCI DSS requirement class (Y). The Manchester syntax for this axiom is expressed as: `Class: {X:Class} SubClassOf: cco:DirectiveICE, sdg:pciDssRequirement some {Y:Class}`. This structure ensures that any instance of the directive X is intrinsically linked to the fulfillment of requirement Y, creating a traceable path from policy implementation to regulatory compliance.

### 1.2 Implementation Context

Organizations processing cardholder data must implement a layered defense strategy, where technical and operational controls are formally mapped to the 12 requirements of the PCI DSS framework. The `DirectiveICE` represents the actionable policy or configuration within the enterprise environment, while the `pciDssRequirement` slot anchors this action to the specific language of the standard (e.g., Requirement 1: Install and maintain a firewall configuration). By populating this mapping, auditors can verify that the internal governance ontology explicitly acknowledges which controls address which specific compliance obligations, thereby reducing the risk of scope creep or unmitigated vulnerabilities.

## 2. HIPAA Physical Safeguards

### 2.1 Definition and Axiomatic Structure

The HIPAA Security Rule categorizes necessary protections into Physical, Technical, and Administrative safeguards. The axiom `hipaa_safeguard_physical` defines the relationship for physical controls. Formally, a directive class (X) is a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` and is characterized by the property `sdg:hipaaSafeguard` targeting a Physical Safeguard class (Y). The Manchester syntax is: `Class: {X:Class} SubClassOf: cco:DirectiveICE, sdg:hipaaSafeguard some {Y:Class}`. This verbalization asserts that "X is something that HIPAA safeguard Y," specifically within the physical domain, ensuring that tangible assets and facilities are managed according to regulatory standards.

### 2.2 Implementation Context

Physical safeguards are measures to protect electronic information systems and related buildings and equipment from natural and environmental hazards, as well as unauthorized intrusion. In a governance framework, directives classified under this axiom address facility access, workstation usage, and device security. The ontological mapping ensures that physical security policies—such as badge access protocols or workstation positioning—are not merely operational procedures but are recognized as formal compliance objects satisfying specific HIPAA Physical Safeguard specifications (e.g., §164.310(a)(1)).

## 3. HIPAA Technical Safeguards

### 3.1 Definition and Axiomatic Structure

Technical safeguards involve the technology and the policy and procedures for its use that protect electronic health information (ePHI) and control access to it. The axiom `hipaa_safeguard_technical` formalizes this by defining a directive class (X) as a `cco:DirectiveICE` that implements a `sdg:hipaaSafeguard` of a Technical nature (Y). The Manchester syntax is identical in structure to the previous axiom but distinct in semantic domain: `Class: {X:Class} SubClassOf: cco:DirectiveICE, sdg:hipaaSafeguard some {Y:Class}`. This relationship ensures that automated mechanisms, such as encryption or access control systems, are formally mapped to the HIPAA Security Rule's technical specifications.

### 3.2 Implementation Context

The technical safeguard domain requires precise mapping between IT system configurations and the privacy standards mandated by HIPAA. Directives in this category typically govern access control, audit controls, integrity controls, and transmission security. By utilizing this ontological structure, organizations can demonstrate that specific technical directives (e.g., an encryption protocol) are not arbitrary IT choices but are direct implementations of requirements such as "Encryption and Decryption" (§164.312(a)(2)(iv)) or "Audit Controls" (§164.312(b)). This mapping is critical for automated compliance scanning and gap analysis.

## 4. HIPAA Administrative Safeguards

### 4.1 Definition and Axiomatic Structure

Administrative safeguards are administrative actions, policies, and procedures to manage the selection, development, implementation, and maintenance of security measures to protect electronic health information. The axiom `hipaa_safeguard_admin` defines the relationship for these governance-level controls. It asserts that a directive class (X), subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE`, functions as a `sdg:hipaaSafeguard` for an Administrative Safeguard class (Y). The Manchester syntax is: `Class: {X:Class} SubClassOf: cco:DirectiveICE, sdg:hipaaSafeguard some {Y:Class}`. This captures the logic that "X is something that HIPAA safeguard Y," where Y represents the administrative policies and oversight mechanisms required by the regulation.

### 4.2 Implementation Context

Administrative safeguards often represent the overarching governance framework within which physical and technical safeguards operate. These directives include risk analysis, workforce training, and contingency planning. The ontological mapping here is crucial for demonstrating that management policies are actively addressing the Administrative Requirements of the Security Rule (e.g., §164.308(a)(1)(ii)(B) for Workforce Security). It bridges the gap between high-level policy documents and operational compliance, ensuring that administrative intent is codified within the technical governance model.
