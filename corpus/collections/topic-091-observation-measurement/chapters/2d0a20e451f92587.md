# Chapter 4: Formal Mapping of Regulatory Directives to Compliance Ontologies

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the formal mechanisms by which regulatory frameworks—specifically the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)—are mapped to the core compliance ontology. The objective is to provide a rigorous, evidence-anchored methodology for classifying organizational controls as instances of `cco:DirectiveICE`. Accordingly, this text establishes the necessary axioms for deriving relational schemas that support audit readiness and automated compliance verification. The following sections define the specific subclass relationships and existential restrictions that bind control classes (`X`) to their respective regulatory requirements or safeguards (`Y`). Implementers must ensure that all data entries into the associated relational tables strictly adhere to these definitions to maintain logical consistency and validity against the governing ontology.

## 4.2 PCI DSS Requirement Directives

The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) prescribes a robust set of requirements intended to ensure the secure handling of cardholder data. Within the ontology, a PCI DSS directive is formalized through the axiom `pci_dss_requirement`. This axiom asserts that a specific control class (`X`) is a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` and necessitates the satisfaction of a specific PCI DSS requirement (`Y`). This mapping is critical for organizations that must demonstrate that their internal policies and technical controls are directly traceable to specific mandates within the PCI DSS framework (e.g., Requirement 1: Install and maintain a firewall configuration).

Accordingly, when an entity defines a control such as a `NetworkFirewallConfiguration`, it is asserting that this control is a type of DirectiveICE and exists to fulfill the requirement `InstallFirewall`. This relationship is not merely associative; it is existential. The control instance *must* possess the property of satisfying the requirement to be valid within the system. The following table illustrates this mapping, providing the primary key identifier, the specific control class (`X`), and the PCI DSS requirement it satisfies (`Y`).

**Table 4-1: Mapping of DirectiveICE Classes to PCI DSS Requirements**

| id | x | y |
|---|---|---|
| PCI-REQ-001 | NetworkSegmentationControl | InstallAndMaintainFirewallConfiguration |
| PCI-REQ-002 | DefaultPasswordProhibitionPolicy | ChangeVendorDefaultPasswords |
| PCI-REQ-003 | CardholderDataEncryptionProtocol | ProtectStoredCardholderData |
| PCI-REQ-004 | TransmissionEncryptionMechanism | EncryptTransmissionOfCardholderData |
| PCI-REQ-005 | AntiMalwareSoftwareUpdateRoutine | UseAndRegularlyUpdateAntiVirusSoftware |
| PCI-REQ-006 | SecureApplicationDevelopmentStandard | DevelopAndMaintainSecureSystems |

The relational schema `t_pci_dss_requirement` enforces this structure. The primary key `id` serves as the unique identifier for the directive mapping. The column `x` represents the class of the directive (the control), while `y` represents the requirement class. The constraint that `y` is `NOT NULL` ensures that every defined control is explicitly linked to a regulatory mandate, thereby preventing the existence of orphaned controls within the governance framework.

## 4.3 HIPAA Physical Safeguards

The HIPAA Security Rule defines Physical Safeguards as physical measures to protect electronic information systems and related buildings and equipment from natural and environmental hazards, and unauthorized intrusion. The ontology captures this through the `hipaa_safeguard_physical` axiom. Here, a class (`X`) representing a physical control is defined as a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` that `hipaaSafeguard` some physical safeguard specification (`Y`).

Entities subject to HIPAA must implement controls such as `FacilityAccessControl` or `WorkstationSecurity`. These are not merely policies but are directive classes that necessitate specific physical actions or states, such as `ContingencyOperations` or `PhysicalDisposal`. The formalization ensures that for every physical control implemented, there is a corresponding safeguard requirement that is being addressed. For instance, a `MediaDisposalProcedure` is a directive that safeguards `HardwareDisposalSanitization`. This distinction is vital for auditors verifying that physical security measures are not merely ad-hoc but are derived from the regulatory text found in 45 CFR § 164.310(a)(1).

**Table 4-2: Mapping of DirectiveICE Classes to HIPAA Physical Safeguards**

| id | x | y |
|---|---|---|
| HIP-PHYS-001 | FacilityAccessControlSystem | ContingencyOperations |
| HIP-PHYS-002 | WorkstationLocationPolicy | WorkstationUseRestrictions |
| HIP-PHYS-003 | MediaStorageSecurityProtocol | PhysicalMediaAccessControl |
| HIP-PHYS-004 | DeviceInventoryControlAsset | HardwareAssetTracking |
| HIP-PHYS-005 | DisposalManagementProcess | MediaDisposalSanitization |

The table `t_hipaa_safeguard_physical` represents the instantiation of this axiom. The column `x` identifies the specific physical control class deployed by the entity, while `y` identifies the safeguard category it addresses. The schema enforces that every physical directive (`X`) is inextricably linked to a safeguard definition (`Y`), ensuring the governance model reflects the full scope of physical compliance required by the regulation.

## 4.4 HIPAA Technical Safeguards

Technical Safeguards under HIPAA involve the technology and the policy and procedures for its use that protect electronic health information and control access to it. The `hipaa_safeguard_technical` axiom formalizes the relationship where a technical control class (`X`) is a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` that `hipaaSafeguard` some technical requirement (`Y`). This covers a broad range of automated mechanisms, from access controls to audit logs.

For example, a class `UniqueUserAuthentication` is a directive that safeguards the `AccessControl` requirement. Similarly, an `AuditLogReviewMechanism` is a directive that safeguards `AuditControl`. The ontology requires that these technical controls be explicitly classified to demonstrate that the electronic Protected Health Information (ePHI) is being protected by the requisite technology. This proposed action of mapping technical controls to the ontology does not impose additional requirements beyond those imposed by HIPAA; rather, it structures the existing requirements to facilitate automated compliance checking and gap analysis.

**Table 4-3: Mapping of DirectiveICE Classes to HIPAA Technical Safeguards**

| id | x | y |
|---|---|---|
| HIP-TECH-001 | UserAuthenticationModule | UniqueUserIdentification |
| HIP-TECH-002 | EmergencyAccessProcedure | EmergencyAccessProcedure |
| HIP-TECH-003 | AutomaticLogoffMechanism | AutomaticLogoffImplementation |
| HIP-TECH-004 | EncryptionTransmissionProtocol | TransmissionSecurity |
| HIP-TECH-005 | IntegrityControlMechanism | DataIntegrityVerification |

The `t_hipaa_safeguard_technical` table encodes these relationships. The `x` column contains the technical directive classes (e.g., specific software configurations or cryptographic modules), while the `y` column contains the abstract safeguard requirements they fulfill. This structure allows compliance officers to query the database and verify that for every technical requirement in the regulation, a corresponding directive class has been implemented and recorded.

## 4.5 HIPAA Administrative Safeguards

Administrative Safeguards are administrative actions, policies, and procedures to manage the selection, development, implementation, and maintenance of security measures to protect electronic health information. These are often the most complex aspect of the HIPAA Security Rule, as they involve human behavior and organizational management. The `hipaa_safeguard_admin` axiom defines a class (`X`) as a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` that `hipaaSafeguard` some administrative requirement (`Y`).

Examples include `SecurityManagementProcess` (safeguarding `RiskAnalysis`) or `WorkforceSecurityPolicy` (safeguarding `AuthorizationSupervision`). These directives represent the governance layer of compliance. The axiom ensures that administrative policies are treated as formal directives with the same rigor as technical controls. For that reason, the classification of these administrative actions within the ontology is essential for establishing a complete security posture. It ensures that the "paperwork" and procedural controls are auditable and linked directly to the regulatory text in 45 CFR § 164.308(a)(1).

**Table 4-4: Mapping of DirectiveICE Classes to HIPAA Administrative Safeguards**

| id | x | y |
|---|---|---|
| HIP-ADM-001 | RiskAssessmentAnalysis | RiskAnalysisAndManagement |
| HIP-ADM-002 | SanctionPolicyFramework | WorkforceSanctionPolicy |
| HIP-ADM-003 | InformationAccessManagementRole | AccessAuthorizationPolicy |
| HIP-ADM-004 | SecurityAwarenessTrainingProgram | SecurityTrainingAndEducation |
| HIP-ADM-005 | ContingencyPlanDocumentation | ContingencyPlanResponse |

The `t_hipaa_safeguard_admin` table captures these administrative mappings. The `x` column refers to the specific policy or procedure class (e.g., the specific training program or sanction policy document), while `y` refers to the high-level administrative safeguard it satisfies. By populating this table, organizations demonstrate that their administrative infrastructure is fully aligned with the HIPAA Security Rule's administrative standards.
