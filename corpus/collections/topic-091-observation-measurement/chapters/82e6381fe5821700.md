# Chapter 7: Regulatory Mapping of Information Control Elements

## 7.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the formal ontological mapping required to align internal governance frameworks with external regulatory mandates. Specifically, it addresses the integration of the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Security Rule within a unified compliance architecture. The content herein establishes the axiomatic relationships between internal directive classes—defined as Information Control Elements (ICE)—and their corresponding regulatory requirements or safeguards. Adherence to these mappings is mandatory for maintaining the organization's compliance posture and ensuring that all procedural directives are traceable to specific legal or contractual obligations. The following sections provide a detailed decomposition of these relationships, supported by relational data structures that instantiate the defined axioms.

## 7.2 PCI DSS Requirement Directives

The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) prescribes a robust set of requirements intended to ensure the secure handling of cardholder data. Within our ontology, the alignment between internal policy and these external mandates is governed by the axiom that a specific class of internal directives is a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` and fulfills a specific PCI DSS requirement. Formally, this is expressed as: {X} is something that pci dss requirement {Y}.

This relationship ensures that every governance artifact deployed within the infrastructure is not merely a procedural suggestion but a direct implementation of a compliance control. For instance, a directive governing network segmentation must be explicitly traceable to Requirement 1 of the PCI DSS standard regarding firewall installation and configuration. By formalizing this link, the organization automates the evidence-gathering process during audits, as the existence of the directive {X} serves as proof of adherence to requirement {Y}.

The following table enumerates specific instances of this mapping, illustrating how internal directive classes ({X}) are instantiated to satisfy distinct PCI DSS requirements ({Y}).

| Directive ID | Directive Class (X) | PCI DSS Requirement (Y) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| PCI-REQ-001 | `NetworkFirewallDirective` | `Req1_FirewallConfiguration` |
| PCI-REQ-002 | `VendorDefaultPasswordDirective` | `Req2_DefaultPasswords` |
| PCI-REQ-003 | `StoredDataEncryptionDirective` | `Req3_StoredDataEncryption` |
| PCI-REQ-004 | `TransmissionEncryptionDirective` | `Req4_TransmissionEncryption` |
| PCI-REQ-005 | `AntiMalwareDirective` | `Req5_AntiMalwareSoftware` |

In the context of Table 7.1, the `NetworkFirewallDirective` represents the internal class of documents and configurations that enforce the policy. This class is axiomatically defined as a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` and possesses the property of satisfying `Req1_FirewallConfiguration`. This structure prevents ambiguity; a firewall policy that cannot be mapped to this specific requirement class is considered orphaned and non-compliant under the governance framework.

## 7.3 HIPAA Physical Safeguards

The HIPAA Security Rule categorizes necessary protections into physical, technical, and administrative safeguards. Physical safeguards are measures to protect physical electronic information systems (EIS) and related buildings and equipment from natural and environmental hazards, as well as unauthorized intrusion. The ontological axiom for this domain asserts that a directive class {X} is a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` and functions as a HIPAA safeguard for a physical asset class {Y}. Verbalized, {X} is something that hipaa safeguard {Y}.

Implementation of physical safeguards requires rigorous facility management. The directives defined in this section cover access control, contingency operations, and facility security plans. For example, a `FacilityAccessControlDirective` is not merely a list of rules; it is a safeguard for the physical entity `FacilityAccessControl`. The axiom binds the procedural document to the physical reality it secures, ensuring that the governance framework accounts for tangible assets.

Table 7.2 details the relational mapping of directive classes to the physical safeguard specifications mandated by HIPAA.

| Directive ID | Directive Class (X) | Physical Safeguard Class (Y) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| HIP-PHYS-001 | `FacilityAccessControlDirective` | `FacilityAccessControl` |
| HIP-PHYS-002 | `WorkstationUseDirective` | `WorkstationSecurity` |
| HIP-PHYS-003 | `DeviceMediaControlDirective` | `DeviceAndMediaControl` |
| HIP-PHYS-004 | `PhysicalAccessLogDirective` | `AccessControlAudit` |

The `WorkstationUseDirective`, as shown in Table 7.2, serves as the governing mechanism for `WorkstationSecurity`. This relationship implies that the directive class inherits the obligations of the safeguard class. Consequently, any instance of the `WorkstationUseDirective` must contain provisions for restricting access to workstations to authorized users only, thereby fulfilling the ontological commitment to the safeguard class {Y}.

## 7.4 HIPAA Technical Safeguards

While physical safeguards address the tangible environment, technical safeguards involve the technology and the policy and procedures for its use that protect electronic protected health information (ePHI) and control access to it. The axiom governing this section mirrors the structure of the physical safeguards but applies to automated mechanisms. The directive class {X} is a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` and acts as a HIPAA safeguard for a technical mechanism class {Y}. Thus, {X} is something that hipaa safeguard {Y}.

Technical safeguards are critical in defining how the software and hardware systems interact with sensitive data. The ontology distinguishes between the policy (the directive) and the mechanism (the safeguard). For example, the `AccessControlDirective` is the administrative rule set, whereas `AccessControlMechanism` represents the technical implementation (such as unique user IDs or automatic logoff). The axiom ensures that for every technical mechanism required by HIPAA, a corresponding directive exists to govern its deployment and usage.

Table 7.3 provides the relational schema for these technical mappings, highlighting the dependency between internal governance directives and technical control implementations.

| Directive ID | Directive Class (X) | Technical Safeguard Class (Y) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| HIP-TECH-001 | `UniqueUserIdentificationDirective` | `UniqueUserIdentification` |
| HIP-TECH-002 | `EmergencyAccessProcedureDirective` | `EmergencyAccessProcedure` |
| HIP-TECH-003 | `AuditControlDirective` | `AuditControlMechanism` |
| HIP-TECH-004 | `IntegrityControlDirective` | `IntegrityMechanism` |
| HIP-TECH-005 | `TransmissionSecurityDirective` | `TransmissionSecurity` |

As evidenced by the row for `AuditControlDirective` in Table 7.3, the directive class safeguards the `AuditControlMechanism`. This implies that the directive must contain specifications for hardware, software, and/or procedural mechanisms that record and examine activity in information systems that contain or use ePHI. The ontological link enforces a strict validation rule: if the `AuditControlMechanism` is compromised or absent, the `AuditControlDirective` is rendered non-compliant, triggering a governance failure state.

## 7.5 HIPAA Administrative Safeguards

Administrative safeguards are administrative actions, policies, and procedures to manage the selection, development, implementation, and maintenance of security measures to protect electronic protected health information (ePHI). These safeguards are the overarching framework that manages the selection and execution of physical and technical safeguards. The axiom for this section defines that a directive class {X} is a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` and serves as a HIPAA safeguard for an administrative function class {Y}. Therefore, {X} is something that hipaa safeguard {Y}.

These directives are typically high-level governance documents that assign responsibility for security within the organization. They include risk analysis, sanction policies, and information system activity reviews. The strength of administrative safeguards lies in their ability to bind the physical and technical domains into a cohesive operational strategy. The `SecurityManagementProcessDirective`, for instance, safeguards the `SecurityManagementProcess` class, ensuring that risk analysis and risk management are not one-time events but continuous, directive-driven activities.

Table 7.4 outlines the mapping of administrative directives to their corresponding safeguard classes, reflecting the hierarchical nature of compliance management.

| Directive ID | Directive Class (X) | Administrative Safeguard Class (Y) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| HIP-ADM-001 | `SecurityManagementProcessDirective` | `SecurityManagementProcess` |
| HIP-ADM-002 | `AssignedSecurityResponsibilityDirective` | `AssignedSecurityResponsibility` |
| HIP-ADM-003 | `WorkforceSecurityDirective` | `WorkforceSecurity` |
| HIP-ADM-004 | `InformationAccessManagementDirective` | `InformationAccessManagement` |
| HIP-ADM-005 | `SecurityAwarenessTrainingDirective` | `SecurityAwarenessAndTraining` |

The `WorkforceSecurityDirective` in Table 7.4 safeguards the `WorkforceSecurity` class. This relationship encompasses the policies and procedures for authorization and/or supervision of workforce members who work with ePHI or in locations where it might be accessed. By axiomatically linking the directive to the safeguard, the organization ensures that all workforce clearance procedures are documented, enforced, and auditable as part of the `cco:DirectiveICE` inventory.
