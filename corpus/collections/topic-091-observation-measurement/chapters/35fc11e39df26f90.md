# Chapter 7: Regulatory Mapping and Control Implementation Directives

## 7.1 Scope and Framework Applicability

This chapter delineates the formal mapping of internal governance directives to external regulatory frameworks, specifically the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Security Rule. It establishes the ontological relationships between control classes and their respective compliance requirements, ensuring that organizational policies are auditable and semantically aligned with legal mandates. The following sections provide a granular analysis of these mappings, supported by relational data structures that instantiate the defined axioms. The objective is to demonstrate how abstract Information Control Elements (ICE) are operationalized into concrete compliance obligations through deterministic schema projection.

## 7.2 PCI DSS Requirement Directives

The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) prescribes a robust set of requirements intended to ensure the secure handling of cardholder data. Within the ontology, the relationship between an internal control directive and a specific PCI DSS requirement is governed by the axiom `pci_dss_requirement`. This axiom asserts that a specific class of directive (`X`) is a subclass of the `cco:DirectiveICE` and fulfills the semantic condition of being a `pciDssRequirement` for a target requirement class (`Y`).

In practical governance terms, this mapping allows an organization to trace a specific internal policy—such as a firewall configuration rule—directly to a specific article of the PCI DSS standard (e.g., Requirement 1). This traceability is critical during audit scenarios, where evidence of implementation must be anchored to the specific regulatory language. The directive serves as the enforcement mechanism, while the requirement class serves as the normative target.

The `x` column represents the specific internal directive class (the `cco:DirectiveICE`), and the `y` column represents the PCI DSS requirement class to which it is bound. This structure ensures that every directive is explicitly linked to a compliance obligation, preventing "orphaned" controls that do not contribute to the regulatory posture.

Similarly, the `VendorDefaultPasswordDirective` enforces `Requirement2`, ensuring that system passwords are not left at vendor defaults. These mappings are not merely categorical; they represent the functional dependency of the organization's security architecture on the standard's specifications.

## 7.3 HIPAA Physical Safeguards

Under the HIPAA Security Rule, "Physical Safeguards" are defined as physical measures to protect electronic information systems and related buildings and equipment from natural and environmental hazards, and unauthorized intrusion. The ontology captures this through the `hipaa_safeguard_physical` axiom. Here, a directive class (`X`) is asserted to be a `cco:DirectiveICE` that functions as a `hipaaSafeguard` for a specific physical safeguard class (`Y`).

The distinction of physical safeguards lies in their tangibility; they govern the physical access to ePHI (electronic Protected Health Information) storage locations, the security of workstations, and the disposal of hardware. The axiom ensures that physical policies—such as badge reader protocols or workstation positioning—are formally recognized as implementations of the HIPAA Physical Safeguard specification set (164.310(a)(1)). This formalization is essential for gap analysis, allowing compliance officers to verify that all necessary physical control domains are covered by active directives.

The schema requires a unique `id` for each mapping, with `x` denoting the organizational directive and `y` denoting the specific HIPAA Physical Safeguard class. This relational view facilitates the audit of physical security controls by isolating them from technical and administrative measures.

Table 7-2: Mapping of Directives to HIPAA Physical Safeguards

| id | x (Directive) | y (HIPAA Physical Safeguard) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| map_phys_01 | FacilityAccessControlDirective | FacilityAccessControls |

As evidenced in Table 7-2, the `FacilityAccessControlDirective` (x) is mapped to `FacilityAccessControls` (y), covering the implementation of procedures to limit physical access to electronic information systems. The `WorkstationPositioningPolicy` ensures that screens are not visible to unauthorized individuals, satisfying the `WorkstationUse` safeguard. Notably, the `DeviceAndMediaControlDirective` and `PhysicalDisposalProcedure` both map to `DeviceAndMediaControls`, demonstrating that a single safeguard class may require multiple distinct directives to achieve full compliance (e.g., tracking movement of hardware *and* ensuring its secure destruction).

## 7.4 HIPAA Technical Safeguards

While physical safeguards address the tangible environment, HIPAA Technical Safeguards concern the technology and the policies and procedures for its use that protect electronic health information and control access to it. The `hipaa_safeguard_technical` axiom formalizes the relationship where a directive (`X`) implements a technical safeguard (`Y`). These are the automated mechanisms—encryption, access logging, authentication protocols—that form the digital perimeter around ePHI.

The complexity of technical safeguards often requires granular directives. For example, the "Access Control" standard under HIPAA encompasses unique user identification, emergency access procedures, automatic logoff, and encryption and decryption. The ontology allows these distinct technical capabilities to be modeled as separate directives (`X`), all subclassing the necessary safeguard class (`Y`). This granularity supports precise implementation specifications; a failure in the `AuditControlMechanism` can be isolated to a specific directive without invalidating the entire "Audit Controls" safeguard implementation.

This structure enforces a defense-in-depth strategy, ensuring that access is not only granted based on identity but is also managed during emergencies and terminated automatically during inactivity. Furthermore, the `EndToEndEncryptionDirective` directly addresses the `TransmissionSecurity` safeguard, mitigating the risk of ePHI interception in transit.

## 7.5 HIPAA Administrative Safeguards

Administrative safeguards are the administrative actions, and policies and procedures, to manage the selection, development, implementation, and maintenance of security measures to protect electronic health information. These are often the most comprehensive and complex components of the HIPAA Security Rule, as they govern the conduct of the workforce. The `hipaa_safeguard_admin` axiom captures the relationship between a governance directive (`X`) and an administrative safeguard class (`Y`).

These directives typically include risk analysis, security management processes, workforce training, and contingency planning. Unlike technical safeguards, which are often automated, administrative safeguards rely on human adherence to policy. Therefore, the ontology must capture the "Directive" nature of these controls—formal documents or mandates that dictate human behavior. The axiom ensures that high-level governance activities, such as a `RiskAssessmentProtocol`, are recognized as valid implementations of the `SecurityManagementProcess` safeguard.

Similarly, the `WorkforceSecurity` safeguard is realized through both punitive measures (`SanctionPolicyDirective`) and educational measures (`SecurityAwarenessTrainingProgram`), illustrating the dual approach of enforcing policy and cultivating culture.
