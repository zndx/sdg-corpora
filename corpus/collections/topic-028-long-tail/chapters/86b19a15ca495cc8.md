# Chapter 7: Structural Governance and Entity Verification Protocols

This chapter establishes the formal ontological constraints governing compliance workflows, specifically focusing on the intersection of processual attestation, artifact provenance, and directive verification. It delineates the mandatory relationships between information content entities, physical locations, and authorized agents within a secure governance framework. The definitions provided herein are binding for all subsequent audit procedures and system implementations, ensuring that all compliance artifacts maintain traceability to validated sources and authorized personnel.

## 1. Attestation Processes and Signatory Requirements

In the context of governance frameworks, an attestation is formally defined not merely as a statement of fact, but as a temporally bounded process (`bfo:Process`) that requires the active participation of an agent. To satisfy the axioms of directive governance, any entity classified as an attestation must possess a specific structural property: it must be signed by a minimum of one person (`cco:Person`). This constraint ensures that no automated or unverified process may be elevated to the status of a formal attestation without human accountability. The "min 1" cardinality restriction is non-negotiable; null values in the signatory field constitute a critical compliance violation, rendering the attestation void for audit purposes.

The relational representation of this axiom isolates the attestation entity (`X`) as the primary object of interest. The primary key serves as the unique identifier for the process instance, while the entity class itself enforces the inheritance of the `bfo:Process` characteristics. This structure allows auditors to query all processes that are legally required to have signatory attached, distinguishing them from background system processes that do not carry legal weight.

### 1.1 Operational Implications
When an auditor reviews the `t_attestation_min_one_signer` relation, they are verifying the *existence* of the process obligation. The row data confirms that a specific process (e.g., "Q3 Financial Audit Process") has been instantiated within the governance system. It is imperative to note that while this table establishes the process, the linkage to the specific human signatory is managed through distinct object properties, ensuring a separation of concerns between the process definition and the agent execution.

## 2. Artifact Provenance and Postal Addressing

Artifacts (`cco:Artifact`) within the compliance domain—whether physical documents, digital records, or fixed assets—must be anchored to a physical reality to ensure verifiability. The axiom governing artifact provenance stipulates that any artifact of interest must possess a postal address (`sdg:hasPostalAddress`). This requirement is critical for maintaining the "long tail" of compliance data, ensuring that high-level directives can be traced back to tangible locations. The relationship between the artifact and its address is existential; an artifact lacking a registered address is considered orphaned and outside the scope of the jurisdiction.

Structurally, this axiom introduces a dependency between the artifact entity and the address entity. The relational schema projects this as a foreign key constraint, where the address identifier (`y`) must reference a valid attestation record. This linkage implies a governance hierarchy: a physical location or postal address is often formally established or "attested to" before it can be associated with a compliance artifact. Consequently, the integrity of the address data is contingent upon the validity of the underlying attestation process.

### 2.1 Address Validation Protocol
The foreign key constraint (`y` references `t_attestation_min_one_signer`) enforces a rigid validation workflow. Before a location such as "Central Records Repository" can be entered into the system as `LOC-1001`, an attestation process (e.g., `ATT-2023-001`) must have occurred. This ensures that the physical existence or legal lease of the site has been verified by a responsible party. This dependency prevents the insertion of fictitious locations into the compliance registry, thereby maintaining the evidentiary value of the artifact registry.

## 3. Directive Verification and Location-Based Compliance

Directives, classified as Directive Information Content Entities (`cco:DirectiveICE`), form the normative layer of the governance framework. These include policies, mandates, and regulatory requirements. For a directive to be operationalized, it must undergo verification. The axiom governing this relationship requires that every directive be verified by a minimum of one entity (`sdg:verifiedBy min 1`). In this specific schema, the verification entity is structurally linked to a location or address artifact. This implies that compliance verification is often a site-specific activity; a directive is verified *at* a specific place or *regarding* a specific address.

This structure facilitates granular compliance reporting. Rather than asserting that a directive is "verified" globally, the system records that "Directive A" is verified at "Site B." This allows for differential compliance status across an organization's footprint (e.g., a directive may be verified at the headquarters but pending at a regional branch). The relational integrity ensures that one cannot claim verification at a location that does not exist or has not been properly attested.

### 3.1 Verification Granularity
By linking the requirement (`x`) to the address (`y`) via the foreign key, the organization creates a heat map of compliance. For instance, `REQ-5002` (Fire Safety) is linked to `LOC-1002` (North Annex Server Room). If a subsequent audit reveals that `LOC-1002` failed a fire inspection, the system can immediately identify all requirements (`REQ-5002`) that are now compromised. This relational topology is essential for risk management and targeted remediation efforts.

## 4. Agent-Based Signing Authority

While Section 1 defined the *process* of attestation, this section defines the *act* of signing. The axiom `attestation_signed_by` asserts that an entity (`X`) is signed by a person (`cco:Person`). This is a distinct concept from the "min 1" requirement; it establishes the existential link between the document or process instance and the specific human agent exercising authority. In legal and regulatory terms, this captures the "wet signature" or its digital equivalent, binding the individual's identity to the content of the entity.

This table serves as the registry of authority. It answers the question "Who signed this?" rather than "Does this require a signature?". In a dense audit environment, this table is frequently cross-referenced with HR records to ensure that the signatory (`cco:Person`) held the appropriate delegation of authority (DOA) at the time the signing process occurred. The absence of a valid entry in this table for a controlled document constitutes a break in the chain of custody.

The table below lists specific processes and the associated signing events. The `t_attestation_signed_by` table embodies the axiom that {X} is something signed by a person, with `id` serving as the primary key for the signing event.

| SIG-9003 | Safety Compliance Attestation |
| SIG-9004 | Data Governance Acknowledgement |

### 4.1 Non-Repudiation
The data in `t_attestation_signed_by` provides the basis for non-repudiation. By isolating the signing event (`id`) and linking it to the specific content (`x`), the system creates an immutable log. Should a signatory later deny involvement, the record `SIG-9002` linked to "Q3 Financial Audit Certification" serves as the system-of-record evidence. This structure is designed to withstand forensic scrutiny during external audits or litigation.

## 5. Evidence Generation in Verification Workflows

The final axiom in this governance sequence addresses the output of verification processes. A verification process (`bfo:Process`) is not merely a procedural check; it must produce tangible evidence (`sdg:producesEvidence`). This evidence acts as the proof-point that the verification was conducted according to the established directives. The schema links this evidence back to the location/address entity, reinforcing the principle that compliance evidence is grounded in specific physical or digital contexts.

This relationship closes the loop on the compliance lifecycle: a Directive (Section 3) is verified at a Location (Section 2), producing Evidence (Section 5). The evidence might take the form of a scan of a physical log, a digital timestamp, or a sensor output. By enforcing the foreign key to the address table, the system ensures that evidence is not floating in abstraction but is firmly attached to the site of the verification activity.
