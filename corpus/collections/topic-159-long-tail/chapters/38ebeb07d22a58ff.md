# Chapter 7: Evidence Structures and Audit Verification Protocols

This chapter delineates the ontological structures required to formalize the capture, production, and aggregation of descriptive information content entities (ICE) within a governance framework. It establishes the necessary relationships between discrete events, ongoing processes, and the evidence or claims derived from them, culminating in the mathematical formalization of belief structures via mass functions. The following sections provide a rigorous examination of these axioms, supported by relational schemas that enforce referential integrity across audit datasets.

## 7.1 Metric Observation and Event Anchoring

In the context of systems governance, a metric is defined strictly as a quantitative measure that must be temporally anchored to a specific occurrence to possess verifiable meaning. The axiom **metric_observed_by_event** posits that a metric (Class X) is a subclass of descriptive information content entity that is `observedAt` a specific event (bfo:0000015). This distinction ensures that raw data points are not treated as abstract entities but are inextricably linked to the temporal instance of their generation.

This anchoring is critical for audit trails. Without the constraint of observation by an event, a metric exists in a vacuum, rendering it useless for root cause analysis or compliance verification. The event serves as the immutable witness to the metric's existence.

The following schema implements this axiom. The primary key is the unique identifier for the metric instance, while the column `x` represents the class of the metric being observed.

## 7.2 Claims and Event Contexts

While metrics represent quantitative data, claims represent qualitative assertions or declarative statements made by agents or systems. The axiom **claim_observed_by_event** asserts that a claim (Class X) is a descriptive information content entity observed at a specific event. In audit protocols, claims differ from metrics in that they require validation against a ground truth, whereas metrics are simply measurements.

For example, during a login event, a system may observe the claim "User is Administrator." This claim is a descriptive entity generated by the event, but its veracity must be authenticated against credentials. The ontological structure treats claims as first-class entities that must be captured and logged exactly as observed, preserving the integrity of the audit record for subsequent forensic analysis.

## 7.3 Evidence Generation via Process Execution

Evidence differs from simple observation in that it implies an artifact generated by the execution of a process. The axiom **evidence_observed_by_process** defines evidence (Class X) as a descriptive information content entity observed during a process (bfo:0000015). A process is distinct from an event in that it has a duration and involves a series of steps or state changes.

In this framework, evidence is the residue of procedural execution. For instance, a database migration process may produce log files (evidence) indicating record counts and failure states. The process acts as the generator, and the evidence is the output that allows auditors to reconstruct the process's behavior post hoc. The ontological constraint ensures that evidence is never dissociated from the process that produced it, maintaining the chain of custody.

## 7.4 Audit as an Evidence Generator

An audit is a specialized process with the explicit purpose of verification and compliance assessment. The axiom **audit_produces_evidence** characterizes an audit (Class X) as a process that `producesEvidence` of the type descriptive information content entity. This is a generative relationship; the audit does not merely observe data but creates new evidence, such as audit reports, findings, or compliance certificates.

This distinction is vital for governance frameworks. The evidence produced by an audit is often the basis for legal or regulatory decisions. By defining the audit as a subclass of processes that produce evidence, the ontology creates a clear lineage from the audit activity to the resulting compliance artifacts. This allows stakeholders to trace any compliance finding back to the specific audit instance that generated it.

## 7.5 Belief Structures and Focal Elements

To handle uncertainty and aggregation of observations, governance frameworks often employ belief functions, specifically within the Dempster-Shafer theory. The axiom **mass_function_min_one_focal** defines a mass function (Class X) as a descriptive information content entity that possesses `hasFocalElement` a minimum of 1 focal element (Class Y).

A mass function represents a degree of belief assigned to a set of propositions (the focal elements). In this ontology, the focal elements are grounded in the metrics defined in Section 7.1. This creates a rigorous mathematical layer where raw metrics (the focal elements) are used to construct higher-order belief structures. For instance, a "System Reliability Belief" may be a mass function that assigns belief to the "ServerLatencyMetric" as a focal element. This relationship is enforced via a foreign key, ensuring that every belief structure is anchored to verifiable, observed metrics.
