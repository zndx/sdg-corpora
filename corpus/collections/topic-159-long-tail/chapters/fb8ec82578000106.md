# Chapter 4: Ontological Structures for Audit and Compliance Evidence

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the formal ontological structures required to govern the generation, observation, and aggregation of evidence within regulated digital environments. It addresses the necessity of mapping descriptive information content entities (DescriptiveICE) to temporal processes and events to satisfy rigorous compliance mandates. The axioms presented herein provide the logical skeleton for constructing audit trails that are both computationally verifiable and semantically rich. By adhering to these structures, governance frameworks ensure that every artifact—whether a raw metric, a derived belief, or a procedural claim—is anchored to a specific temporal occurrence, thereby establishing non-repudiation and traceability.

## 4.2 Audit Processes and Evidence Production

In the context of governance frameworks, an audit is formally defined not merely as a retrospective review but as a distinct processual entity (bfo:0000015) that actively generates informational artifacts. The relationship between the auditing process and the evidence it produces is foundational; without this causal link, data exists in a vacuum, devoid of the provenance required for regulatory validation. The axiom `audit_produces_evidence` asserts that any instance of an audit process must produce at least one descriptive information content entity. This entity serves as the immutable record of the process's execution.

The integrity of the audit trail relies on the strict typing of these relationships. The audit process is the `bfo:0000015` (the occurrent), while the output is a `cco:DescriptiveICE` (the continuant). This distinction prevents category errors in data modeling, ensuring that one does not confuse the act of measurement with the measurement itself. For instance, a "Quarterly Access Control Review" is the process; the resulting "Access Log Manifest" is the evidence.

### 4.2.1 Relational Implementation of Audit Production

To implement this axiom within a relational database management system (RDBMS), the table `t_audit_produces_evidence` is utilized. The primary key (`id`) uniquely identifies the specific audit instance. The column `x` represents the audit process entity itself. This structure ensures that every row corresponds to a distinct event of evidence generation, satisfying the requirement that the process produces a descriptive entity.

## 4.3 Event-Based Metric Observation

While audits produce high-level evidence, granular compliance is often maintained through the continuous observation of specific metrics. The axiom `metric_observed_by_event` refines the concept of evidence by defining a subclass of `DescriptiveICE` that is strictly quantitative and temporally anchored to an event. Here, the metric is not merely a number but a descriptive entity that gains validity through its association with a specific occurrence (bfo:0000015).

This distinction is critical for high-frequency trading environments, industrial control systems, or real-time monitoring platforms where the "state of the world" at a specific instant is the object of compliance. The metric acts as a proxy for the event's properties. For example, the metric "CPU_Temperature_95C" is only meaningful when observed at the timestamp of the "System Overload Event." The ontological structure enforces that a metric cannot exist as a floating abstraction; it must be `observedAt` some event.

### 4.3.1 Relational Implementation of Metric Observation

The table `t_metric_observed_by_event` captures these observations. The primary key (`id`) provides a unique reference to the specific metric observation instance. The column `x` denotes the metric entity (the DescriptiveICE). This table serves as the central registry for all quantitative data points that may later be aggregated into reports or used as focal elements in belief calculations.

## 4.4 Belief Structures and Focal Elements

Advanced compliance frameworks often employ probabilistic reasoning to handle uncertainty or incomplete data. The axiom `mass_function_min_one_focal` introduces the concept of a mass function—a mathematical construct from Dempster-Shafer theory—represented here as a subclass of `DescriptiveICE`. This entity does not merely describe a fact but describes a degree of belief assigned to a specific subset of possibilities, known as the focal element.

Crucially, this axiom mandates that the mass function must have a minimum of one focal element (`sdg:hasFocalElement min 1`). In this schema, the focal element (`Y`) is not an arbitrary set but is specifically constrained to be a metric observed by an event (referencing the entity defined in Axiom 2). This linkage allows the governance framework to propagate uncertainty from raw measurements to higher-level compliance beliefs. For instance, a mass function might assign a 0.9 belief mass to the "Latency_Ms_Event_Transaction_8842" being within acceptable bounds, thereby quantifying the system's compliance status probabilistically.

### 4.4.1 Relational Implementation of Mass Functions

The table `t_mass_function_min_one_focal` formalizes this relationship. The primary key (`id`) identifies the specific mass function assignment. Column `x` represents the mass function entity (the belief structure). Column `y` represents the focal element, which is a foreign key referencing the `id` in `t_metric_observed_by_event`. This referential integrity ensures that every belief is grounded in a concrete, observed metric.

## 4.5 Process-Linked Evidence Artifacts

Distinct from quantitative metrics, evidence often encompasses qualitative artifacts such as logs, screenshots, or configuration dumps. The axiom `evidence_observed_by_process` categorizes these artifacts as `DescriptiveICE` that are `observedAt` a process. While similar to metric observation, this axiom is typically applied to broader, longer-running processes (bfo:0000015) rather than instantaneous events.

This category captures the "heavy" evidence often required during forensic audits. For example, a "Database Consistency Check" is a process; the "Checksum Report" generated and observed during that process is the evidence. The ontological separation between metrics (Axiom 2) and evidence (Axiom 3) allows auditors to query for numerical telemetry or documentary proof separately, depending on the requirements of the specific regulation (e.g., GDPR vs. SOC 2).

### 4.5.1 Relational Implementation of Process Evidence

The table `t_evidence_observed_by_process` stores these artifacts. The primary key (`id`) uniquely identifies the evidence artifact. Column `x` holds the descriptive entity representing the evidence. This structure ensures that every piece of documentary proof is timestamped and associated with the specific process instance that generated or captured it.

## 4.6 Event-Anchored Claims

The final structural component involves claims—assertions made by agents or systems regarding the state of the world. The axiom `claim_observed_by_event` defines a claim as a `DescriptiveICE` observed at an event. Unlike metrics, which are objective measurements, claims represent subjective or declarative statements that must be recorded for accountability.

Examples of claims include a user asserting "I consent to data processing" or a system declaring "Transaction Approved." These claims are vital for "non-repudiation" services. By anchoring the claim to an event (bfo:0000015), the framework creates an immutable record of *what* was claimed, *when* it was claimed, and *during which occurrence*. This prevents retroactive modification of contractual or regulatory agreements within the system.

### 4.6.1 Relational Implementation of Claims

The table `t_claim_observed_by_event` manages these assertions. The primary key (`id`) provides a unique identifier for the claim instance. Column `x` contains the claim entity. This table acts as the ledger for all declarative statements passing through the system, providing the necessary evidence for legal and compliance disputes.
