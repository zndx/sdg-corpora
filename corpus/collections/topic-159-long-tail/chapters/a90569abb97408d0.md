# Chapter 4: Evidence Aggregation and Belief Structures in Automated Compliance Frameworks

This chapter delineates the formal ontological structures required to model the generation, observation, and aggregation of evidence within high-assurance audit environments. It provides a rigorous examination of the axioms governing descriptive information content entities (ICE), specifically focusing on the derivation of metrics, claims, and evidence from occurrents (processes and events). Furthermore, it details the implementation of belief structures—specifically mass functions and focal elements—to handle uncertainty in compliance determinations. The content is anchored to the relational schema projections of the underlying OWL ontology, ensuring data integrity and traceability from raw observation to final audit assertion.

## 4.1 Metrics Observed by Event

In the context of automated auditing, a metric is defined as a specific type of descriptive information content entity that quantifies a particular attribute of a system or process. According to Axiom 2, a metric is axiomatically linked to a specific temporal boundary known as an occurrent, denoted by the BFO entity `bfo:0000015` (a process or event). This binding ensures that quantitative data is not floating free of its temporal context; rather, every metric instance must be "observed at" a distinct event. This constraint is critical for audit trails, as it prevents the ambiguity of "orphaned" data points and establishes a verifiable timeline for system performance.

The relational projection of this axiom, `t_metric_observed_by_event`, enforces a primary key structure where the identifier of the metric serves as the unique reference. The column `x` represents the class of the metric being observed (e.g., `LatencyMetric`, `ErrorRateMetric`). While the table structure itself captures the entity definition, the semantic link to the event is implied by the ontology's subclass restriction to `cco:DescriptiveICE` and the `observedAt` object property.

## 4.2 Claims Observed by Event

Distinct from raw metrics, claims represent assertions or propositions regarding the state of the system. Axiom 4 defines a claim as a descriptive ICE that is similarly anchored to an event (`bfo:0000015`). However, whereas metrics describe *what* was measured (quantitatively), claims describe *what* is asserted to be true (qualitatively or logically) based on that measurement. The ontological distinction is vital for governance frameworks, as it separates the generation of data (metrics) from the interpretation of that data (claims).

For example, a system may observe a `CPUUtilizationMetric` reading 99%. The corresponding claim observed at the same event might be an instance of `ResourceSaturationClaim`. The schema for `t_claim_observed_by_event` captures these instances, ensuring that every assertion is traceable to the specific event that triggered its generation.

**Table 4.2: Instances of Claims Observed by Events**

| :--- | :--- |

The integrity of the audit report relies on the strict adherence to this structure. By treating claims as first-class information entities observed at events, the framework ensures that subjective interpretations or automated rule-based evaluations are temporally anchored and auditable in their own right.

## 4.3 Evidence Observed by Process

While events are typically point-in-time or short-duration occurrences, processes represent extended temporal intervals. Axiom 3 formalizes the concept of evidence as a descriptive ICE that is observed *during* a process (`bfo:0000015`). Evidence differs from metrics and claims in its granularity and duration; it is often an aggregation of multiple events and data points collected over the lifecycle of a process (e.g., a full backup cycle, a login session, or a financial reconciliation batch).

The table `t_evidence_observed_by_process` catalogs these artifacts. In a compliance scenario, evidence serves as the substantiating body of proof for audit findings. The ontological requirement that evidence be a subclass of `cco:DescriptiveICE` ensures that it is treated as an information artifact, distinct from the physical process that generated it.

By linking evidence to the process level, auditors can verify not just that a specific event occurred, but that a broader operational procedure was executed correctly and completely. This is essential for certifying compliance with frameworks such as ISO 27001 or SOC 2, where process adherence is a primary control objective.

## 4.4 Audit Production of Evidence

The audit function itself is modeled as a specific type of process. Axiom 5 posits that an Audit (a class of occurrent) is an entity that *produces* evidence. This is a directional relationship: the audit activity (the `bfo:0000015`) generates the descriptive ICE (the evidence). This axiom establishes the provenance chain for audit artifacts, ensuring that every piece of evidence can be traced back to the specific audit procedure that created it.

The schema `t_audit_produces_evidence` captures instances of audit processes. In a technical implementation, this table would likely join with other process tables to provide a comprehensive view of the audit lineage. The critical distinction here is the active role of the audit: it is not merely observing pre-existing data, but actively synthesizing and producing new informational entities.

## 4.5 Mass Functions and Focal Elements

In complex environments where sensor data or log entries may be incomplete or noisy, audit systems often employ belief functions rather than binary true/false logic. Axiom 1 introduces the concept of a Mass Function, a descriptive ICE that assigns a "mass" (basic probability assignment) to at least one Focal Element. A focal element represents a subset of hypotheses to which a non-zero belief is assigned.

The relational schema `t_mass_function_min_one_focal` is structurally significant because it establishes a foreign key dependency. The focal element (`y`) must reference an existing metric (`t_metric_observed_by_event`). This constraint enforces a rigorous standard: belief structures cannot be arbitrarily assigned to abstract concepts; they must be grounded in specific, observed metrics. This anchors the uncertainty analysis in the empirical data collected in Section 4.1.

For example, consider a `PacketLossMetric` (ID `M-OBS-2023-001`). The auditor may not be certain if the loss indicates a benign network blip or a malicious denial-of-service. A mass function instance might assign a mass of 0.7 to the hypothesis set "Anomaly" and 0.3 to "Normal," anchored to that specific metric.

**Table 4.5: Mass Function Instances with Focal Element Dependencies**

| id | x | y |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| MF-ASSIGN-001 | `NetworkCongestionBelief` | M-OBS-2023-001 |
| MF-ASSIGN-002 | `HostInstabilityBelief` | M-OBS-2023-002 |
| MF-ASSIGN-003 | `StorageContentionBelief` | M-OBS-2023-003 |

In this table, column `x` represents the class of the mass function (the belief structure itself), while column `y` is the foreign key referencing the `id` from `t_metric_observed_by_event`. The referential integrity ensures that a belief cannot exist without a corresponding observed metric. This structure supports the Dempster-Shafer theory of evidence within the audit framework, allowing for the mathematical combination of evidence from multiple sources to arrive at a final, defensible compliance conclusion.
