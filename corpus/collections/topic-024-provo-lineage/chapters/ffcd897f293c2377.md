# Chapter 12: Ontological Structures for Asset Valuation and Anomaly Detection

## 12.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the formal ontological structures required to model asset valuation, pricing mechanisms, and the detection of statistical outliers within a financial auditing framework. The objective is to provide a rigorous, evidence-anchored methodology for representing physical and digital artifacts (`cco:Artifact`) in relation to their descriptive information content entities (`cco:DescriptiveICE`). By establishing a deterministic relational schema, auditors and compliance officers can ensure that valuation assertions are substantiated by granular, verifiable data points. The following sections introduce three core axioms—descriptive claims, product pricing, and attribute-based outlier detection—detailing their logical definitions, their implementation in relational databases, and their practical application in high-frequency trading environments and fixed-income portfolio analysis.

## 12.2 Descriptive Information Content Entities

### 12.2.1 Ontological Definition

The foundational unit of audit evidence is the Descriptive Information Content Entity (ICE). In the context of the Common Core Ontologies (CCO), a `cco:DescriptiveICE` is defined as an information content entity that is about some entity. It is not the entity itself (the asset, the trade, or the contract), but rather the data or assertion regarding that entity. In financial auditing, distinguishing between the *Artifact* (e.g., a bond certificate) and the *DescriptiveICE* (e.g., the reported yield of that bond) is critical for maintaining data integrity and preventing category errors in reporting.

The axiom `outlier_claim_basic` formalizes this concept. It asserts that any instance of a claim or observation within the system is, by definition, a subclass of `cco:DescriptiveICE`. This classification serves as the primary key for all subsequent logical derivations. Without this foundational categorization, one cannot reliably link a physical asset to its market behavior or audit status.

### 12.2.2 Role in Audit Evidence

When an auditor examines a portfolio, they are not interacting directly with the financial instruments but rather with the descriptions of those instruments. These descriptions include prices, yields, maturity dates, and credit ratings. By modeling these as `DescriptiveICE` instances, the audit framework isolates the "claim" from the "fact." This isolation allows for versioning, provenance tracking, and the identification of discrepancies between the system's record and the external reality.

For instance, if a trading system records a price for a specific equity, that price entry is an information entity. It is a claim about the equity's value at a specific timestamp. If a subsequent data feed provides a different price, the system does not overwrite the artifact; rather, it generates a new `DescriptiveICE`. This persistence is vital for forensic analysis and regulatory reporting.

### 12.2.3 Relational Representation

The relational schema for `t_outlier_claim_basic` enforces the uniqueness of each descriptive claim. The primary key (`id`) serves as the immutable identifier for the information entity, while the `x` column denotes the specific class or type of the descriptive entity (e.g., "PriceObservation," "YieldCalculation").

The table below illustrates the instantiation of basic descriptive claims within a hypothetical audit dataset. Note that the `id` values will serve as foreign keys in subsequent tables, establishing the dependency chain between the claim and the artifact it describes.

## 12.3 Artifact Pricing and Valuation Assertions

### 12.3.1 Linking Artifacts to Valuations

While the previous section addressed the nature of the claim itself, this section addresses the relationship between the claim and the subject of the claim. In financial ontology, the subject is typically an `cco:Artifact`—a physical or digital object that has been produced or modified by an agent. Examples of artifacts include equity shares, derivative contracts, or fixed-income securities.

The axiom `schemaorg_product_price` establishes a necessary existential restriction: for a class to be recognized as a priced product within the system, it must be a subclass of `cco:Artifact` and must possess the property `sdg:hasPrice`. This property links the Artifact (X) to a Price (Y). Crucially, within this schema, the Price (Y) is modeled not as a raw floating-point number, but as a reference to a `DescriptiveICE`. This design choice enforces the principle that all valuations are, fundamentally, assertions or observations that require provenance and validation.

### 12.3.2 The Price as an Information Entity

By defining the Price (Y) slot as a foreign key referencing the `t_outlier_claim_basic` table, the ontology mandates that a price cannot exist in a vacuum. Every price must be traceable to a specific descriptive claim. This structure mitigates the risk of "orphan" data—valuations that appear in ledgers without a corresponding audit trail or source documentation.

Consider the compliance requirements for MiFID II or SEC Rule 605/606. Regulators require not just the execution price, but the time of execution, the venue, and liquidity context. By treating the price as a `DescriptiveICE`, the system can attach rich metadata to the valuation entity (Y) before linking it to the Artifact (X). This allows the audit framework to query not just "What is the price of Bond A?" but "Under what descriptive claim (e.g., closing price vs. bid-ask average) is this price recorded?"

### 12.3.3 Relational Mapping

The relational schema `t_schemaorg_product_price` implements this linkage. The column `x` represents the Artifact (e.g., a specific CUSIP or ISIN), and `y` represents the `id` of the descriptive claim (the price observation). The foreign key constraint (`y` references `t_outlier_claim_basic`) ensures referential integrity: one cannot assign a price to an artifact if that price has not been formally defined as a descriptive entity.

**Table 12.2: Artifact Pricing Assertions**

| id | x | y |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `artifact_price_01` | `CorpBond_SeriesA_2024` | `desc_claim_001` |
| `artifact_price_02` | `Equity_Common_BrandX` | `desc_claim_002` |
| `artifact_price_03` | `Derivative_Option_Call_100` | `desc_claim_001` |

*Table 12.2 embodies Axiom 1 (`schemaorg_product_price`). The `x` column identifies the Artifact (e.g., `CorpBond_SeriesA_2024`), and the `y` column links it to a specific Price Observation (`desc_claim_001`) from Table 12.1. The Foreign Key constraint on `y` validates that the price is a registered descriptive entity.*

## 12.4 Composite Attribute Sets and Outlier Detection

### 12.4.1 Complex Claims and Minimum Cardinality

The final concept introduced in this chapter addresses the complexity of modern financial anomalies. A simple price observation (Axiom 2) attached to an artifact (Axiom 1) is often insufficient to describe a regulatory anomaly or a complex trading pattern. Anomalies are frequently defined by the presence of specific attributes or a set of conditions that deviate from the norm.

The axiom `outlier_claim_min_one_attribute` defines a more specialized subclass of `cco:DescriptiveICE`. This entity represents a "complex claim"—such as a compliance alert or a statistical outlier flag—that is characterized by the possession of an AttributeSet. The logical restriction `min 1 {Y:Class}` dictates that for such a claim to be valid, it must be associated with at least one attribute set. In this schema, the attribute set (Y) is modeled as a reference to a `schemaorg_product_price` instance.

This structure effectively models a "claim about a price." For example, an auditor might generate an outlier claim stating that "The price of Bond A is suspicious." This claim (X) is a `DescriptiveICE`. The evidence for this claim is the `AttributeSet` (Y), which consists of the specific `ProductPrice` record (the price value and its context) that triggered the alert.

### 12.4.2 Governance and Forensic Analysis

From a governance perspective, this structure creates a layered audit trail. The base layer is the raw observation (`t_outlier_claim_basic`). The intermediate layer is the valuation of the asset (`t_schemaorg_product_price`). The top layer is the analytical judgment or alert (`t_outlier_claim_min_one_attribute`).

This tripartite structure is essential for defending audit decisions. If a trader is investigated for market manipulation, the investigation report (the complex claim) must point directly to the specific pricing data (the attribute set) that formed the basis of the suspicion. By enforcing a foreign key relationship from the Outlier Claim to the Product Price, the system ensures that every analytical conclusion is grounded in verifiable, atomic data.

### 12.4.3 Relational Implementation

The table `t_outlier_claim_min_one_attribute` captures these complex assertions. The column `x` identifies the nature of the outlier claim (e.g., "PriceDiscrepancyAlert"), while `y` references the `id` of the product price instance that serves as the attribute set for the claim.

**Table 12.3: Outlier Claims with Attribute Sets**

| :--- | :--- | :--- |

*Table 12.3 embodies Axiom 3 (`outlier_claim_min_one_attribute`).
