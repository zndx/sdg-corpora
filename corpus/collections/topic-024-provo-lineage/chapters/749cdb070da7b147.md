# Chapter 4: Ontological Structures for Compliance Evidence Verification

## 4.1 Scope and Framework Applicability

This chapter delineates the formal ontological structures required to substantiate compliance evidence within high-frequency trading environments and automated audit systems. The primary objective is to establish a rigorous mapping between abstract ontology axioms—specifically those derived from the Common Core Ontologies (CCO) and the Semantic Data Governance (SDG) framework—and their deterministic relational projections. By anchoring data lineage in these structures, auditors can verify that descriptive information content entities (ICEs) are not merely isolated data points but are intrinsically linked to tangible artifacts and their attributed valuations. The following sections provide a detailed analysis of three core axiom templates, their verbalization in natural language, and the corresponding relational schemas that enforce referential integrity and constraint validation.

## 4.2 Descriptive Information Content Entities

### 4.2.1 Definition and Axiomatic Basis

At the foundational layer of any evidence-based audit lies the Descriptive Information Content Entity (ICE). In the context of the Manchester Syntax for the Web Ontology Language (OWL), this concept is formalized as:

**Class: {X:Class} SubClassOf: cco:DescriptiveICE**

Verbalized, this axiom asserts: **{X} is a descriptive information content entity.**

This classification is critical for distinguishing raw data observations from the physical artifacts they describe. A DescriptiveICE serves as the atomic unit of information—a claim, a measurement, or a state description—that exists independently of the medium used to store it. Within a compliance framework, this entity represents the "claim" made by a system or a reporter regarding a specific state of affairs. It is the evidentiary anchor; without a valid DescriptiveICE, no higher-level assertion about asset valuation or attribute attribution can be mathematically verified.

### 4.2.2 Relational Projection and Evidence Storage

The relational schema corresponding to this axiom, `t_outlier_claim_basic`, enforces the uniqueness of these claims. The structure is designed to capture the identifier of the claim (`id`) and its classification (`x`), ensuring that every distinct observation is cataloged before it is associated with other domain objects.

## 4.3 Artifact Pricing and Valuation Linkages

### 4.3.1 The Artifact-Price Relationship

The second ontological concept bridges the gap between abstract information and concrete economic reality. It addresses how an Artifact—a distinct physical or digital object—is associated with a specific Price. The axiom is expressed as:

**Class: {X:Class} SubClassOf: cco:Artifact, sdg:hasPrice some {Y:Class}**

Verbalized, this axiom asserts: **{X} is something that has price {Y}.**

This structure implies that an Artifact (X) cannot possess a price value in isolation; rather, it "has a price" (Y) which is itself a reference to a specific Information Content Entity. In database terms, the price is not merely a floating-point number attached to a product row; it is a relational pointer to a specific `DescriptiveICE` (the claim regarding the price). This indirection is essential for auditability, as it preserves the provenance of the valuation. If a price is disputed, the audit trail leads directly to the specific claim entity (`y`) that authorized that value.

### 4.3.2 Schema Constraints and Foreign Key Logic

The relational schema `t_schemaorg_product_price` implements this axiom through a foreign key constraint. The column `y` is strictly typed to reference the `id` of the `t_outlier_claim_basic` table. This enforces the rule that a price cannot exist for an artifact unless a valid descriptive claim has already been registered.

For instance, the `IndustrialPump_Assembly_01` is linked to `claim_001`, formalizing the assertion that the artifact's price is defined by that specific observation.

## 4.4 Attribute Set Validation and Outlier Detection

### 4.4.1 Complex Claims with Minimum Cardinality

The final ontological structure introduces complexity by defining a DescriptiveICE that must contain at least one attribute derived from a product price. This is often utilized in outlier detection, where a compliance claim (e.g., "This valuation is anomalous") must be substantiated by referencing the specific pricing artifact that triggered the alert. The axiom is defined as:

**Class: {X:Class} SubClassOf: cco:DescriptiveICE, sdg:hasAttributeSet min 1 {Y:Class}**

Verbalized, this axiom asserts: **{X} is a descriptive information content entity** that possesses an attribute set containing at least one member {Y}.

In this context, {X} represents a higher-level compliance claim or an outlier flag, while {Y} represents the specific product-price artifact being analyzed. The `min 1` cardinality constraint ensures that an outlier claim cannot exist in a vacuum; it must be anchored to at least one pricing event. This prevents the generation of "orphan" audit alerts that cannot be traced back to a transaction or valuation event.

### 4.4.2 Relational Implementation of Outlier Claims

The schema `t_outlier_claim_min_one_attribute` enforces this relationship. The column `y` functions as a foreign key referencing the `t_schemaorg_product_price` table. Consequently, the chain of reference is complete: the Outlier Claim references the Product Price, which in turn references the Basic Price Claim. This three-layer hierarchy (Observation -> Valuation -> Analysis) provides the dense, evidence-anchored structure required for regulatory reporting.

## 4.5 Summary of Relational Integrity

The integration of these three axiom templates creates a robust framework for data governance. By strictly defining the relationships between Artifacts, Prices, and Outlier Claims through foreign key constraints, the system ensures that:

1.  **Provenance is preserved:** Every price (`y` in Table 4.2) is traceable to a foundational claim (`id` in Table 4.1).
2.  **Context is maintained:** Every outlier or analysis (`x` in Table 4.3) is irrevocably linked to the specific pricing event (`y` in Table 4.3) that prompted it.

This structure eliminates ambiguity in financial reporting and provides a deterministic path for auditors to verify the validity of compliance assertions. The move from simple descriptive entities to complex, attribute-laden claims reflects the maturity of the data model, ensuring that "evidence" is not just a label, but a structurally enforced property of the information system.
