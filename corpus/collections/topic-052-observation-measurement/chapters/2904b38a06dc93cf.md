# Chapter 4: Regulatory Identification and Compliance Mapping in Supply Chain Artifacts

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the formal ontological structures governing the identification, classification, and compliance verification of commercial artifacts within a regulated supply chain ecosystem. The primary objective is to establish a rigorous framework for mapping physical and digital entities to their corresponding regulatory descriptors, specifically focusing on tax identification, attribute verification, ISO 27001 directive alignment, and financial pricing mechanisms. The definitions provided herein are grounded in the Common Core Ontology (CCO) and extended through specific domain axioms relevant to auditability and governance. Auditors and compliance officers must utilize these relational mappings to ensure that every artifact introduced into the inventory system is substantiated by valid descriptive information content entities (ICE) and directive claims.

## 4.2 Artifact Identification and Taxation Schema

In the context of commercial governance, the `cco:Artifact` represents the fundamental unit of analysis—a physical object or digital file that is the product of a deliberate fabrication process. To ensure regulatory adherence, every such artifact must be linked to a formal taxation identifier. This relationship is not merely associative but structural; the artifact possesses a tax identifier as an inherent property of its commercial existence.

The axiom governing this relationship posits that an Artifact is a class of entity that bears a Tax ID. This Tax ID is not a simple string literal but is treated as a distinct class entity within the system, allowing for complex validation and cross-referencing against external revenue databases. The integrity of the supply chain relies on the unambiguous mapping of the artifact to this identifier.

## 4.3 Descriptive Information Content Entities and Outlier Claims

To facilitate automated auditing and anomaly detection, the system employs `cco:DescriptiveICE` (Descriptive Information Content Entity). These entities are not the artifacts themselves but data structures that describe, assert, or claim properties about the artifacts. A critical subset of these is the "Outlier Claim," which represents a descriptive entity that deviates from standard baselines or requires specific attribute validation.

The axiom `outlier_claim_min_one_attribute` asserts that any valid DescriptiveICE must possess a minimum of one attribute set. This ensures that descriptive claims are never empty; they must carry a payload of definitional data. Furthermore, the relational structure enforces a bidirectional dependency: the attribute set referenced by the claim must, in turn, reference a valid Tax ID record. This circular dependency acts as a validation lock, preventing the creation of orphaned claims or unverified attributes.

This structure is essential for audit trails that must demonstrate not just that a claim exists, but that it is rooted in a verified fiscal identity.

## 4.4 ISO 27001 Clause Alignment and Directives

Governance frameworks frequently require the mapping of internal controls to external standards, specifically ISO/IEC 27001. Within this ontology, `cco:DirectiveICE` represents prescriptive information content entities—rules, policies, or instructions that govern behavior. The axiom `iso27001_clause_subclass` formalizes the relationship between these directives and specific clauses within the ISO 27001 standard.

This mapping ensures that every internal directive can be traced back to a specific regulatory requirement (e.g., Annex A.9 Access Control). The relational schema dictates that the ISO clause reference (`y`) must be a valid instance of a Descriptive ICE (specifically, an outlier claim), thereby linking the directive to a verified, attribute-rich claim. This enforces a hierarchy where directives are not abstract wishes but are grounded in verified descriptive entities that have passed the tax and attribute validation gates.

## 4.5 Attribute Set Composition

While previous sections have referenced attribute sets in the context of claims and tax IDs, it is necessary to define the internal composition of these sets. An attribute set is a `cco:DescriptiveICE` that aggregates specific designators or properties. The axiom `attribute_set_min_one` asserts that any such set must contain at least one `cco:DesignativeICE`—a term or symbol that designates a specific feature or value.

This structure prevents the existence of null or placeholder attribute sets. In practice, this means that for an artifact to be valid, its associated attribute set must contain concrete designative information, such as a serial number, a classification code, or a technical specification parameter. This is the granular level of data that fuels the higher-level compliance checks.

By querying this table, a compliance engine can verify that the attribute sets linked to tax IDs and claims are indeed valid, populated entities within the system, rather than null references.

## 4.6 Commercial Pricing Structures

The final component of this governance framework addresses the financial aspect of the artifacts: the price. The axiom `schemaorg_product_price` defines a relationship between an `cco:Artifact` and a price class. Similar to the Tax ID relationship, the price is treated as a distinct entity class rather than a primitive data type. This allows for complex pricing logic, including multi-currency support, tiered pricing models, and historical price tracking.

Crucially, the relational schema enforces that the price entity (`y`) must be a valid instance of a Descriptive ICE (an outlier claim). This constraint links the financial data to the same validation chain used for tax IDs and compliance clauses. It ensures that a price cannot be assigned to an artifact unless it is associated with a verified claim that contains the necessary attribute sets and designative information. This prevents "ghost" prices—financial entries that lack audit trails—from contaminating the ledger.

This end-to-end traceability, from the physical artifact to its financial and regulatory descriptors, constitutes the core value of the ontological approach described in this chapter.
