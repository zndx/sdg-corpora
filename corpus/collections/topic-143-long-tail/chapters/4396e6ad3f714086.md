# Chapter 4: Axiomatic Mapping of Evidence Structures to Relational Schemas

This chapter delineates the formal translation of high-level ontology axioms into normalized relational structures, specifically targeting the requirements of audit-ready data repositories. We focus on four distinct axiom templates that govern the definition of evidence structures, belief intervals, and governance directives. The objective is to demonstrate how semantic constraints—expressed in Web Ontology Language (OWL) Manchester syntax—are deterministically decomposed into tabular schemas with enforced referential integrity. The discussion proceeds from foundational logical definitions to complex compositional structures, ensuring that each entity is anchored by a primary key and that inter-table relationships are strictly maintained via foreign key constraints.

## 1. Logical Exclusion and Class Equivalence

### 1.1 Formal Definition
The foundational unit of logical classification within the ontology is the axiom of equivalence through intersection and negation. This construct, identified by the template `equiv_intersect_with_negation`, asserts that a specific Class $X$ is logically equivalent to the intersection of Class $Y$ and the complement of Class $Z$. In formal terms, $X \equiv Y \cap \neg Z$. This mechanism is critical for defining domains by inclusion criteria and simultaneous exclusion constraints, a common requirement in compliance frameworks where specific data types must be isolated from deprecated or exempt categories.

### 1.2 Relational Implementation
To implement this axiom within a relational database management system (RDBMS), the schema `t_equiv_intersect_with_negation` is utilized. This table captures the tripartite relationship between the defined class, the included superclass, and the excluded superclass. The primary key (`id`) serves as the unique identifier for the logical rule instance. This identifier is subsequently utilized as a foreign key by other tables that depend on this specific logical definition, ensuring that any class referencing this definition adheres to the established exclusion constraints.

## 2. Mass Functions and Frames of Discernment

### 2.1 The Descriptive Information Content Entity
The concept of a Mass Function is central to evidence theory, serving as a mathematical generalization of probability distributions. Within the ontology, a Mass Function is axiomatized as a subclass of `cco:DescriptiveICE` (Information Content Entity). The specific axiom `mass_function_equiv_frame_and_assignment` further constrains this entity by requiring it to operate over a specific Frame of Discernment and to assign mass to a target subset. The Frame of Discernment represents the set of mutually exclusive propositions under consideration, while the mass assignment represents the basic belief allocation.

### 2.2 Frame of Discernment Constraints
A critical integrity constraint in this schema is the dependency of the Frame of Discernment on the logical definitions established in Section 1. The relational schema `t_mass_function_equiv_frame_and_assignment` enforces this by linking the `y` slot (the Frame of Discernment) to the primary key of `t_equiv_intersect_with_negation`. This foreign key relationship guarantees that the universe of discourse for any mass function is explicitly defined via a logical intersection rule, preventing ambiguous or undefined frames in evidence calculations.

## 3. Belief Intervals and Probabilistic Bounds

### 3.1 Defining Belief and Plausibility
In evidential reasoning, a proposition's uncertainty is often characterized by a Belief Interval rather than a single point probability. This interval is bounded by a lower limit, Belief ($Bel$), and an upper limit, Plausibility ($Pl$). The axiom `belief_interval_equiv_bel_and_pl` formalizes this by defining a Class $X$ (the interval) as a `cco:DescriptiveICE` that possesses a specific Belief Lower Bound and a Plausibility Upper Bound. This structure allows for the representation of ignorance or uncertainty within the system, where the gap between $Bel$ and $Pl$ quantifies the degree of unknown information.

### 3.2 Interval Construction and Logical Anchoring
The relational mapping for belief intervals necessitates a strict link between the quantitative bounds and the qualitative definitions of the system. The schema `t_belief_interval_equiv_bel_and_pl` establishes that the Belief Lower Bound (`y`) is not merely a numerical value but is derived from a logical class definition found in `t_equiv_intersect_with_negation`. This design ensures that the lower bound of belief is calculated or defined strictly over the domain specified by the logical exclusion rules (e.g., belief is calculated only over "ValidFinancialRecords" and not "VoidedTransactions").

## 4. Governance Directives and Attribution

### 4.1 Directive Information Content Entities
Governance frameworks rely on directives—mandates, policies, or orders—that drive operational behavior. The ontology defines a Directive as a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE`. The axiom `directive_authored_and_applies` specifies that every directive must have an author and a target application. This structure is essential for audit trails, as it explicitly links the governance artifact (the directive) to the responsible entity and the scope of its enforcement.

### 4.2 Authorship by Evidence Models
A sophisticated feature of this ontology is the attribution of authorship. In the schema `t_directive_authored_and_applies`, the author (`y`) is not a simple agent string but a reference to a Mass Function entity (from `t_mass_function_equiv_frame_and_assignment`). This implies that directives can be generated or "authored" algorithmically based on the state of evidence models. For instance, a Mass Function detecting a high probability of system failure may author a Directive to shut down a component. The foreign key constraint ensures that only valid, defined evidence structures can serve as authors for governance directives, enforcing a chain of evidence-to-action.
