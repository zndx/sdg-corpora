# Chapter 4: Quantitative Compliance Metrics and Evidence Aggregation

This chapter delineates the formal framework for integrating Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) requirements with quantitative risk metrics derived from evidence theory. It provides a rigorous methodology for mapping regulatory directives to descriptive information content entities (ICE), specifically focusing on the statistical properties of baseline rates, evidence correlation, and stochastic independence. The objective is to establish a reproducible audit trail where compliance assertions are mathematically anchored to observable data patterns.

## 4.1 PCI DSS Requirement Mapping

In the context of the Common Core Ontologies (CCO), a PCI DSS requirement is modeled as a Directive Information Content Entity (ICE). This entity serves as a prescriptive norm that mandates specific control objectives. To achieve quantitative compliance, these directives must be linked to measurable Descriptive ICEs. The relationship is formalized by the axiom that a PCI DSS Requirement is a directive which necessitates a specific class of measurable evidence or metric.

For instance, a directive regarding access control (Requirement 8.1) is not merely a textual rule but a logical constraint that must be satisfied by a specific metric, such as the lift in anomaly detection rates following the implementation of multi-factor authentication. This mapping transforms qualitative regulatory text into a structured data relationship suitable for automated verification and continuous monitoring.

## 4.2 Lift with Baseline Rate

The concept of "lift" is central to evaluating the efficacy of security controls. In statistical terms, lift measures the ratio of the probability of an event (e.g., a security breach) occurring under a specific condition versus the probability of it occurring under baseline conditions. A Descriptive ICE representing a lift metric must be subclassed to include a specific baseline rate. This baseline rate acts as the denominator in the lift calculation, representing the expected frequency of the event in the absence of the control being evaluated.

Formally, a Class X (the Lift Metric) is defined as a Descriptive ICE that possesses a Baseline Rate Y. This relationship ensures that every reported lift value is grounded in a statistically valid reference point. Without this anchoring, lift values are mathematically undefined and operationally meaningless. The baseline rate itself is often derived from historical incident data or industry benchmarks, providing a normative standard against which current performance is measured.

## 4.3 Evidence Correlated With

Correlation analysis is fundamental to distinguishing between signal and noise in security telemetry. A Descriptive ICE representing a specific observation or data point is often defined by its correlation with another metric. In this ontology, a Class X (Evidence) is defined as a Descriptive ICE that is correlated with Class Y (typically a Lift Metric). This circular dependency—where Lift references Baseline Evidence, and Baseline Evidence references Lift—creates a reinforced definition of the metric's context.

For example, the evidence of "Failed Login Attempts" is correlated with the "Authentication Failure Lift." This correlation implies that as the frequency of failed logins deviates from the baseline, the lift metric changes proportionally. This structural relationship allows auditors to trace the upstream drivers of a compliance metric. It ensures that the quantitative assertions made during an audit are not abstract but are strictly derived from raw, correlated evidence streams.

## 4.4 Pignistic Transformation

When dealing with uncertainty in evidence aggregation, particularly within Dempster-Shafer theory, the Pignistic Transformation is utilized to convert a belief function into a probability distribution. In this framework, a Descriptive ICE (Class X) representing a belief state or uncertain observation must possess a Pignistic Transform (Class Y). This transformation is crucial for decision-making processes where compliance requires a probabilistic threshold (e.g., "the probability of a compromise is less than 0.01%").

The transformation maps the mass of belief assigned to a set of hypotheses to a single probability distribution. This allows for the integration of expert judgment or ambiguous sensor data into the rigorous quantitative framework required for PCI DSS reporting. By axiomatizing this transformation, the ontology ensures that uncertainty is quantified and reduced to a standard probabilistic format before it influences risk calculations.

## 4.5 Evidence Independent Of

To ensure the statistical validity of composite risk metrics, it is necessary to explicitly model independence between different evidence streams. A Descriptive ICE (Class X) is defined as being evidence independent of another Class Y. This axiom is critical for avoiding "double counting" risk factors. If two evidence sources are highly correlated (e.g., "port scan" and "firewall drop"), treating them as independent would artificially inflate the risk score.

Formally declaring independence allows the compliance framework to apply the product rule of probability safely. It asserts that the occurrence of event X provides no information regarding the occurrence of event Y. In the context of the relational schema, this independence is often asserted against a Lift Metric to demonstrate that the baseline calculation for that metric is not skewed by overlapping variables.
