# Chapter 4: Formalization of Compliance Directives and Evidence Correlation in PCI-DSS Governance Frameworks

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the formal ontological structures required to map Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI-DSS) directives to observable system behaviors and statistical anomalies. Within the context of high-assurance audit environments, it is insufficient to merely cite regulatory text; rather, compliance officers must establish a traceable lineage between abstract requirements (`cco:DirectiveICE`) and concrete descriptive artifacts (`cco:DescriptiveICE`). The following sections introduce five foundational axioms that govern this lineage. These axioms facilitate the rigorous definition of baseline rates, the correlation of forensic evidence, and the transformation of belief functions into probabilistic metrics suitable for executive reporting. The objective is to provide a deterministic framework for validating that a given control implementation not only exists but operates within statistically defined parameters.

## 4.2 PCI DSS Requirements and Operational Mappings

The fundamental unit of governance is the requirement itself. However, for automated auditing and continuous monitoring systems to function, a textual requirement must be axiomatically linked to a specific operational context. We define this relationship through the concept that a PCI DSS Requirement is a directive that necessitates a specific "lift" scenario—a measurable deviation from a normative state.

Formally, we assert: **{X} is something that pci dss requirement {Y}**. Here, {X} represents the specific control or directive class (e.g., "Two-Factor Authentication Control"), while {Y} represents the specific instance of a lift-with-baseline-rate that the directive seeks to enforce or monitor. This mapping transforms a static regulation into a dynamic observable variable.

To instantiate this axiom, consider the following relational schema which enforces that every requirement must point to a valid, defined lift scenario. The primary key is the requirement identifier, while the foreign key ensures referential integrity to the operational metrics table.

## 4.3 Lift with Baseline Rate

To validate a directive, one must measure the system's behavior against a normative standard. We utilize the concept of a "lift," derived from anomaly detection and statistics, representing the ratio of observed behavior to expected behavior. The axiom governing this concept is: **{X} is something that has baseline rate {Y}**.

In this structure, {X} is the descriptive information content entity (ICE) representing the specific lift metric (e.g., "Failed Authentication Lift"), and {Y} is the baseline rate itself. Crucially, within our ontology, the baseline rate is not a simple scalar value but is modeled as a specific instance of evidence correlation. This recursive definition allows the baseline to be defined by its relationship to historical evidence, rather than an arbitrary static number.

The relational schema for this concept enforces that every lift metric must be associated with a valid evidence correlation record. This ensures that "baselines" are never assumed but are always derived from correlated evidentiary data.

For instance, `lift_auth_baseline` represents the metric for failed authentication attempts. Its baseline rate (`y`) is determined by `ev_corr_auth_001`. This structure implies that the "normal" rate is not a fixed configuration but a dynamic state derived from the correlation defined in the evidence table. This is critical for environments where usage patterns fluctuate; the baseline adjusts as the correlated evidence shifts, maintaining the integrity of the "lift" calculation.

## 4.4 Evidence Correlation

The integrity of the baseline rate depends entirely on the robustness of the underlying evidence. We define the relationship between an observed artifact and its context via the axiom: **{X} is something that evidence correlated with {Y}**.

Here, {X} represents the specific evidence artifact (e.g., a specific log entry, system state, or forensic snapshot), and {Y} represents the lift metric with which it is correlated. This creates a bidirectional link: the lift metric defines the scope of the evidence, and the evidence validates the lift metric. This circular dependency is intentional and models the feedback loop present in continuous monitoring systems where observed evidence recalibrates the detection algorithms (lifts).

The schema for this axiom requires that every piece of evidence must be correlated with a valid lift metric, closing the loop initiated in Section 4.3.

If the system logs an anomaly in `SystemLog_AuthFailures`, the correlation mechanism updates the state of `ev_corr_auth_001`, which subsequently alters the baseline rate for `lift_auth_baseline`. This dynamic interplay is the essence of adaptive compliance monitoring.

## 4.5 Pignistic Transformation

While correlation provides a mechanism for defining baselines, compliance reporting often requires a single probabilistic score rather than a set of complex, interdependent belief functions. To address this, we employ the concept of Pignistic Transformation, a method from evidence theory (Dempster-Shafer) used to convert a belief function into a probability distribution.

The axiom is defined as: **{X} is something that has pignistic transform {Y}**. In this context, {X} is the descriptive ICE representing the reporting or scoring mechanism, and {Y} is the specific lift instance that is being transformed. This allows the governance framework to take the nuanced "lift" data (which may contain uncertainty or intervals) and transform it into a crisp probability for decision-making purposes.

The relational schema ensures that any transformation process is strictly tied to a valid lift metric, preventing the calculation of probabilities on undefined or non-existent data.

**Table 4.4: Pignistic Transformations**
This table embodies the `pignistic_transformation` axiom. It maps the transformation process (X) to the source lift metric (Y). The `id` is the primary key; `y` is a foreign key referencing `t_lift_with_baseline_rate`, ensuring that probabilities are only calculated for established metrics.

| id | x | y |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| trans_pig_01 | RiskProbabilityVector | lift_auth_baseline |
| trans_pig_02 | ComplianceScoreMatrix | lift_fail_lockout |
| trans_pig_03 | ThreatLevelIndex | lift_traffic_filter |

For example, `trans_pig_01` represents the generation of a `RiskProbabilityVector`. This vector is derived by applying the pignistic transformation to `lift_auth_baseline`. This process takes the potentially fuzzy evidence surrounding failed authentication attempts and converts it into a hard percentage risk score, which can then be compared against the risk appetite defined in the PCI DSS requirement.

## 4.6 Evidence Independence

A critical component of any audit framework is the verification that evidence sources are distinct and not redundant or circularly dependent in a way that obscures the truth. We formalize this via the axiom: **{X} is something that evidence independent of {Y}**.

In this structure, {X} represents a specific evidence stream or artifact, and {Y} represents a lift metric. This assertion states that the evidence {X} is statistically or logically independent of the lift metric {Y}. This is distinct from the correlation axiom; while correlation links evidence to a metric for definition, independence asserts that a specific piece of evidence *does not* influence a specific metric, thereby serving as a control group or a sanity check.

The schema for this axiom links an evidence entity to a lift metric to explicitly define what it *does not* affect. This is useful for isolating variables in complex systems where multiple lift metrics may exist.

In Table 4.5, `ev_ind_01` asserts that the `PhysicalAccessLog` is independent of `lift_auth_baseline`. This means that physical entry logs should not statistically influence the calculation of the digital authentication failure lift. This independence is crucial for root cause analysis; if `lift_auth_baseline` triggers an alert, the auditor knows that the cause is likely digital (e.g., a brute force attack) rather than physical (e.g., an intruder at the console), because the physical logs are axiomatically defined as independent of that specific lift.
