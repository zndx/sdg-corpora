# Chapter 7: Ontological Mapping of Digital Evidence and Compliance Assertions

This chapter delineates the formal ontological structures required to map digital artifacts and process traces to compliance claims within a regulated information environment. It provides a rigorous examination of the axioms governing the relationship between evidentiary data classes and descriptive information content entities (ICE), specifically within the context of audit readiness and forensic reconstruction. The following sections detail the specific subclass constraints and property assertions necessary to establish a verifiable chain of evidence, ensuring that all digital objects are properly categorized according to their role in supporting or refuting regulatory assertions.

## 7.1 Trace Support for Descriptive Claims

In the context of digital forensics and continuous monitoring, a "trace" is defined not merely as a log entry, but as a temporal process occurring within the system boundary. Axiomatically, we define a specific class of entities that are processes (`bfo:0000015`) and possess the capability to support a claim. This distinction is critical for auditors who must distinguish between the static artifact (the log file stored on disk) and the dynamic process (the execution of the transaction) that the trace represents. The ontology asserts that any instance of this class is fundamentally a process that substantiates a `DescriptiveICE`, which serves as the logical proposition or claim being evaluated (e.g., "The transaction was authorized").

When constructing an audit trail, the practitioner must identify the specific process traces that correspond to assertions made in the compliance report. For example, a `NetworkPacketTrace` is a process that supports the claim that data transmission occurred securely. Without this ontological linkage, the evidence remains an isolated data point lacking probative value. The classification ensures that the trace is understood as an event unfolding over time, thereby satisfying the temporal requirements of most regulatory frameworks regarding the sequence of events.

## 7.2 Kernel Anomalies as Artifacts Supporting Claims

While traces represent processes, kernel anomalies represent distinct, discrete artifacts that result from system failures or malicious activities. According to the governing axiom, a kernel anomaly is a subclass of `cco:Artifact`. Unlike the process trace, the artifact is a static, dependent entity that exists independently of the process that created it, yet it retains the property of supporting a claim. In this context, the artifact supports a `DescriptiveICE` regarding the system state—specifically, claims related to system integrity, availability, or the occurrence of a security incident.

For the forensic analyst, the kernel anomaly (such as a core dump or a ring buffer overflow remnant) is the "smoking gun" that substantiates the claim of a kernel-level compromise. The ontology mandates that these objects be classified as artifacts to differentiate them from the operational processes of the system. This classification allows for the application of specific retention policies and hashing algorithms required for evidentiary integrity. The claim supported here is often negative or exceptional in nature (e.g., "The system experienced a failure consistent with a buffer overflow attack"), distinguishing it from the routine claims supported by standard traces.

## 7.3 Column Lineage for Compliance Claims

Data governance frameworks require that the transformation and movement of sensitive data be fully transparent. The axiom governing column lineage establishes that specific artifacts—specifically metadata objects describing data transformation—support compliance claims. These artifacts are classified under `cco:Artifact` and are functionally distinct from raw data traces. They are structured records that map the origin, transformation, and destination of data elements. The claim supported by these artifacts is typically a compliance assertion, such as "Personally Identifiable Information (PII) was masked prior to export," which is a specific type of `DescriptiveICE`.

In practice, the `ColumnLineageArtifact` provides the evidentiary basis for data privacy regulations (e.g., GDPR CCPA). It demonstrates that the data handling procedures enacted by the organization align with the documented policies. The ontology treats the lineage metadata as an artifact because it is a constructed record, often generated by ETL (Extract, Transform, Load) frameworks or data catalogs, rather than a direct system process log. This distinction is vital for auditors verifying that data governance controls are embedded within the data architecture itself.

## 7.4 Mapping Directives to PCI DSS Requirements

Organizational policy must be formally mapped to external regulatory standards to demonstrate compliance. This section addresses the axiom where a directive (an internal mandate or policy document) is a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE`. The critical relationship defined here is `sdg:pciDssRequirement`, which links the internal directive class (`X`) to a specific external requirement class (`Y`). This mapping creates a formal bridge between the organization's internal governance vocabulary and the rigid requirements of the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS).

The verbalization of this axiom—"X is something that pci dss requirement Y"—implies that the directive is not merely a suggestion but is the operational implementation of the requirement. For instance, an internal `AccessControlDirective` is the embodiment of PCI DSS Requirement 7 (Restrict access to system components and cardholder data by business need to know). By formalizing this relationship in the ontology, the organization automates the gap analysis process, allowing compliance software to verify that every relevant PCI DSS requirement has a corresponding internal directive artifact.

## 7.5 Organizational Identification and Tax ID Assertions

Entity resolution is a foundational component of third-party risk management. The axiom `schemaorg_org_taxid` defines a relationship wherein an organization (represented as an `cco:Artifact` or a digital record of an organization) possesses a Tax ID. This relationship (`sdg:hasTaxID`) links the organizational entity (`X`) to a specific tax identifier class (`Y`). In the context of audit and compliance, this axiom ensures that vendor records, beneficiary accounts, and counterparties are uniquely identified and legally distinct, preventing ambiguity in financial disclosures and sanctions screening.

The classification of the organization as an `Artifact` in this context refers to the digital profile or record maintained within the enterprise resource planning (ERP) system, rather than the legal entity itself. This digital record must contain the `hasTaxID` property to be considered valid for transaction processing. This ontological constraint prevents the creation of vendor records without proper tax documentation, thereby enforcing compliance with fiscal regulations and Know Your Customer (KYC) protocols.
