# Chapter 4: Evidence Lineage and Regulatory Compliance in Digital Forensics

## 4.1 Preamble and Scope

This chapter establishes the ontological framework governing the relationship between digital artifacts, descriptive claims, and regulatory directives within a compliant forensic environment. It delineates the structural axioms that bind specific classes of evidence—ranging from kernel-level anomalies to column-level data lineage—to the descriptive information content entities (ICE) they support. Furthermore, it provides the necessary mappings to external regulatory standards, specifically the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS), and mandates the inclusion of statutory identifiers such as tax IDs to ensure entity resolution. The following sections detail these concepts through the lens of formal ontology and relational schema implementation, providing auditors and compliance officers with a rigorous method for evidence classification.

## 4.2 Trace Evidence and Claim Support

### 4.2.1 Conceptual Framework

In the context of digital forensic audits, a "trace" is defined not merely as a data point, but as a processual entity that substantiates a specific claim. Under the axiom `trace_supports_claim`, the ontology defines a class {X} as a subclass of `bfo:0000015` (a process) that necessarily supports a claim represented by a `cco:DescriptiveICE`. This distinction is critical for audit trails: the trace is the *occurrence* (the process of logging or the flow of data), while the claim is the *information* derived from that occurrence.

For example, a network flow log entry is not static data; it is a record of a process (the transmission of packets). This process serves as the evidentiary basis for the claim that "unauthorized data exfiltration occurred." The ontological structure ensures that every descriptive claim made in an audit report must be backed by a processual trace that satisfies the `bfo:0000015` criterion. This prevents the reliance on hearsay or unsubstantiated assertions within the governance framework.

## 4.3 Kernel Anomalies as Artifacts

### 4.3.1 Conceptual Framework

While general traces represent standard process flows, kernel anomalies represent deviations from expected operational baselines. The axiom `kernel_anomaly_supports_claim` classifies {X} as a subclass of `cco:Artifact`. Unlike the processual nature of a general trace, a kernel anomaly is treated as a discrete artifact—a distinct object or state snapshot—that supports a claim regarding system integrity or compromise.

This distinction is vital for high-assurance environments. A kernel anomaly, such as a mismatch in system call tables or an unexpected hook in the interrupt descriptor table, is a tangible artifact of manipulation. It supports the claim that "the operating system kernel has been modified." By classifying these as artifacts rather than mere processes, the ontology allows for static analysis, hashing, and chain-of-custody procedures that are distinct from those applied to transient log entries.

## 4.4 Column Lineage for Compliance Claims

### 4.4.1 Conceptual Framework

Data governance frameworks require that the transformation of sensitive data be verifiable from ingestion to reporting. The axiom `column_lineage_for_compliance_claim` addresses this by defining a class {X} (a specific data column or field artifact) that supports a claim about data handling. This class is a subclass of `cco:Artifact`, emphasizing that the data structure itself is the object of compliance scrutiny.

For instance, to support the claim that "Personally Identifiable Information (PII) was tokenized prior to storage," the artifact in question is the destination database column. The lineage proves that the source column (raw PII) was transformed into the target column (token). The artifact (the target column) supports the descriptive claim (the data is secure). This ontological mapping is essential for satisfying regulations such as GDPR and CCPA, where data provenance is a legal requirement.

## 4.5 PCI DSS Requirement Mapping

### 4.5.1 Conceptual Framework

Compliance with the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) requires a granular mapping between internal governance directives and the specific requirements of the standard. The axiom `pci_dss_requirement` formalizes this by defining a class {X} (a `cco:DirectiveICE`) as having a specific PCI DSS requirement {Y}. This relationship ensures that internal policies are not vague statements of intent but are explicitly anchored to external regulatory controls.

A `DirectiveICE` is an Information Content Entity that prescribes behavior. By asserting that a directive has a `pciDssRequirement`, the ontology creates a rigid link. For example, an internal directive titled "Quarterly Firewall Review" must map to "Requirement 1.1.6" of PCI DSS regarding firewall configuration reviews. This mapping facilitates automated compliance auditing and gap analysis, as the system can verify that for every directive {X}, a corresponding requirement {Y} exists and is satisfied.

## 4.6 Organizational Tax Identification

### 4.6.1 Conceptual Framework

In multi-tenant and supply-chain audit environments, the accurate identification of legal entities is paramount. The axiom `schemaorg_org_taxid` integrates standard semantic web structures by defining a class {X} (an `cco:Artifact`, typically an organizational profile or vendor record) as having a specific Tax ID {Y}. This property is crucial for disambiguating entities that may share names but have distinct legal statuses.

This axiom applies to artifacts that represent organizations within the system. For example, a "Vendor Profile" artifact must possess a `hasTaxID` property pointing to a valid tax identifier class (such as an EIN in the US or a VAT ID in the EU). This ensures that compliance claims regarding third-party risk management are anchored to the correct legal entity, preventing errors where a subsidiary is audited in place of a parent company, or vice versa.
