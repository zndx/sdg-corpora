# Chapter 4: Governance Directives and Enforcement Mechanisms

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter establishes the structural and functional requirements for governance directives within a compliant information architecture. It delineates the ontological definitions of policies, requirements, and attestations, specifying the necessary relationships between these directives and their respective enforcement, verification, and execution agents. The provisions herein ensure that all governance artifacts are formally defined, properly scoped, and operationally valid, thereby satisfying the rigorous demands of auditability and regulatory compliance.

## 4.2 Foundational Directives

In accordance with the foundational principles of information governance, a directive is formally defined as a Directive Information Content Entity (`cco:DirectiveICE`). This classification serves as the root node for all normative instructions within the system. A directive, in this context, is any information entity that prescribes, mandates, or guides behavior or state. It is distinct from physical entities or temporal processes; rather, it exists as a codifiable artifact that conveys intent.

Accordingly, the basic axiom for a policy asserts that any entity classified as a Policy must inherently be a Directive Information Content Entity. This definition is not merely taxonomic but functional: it establishes that the entity is eligible to be interpreted, enforced, and audited. Without this classification, an informational artifact cannot be treated as a binding governance object. The following table illustrates instances of classes that satisfy this basic axiom, serving as the elementary building blocks of the governance framework.

## 4.3 Enforcement Obligations

A directive without an enforcement mechanism is structurally void. To ensure operational efficacy, the governance framework mandates that any Policy must be enforced by a minimum of one Agent or Role. This requirement is captured by the `policy_min_one_enforcer` axiom, which stipulates that for a Policy to be valid, there must exist at least one entity (`Y`) that bears the responsibility of its execution.

This relationship is critical for audit trails. When a compliance officer reviews a directive, they must be able to immediately identify the accountable party. The enforcer may be a specific role, such as a Data Protection Officer, or an automated system component designated as a responsible agent. The constraint `min 1` ensures that there is no ambiguity regarding ownership; a policy that lacks a defined enforcer is considered incomplete and non-binding under the framework's validation rules.

## 4.4 Scope of Governance

To prevent regulatory overreach and ensure precise applicability, the framework defines strict boundaries regarding what a Policy may govern. The `policy_only_governs_artifacts` axiom asserts that a Policy may only have a governance scope over Artifacts (`cco:Artifact`). This restriction is fundamental to the separation of concerns within the ontology: policies govern information objects (documents, records, logs, databases) rather than governing people or physical processes directly.

By constraining the scope to artifacts, the framework facilitates automated compliance checking. A policy governing a "DatabaseRecord" can be programmatically evaluated against the state of that record. If a policy were permitted to govern a "Person" directly, the implementation would require subjective interpretation of human behavior, which is incompatible with precise technical governance. Therefore, all valid directives must be scoped to artifacts, ensuring that compliance is a matter of verifying the state and disposition of information entities.

## 4.5 Verification Protocols

While enforcement ensures a policy is acted upon, verification ensures that the requirements of the policy are being met. The `requirement_min_one_verification` axiom stipulates that any Requirement—a specific type of directive often nested within a broader policy—must be verified by at least one Verifier. This distinction is vital: enforcement is the mechanism of action, whereas verification is the mechanism of control and assurance.

In practice, this means that for every technical or procedural requirement defined in the system, there must be a corresponding check or validation process. For instance, if a requirement mandates "password complexity," the verifier might be an "AuthenticationModule." The axiom enforces a one-to-many dependency where the requirement cannot exist in isolation from its verification logic. This structure guarantees that the system is inherently auditable; every requirement carries with it the definition of how it is proven.

## 4.6 Attestation Processes

The final component of the governance framework involves the formal ratification of compliance through attestation. An attestation is defined as a Process (`bfo:Process`), specifically a temporal occurrence that unfolds over time. The `attestation_min_one_signer` axiom mandates that any such process must involve at least one Signer (`cco:Person`). This requirement anchors the digital or physical process to a responsible human agent, providing non-repudiation.

Attestation serves as the bridge between technical verification and legal accountability. While a system may verify a requirement automatically (as defined in Section 4.5), the attestation process is the human-led affirmation that the verification results are accurate and acceptable. By requiring a signer, the framework ensures that a natural person is legally assuming responsibility for the validity of the attested state. This process is critical for high-compliance domains such as financial reporting and safety-critical systems, where automated logs alone are insufficient for legal liability.
