# Chapter 7: Governance Frameworks and Directive Verification Mechanisms

## 7.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter establishes the structural requirements for directive governance within the enterprise ontology, delineating the formal relationships between information content entities, enforcement agents, and verification mechanisms. The provisions herein define the necessary conditions for a policy to be considered valid, enforceable, and verifiable within the system. Furthermore, this chapter specifies the constraints governing the scope of directives and the procedural requirements for attestation processes. Accordingly, the definitions and axioms presented serve as the foundation for all subsequent compliance auditing and regulatory reporting activities.

## 7.2 Basic Directive Structure

### 7.2.1 Definition and Ontological Status

A directive, within the context of the Common Core Ontologies (CCO), is classified as a Directive Information Content Entity (ICE). This classification asserts that the entity is not merely a collection of data but a prescriptive information artifact that conveys normative instructions. The basic axiom governing this concept is straightforward: for an entity to be managed as a governance object, it must first be instantiated as a member of the class of directives.

This proposed action merely proposes to define the foundational class of governance objects and does not impose additional semantic requirements beyond those inherent in the definition of a Directive ICE. For that reason, this classification:
*   Does not impose a data collection burden beyond the standard metadata requirements for information entities;
*   Is not a "complex regulatory action" subject to extended review, as it defines the base ontology layer;
*   Serves as the necessary antecedent for all subsequent enforcement and verification relations.

## 7.3 Enforcement Obligations

### 7.3.1 The Necessity of Enforcement

A directive without an designated agent or mechanism for enforcement is operationally void. The ontology mandates that any valid directive must be associated with a minimum of one enforcing entity. This relationship is captured by the axiom `policy_min_one_enforcer`, which asserts that a Directive ICE (`X`) must be enforced by a minimum of one entity (`Y`). This constraint ensures that accountability is baked into the definition of the policy itself, rather than being an afterthought.

The enforcing entity (`Y`) may be a specific role, an organizational unit, or an automated system, provided it has the capacity to execute the directives contained within the policy. This relationship is not merely advisory; it is a structural requirement for the policy to be considered active within the governance framework.

## 7.4 Scope Limitations: Artifact Governance

### 7.4.1 Defining the Governed Scope

Directives do not exist in a vacuum; they must apply to specific objects or processes within the enterprise. However, to maintain semantic precision and avoid ambiguity in regulatory reporting, the ontology restricts the scope of directives. Specifically, the axiom `policy_only_governs_artifacts` stipulates that a Directive ICE (`X`) governs a scope that is restricted exclusively to Artifacts (`cco:Artifact`).

This restriction has significant implications for compliance. It means that policies should not be written to govern "people" or "abstract concepts" directly. Rather, policies govern the artifacts associated with them—such as software configurations, documents, records, or physical equipment. By anchoring governance to artifacts, the framework ensures that compliance checks can be performed against tangible, auditable entities.

## 7.5 Verification Mechanisms

### 7.5.1 Distinction Between Enforcement and Verification

While enforcement ensures that a directive is executed, verification provides the evidence that the directive has been executed correctly. The axiom `requirement_min_one_verification` formalizes this by asserting that a Directive ICE (`X`), specifically when framed as a requirement, must be verified by a minimum of one entity or process (`Y`).

Verification is a critical component of the audit trail. It transforms a subjective claim of compliance ("we followed the policy") into an objective fact supported by evidence ("the auditor verified the logs"). The verifier (`Y`) may be an internal audit team, an external compliance consultant, or an automated validation script. The "min 1" cardinality ensures that no requirement is left unchecked or unverified.

## 7.6 Attestation Processes

### 7.6.1 The Process of Attestation

Attestation is the formal act of validating the truth or validity of something. In the context of this framework, attestation is modeled as a process (specifically, a subclass of `bfo:0000015`, a Process). The axiom `attestation_min_one_signer` asserts that for an attestation process (`X`) to be valid, it must be signed by at least one Person (`cco:Person`).

This distinction is vital: attestation is not a static document but a *process* that results in a signed assertion. The process involves the review of evidence, the application of judgment, and the final act of signing. The requirement for a "signer" ensures individual accountability and non-repudiation. Without a signer, an attestation process is incomplete and cannot be considered legally or procedurally binding.
