# Chapter 4: Governance Structures and Directive Compliance in the Secure Data Framework

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the structural components of the Secure Data Governance Framework (SDGF), specifically focusing on the ontological relationships that define directive information content entities. The objective is to establish a rigorous, auditable foundation for policy management, ensuring that all mandates are formally defined, properly scoped, and operationally enforceable. The provisions herein describe the necessary conditions for a policy to be considered valid within the system, the assignment of enforcement and verification authorities, and the procedural requirements for attestation. Compliance with these structural axioms is mandatory for all regulatory modules within the SDGF, ensuring that governance artifacts are not merely textual but are executable logic objects with defined agency and scope.

## 4.2 Foundational Directive Classification

At the most fundamental level, the governance framework relies on the precise categorization of regulatory instruments. Within the ontology, a directive is not merely a suggestion but a codified information entity that carries prescriptive weight. The axiom `policy_basic` establishes the baseline definition for these entities. It posits that any entity classified as a Policy (X) is, by definition, a Directive Information Content Entity (`cco:DirectiveICE`). This classification serves as the primary ontological anchor, distinguishing governance artifacts from unstructured documentation.

This distinction is critical for auditability. By treating policies as distinct information entities, the framework allows for versioning, automated dissemination, and conflict resolution. A "Policy" in this context represents a stable, high-level mandate—such as a Data Retention Policy or an Access Control Standard—that persists over time and governs organizational behavior. ## 4.3 Enforcement Mechanisms and Agency

A directive without an assigned agent remains theoretical; it lacks the mechanism to transition from a static state to an active state of compliance. To address this, the framework utilizes the axiom `policy_min_one_enforcer`. This axiom extends the definition of a Policy (X) by imposing a cardinality constraint: it must be enforced by a minimum of one Enforcer (Y). The Enforcer is typically an organizational role, a committee, or an automated system agent responsible for the execution of the policy's mandates.

This relationship creates a binding dependency. The validity of a policy within the SDGF is contingent upon the existence of a designated enforcement authority. This prevents the creation of "orphan" policies that prescribe rules without assigning responsibility. For instance, a Data Classification Policy is effectively dormant unless a specific governance body, such as the Data Management Office, is formally linked to it via this axiom. The following table illustrates this binding, mapping specific policy directives to their designated enforcement bodies. ## 4.4 Verification Protocols and Audit Trails

While enforcement ensures the active application of a rule, verification provides the necessary assurance that the rule has been applied correctly. The axiom `requirement_min_one_verification` addresses this by distinguishing the "Requirement" subclass of directives. It asserts that a Requirement (X) must be verified by at least one Verifier (Y). This separation of concerns—between the entity that enforces a rule and the entity that verifies its adherence—is a cornerstone of the "separation of duties" principle inherent in the framework.

In practice, this means that while a Security Team may enforce a Multi-Factor Authentication requirement, the verification of that requirement's efficacy might fall to an Internal Audit Committee or a third-party assessor. This axiom ensures that compliance is not merely self-reported but is subject to objective validation. The relational structure below captures these verification assignments. The `x` column identifies the specific requirement being audited, and the `y` column identifies the entity authorized to perform the verification, ensuring that every critical requirement has an associated check mechanism.

## 4.5 Scope Limitation: The Artifact-Centric Constraint

A common failure mode in governance frameworks is scope creep, where policies are written so broadly that they attempt to govern human behavior directly rather than the informational objects that humans interact with. The axiom `policy_only_governs_artifacts` imposes a strict ontological constraint to prevent this. It stipulates that a Policy (X) governs a scope that is restricted exclusively to Artifacts (`cco:Artifact`). An Artifact is defined as a discrete information entity, such as a document, a database record, a source code file, or a system configuration.

By limiting the scope of governance to artifacts, the framework ensures that policies are machine-readable and enforceable at the data layer. A policy governs a "Financial Record" or a "Source Code Repository," not the "Accountant" or the "Developer" directly. The human element is addressed through the enforcement and attestation relationships described elsewhere, but the direct object of the governance rule is always the artifact. This constraint is essential for automated policy enforcement engines (e.g., Data Loss Prevention tools) that require a defined target artifact type to function. ## 4.6 Attestation Processes and Signatory Authority

The final component of the governance structure involves the formal acknowledgment of compliance. Attestation is modeled not as a static state but as a process (`bfo:0000015`) that occurs over time. The axiom `attestation_min_one_signer` defines the parameters of this process. It asserts that an Attestation (X) must be signed by a minimum of one Person (`cco:Person`). This requirement ensures that there is always individual accountability associated with the completion of a governance process.

Attestation serves as the "handshake" between the governed process and the governance framework. It is the mechanism by which a human agent certifies that a particular set of directives has been satisfied or that a specific artifact complies with the relevant policy. By requiring a signer, the framework creates a non-repudiable link between the process outcome and a responsible individual. This is critical for regulatory reporting and internal investigations.
