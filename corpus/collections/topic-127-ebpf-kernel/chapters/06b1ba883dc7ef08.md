# Chapter 7: Governance Directives and Verification Protocols

This chapter establishes the formal ontological framework governing the creation, verification, and attestation of regulatory directives within the enterprise. It delineates the structural relationships between information content entities, processes, and agents, ensuring that all compliance activities are traceable, evidence-based, and authorized. The following sections define the requisite axioms for binding requirements to verification procedures, mandating evidence production, and enforcing signatory accountability.

## 1. Attestation Processes and Minimum Signatory Requirements

### 1.1 Definition of Attestation as a Process
In the context of governance, an attestation is formally defined as a specific type of process (`bfo:0000015`). It is not merely a static document but a temporal occurrence that validates the integrity of a state of affairs or a set of data. To be valid within the system, this process must be initiated by an authorized agent. The axiom `attestation_min_one_signer` asserts that any class of attestation (`X`) must involve a signatory relationship with at least one person (`cco:Person`). This ensures that no automated or purely procedural attestation occurs without human accountability and explicit endorsement.

### 1.2 Implementation of Signatory Constraints
The enforcement of this axiom is critical for audit trails. When an attestation process is instantiated, the system validates that the `sdg:signedBy` property is populated with a valid reference to a natural person. This prevents the delegation of sign-off authority to non-accountable system agents. For instance, a "Quarterly Compliance Attestation" cannot be marked as complete until a designated Compliance Officer has applied their digital signature, thereby linking the process to a liable individual.

## 2. Verification Mandates for Directive Requirements

### 2.1 Linking Directives to Verification
Directives within the governance framework are modeled as Directive Information Content Entities (`cco:DirectiveICE`). However, the issuance of a directive is insufficient to ensure compliance; there must exist a procedural mechanism to confirm adherence. The axiom `requirement_min_one_verification` stipulates that any requirement class (`X`) must be verified by at least one verification class (`Y`). This relationship (`sdg:verifiedBy`) creates a binding contract between the normative statement (the requirement) and the operational check (the verification).

### 2.2 Verification Class Structures
The verification class (`Y`) represents the methodology or protocol used to assess compliance. This could be an automated script, a manual audit, or a third-party inspection. By mandating a minimum of one verification, the ontology ensures that every requirement is testable. A requirement without a corresponding verification class is considered incomplete and is rejected during the ontology validation phase.

## 3. Evidence Production in Verification Processes

### 3.1 The Role of Artifacts in Verification
Verification processes are substantive activities that must result in the creation of observable data. According to the axiom `verification_min_one_evidence`, a verification class (`X`), which is a process (`bfo:0000015`), must produce at least one evidence class (`Y`). The `sdg:producesEvidence` property links the act of verification to the resulting artifact. This artifact serves as the immutable record of the verification event, available for future audit or inspection.

### 3.2 Evidence Class Typing
The evidence class (`Y`) can take various forms, such as a PDF report, a database log snapshot, or a signed checksum. The critical constraint is that the evidence must be a distinct entity generated by the process. This axiom prevents "empty" verifications where a process is executed without generating a traceable record of its execution or findings.

## 4. Specification and Targeting of Requirements

### 4.1 Decomposition of Directive Content
To be actionable, a requirement must possess both semantic content and a defined scope. The axiom `requirement_equiv_specifies_target` provides an equivalence definition for a requirement class (`X`). It states that `X` is equivalent to a DirectiveICE that specifies some content (`Y`) and applies to some target (`Z`). The `sdg:specifies` property captures the normative substance of the directive (e.g., "wear protective gear"), while the `sdg:appliesTo` property identifies the entity class subject to the directive (e.g., "laboratory technician").

### 4.2 Targeting and Scope Definition
This tripartite structure (Requirement—Content—Target) allows for precise governance queries. It enables the system to answer questions such as "What requirements apply to this specific class of assets?" or "What specific controls are mandated for this personnel role?" The equivalence axiom ensures that a requirement is never defined in a vacuum; it is intrinsically bound to its subject matter and its target audience.

## 5. Signatory Authorization in Attestation

### 5.1 The Act of Signing
While Section 1 established the *necessity* of a signer, this section addresses the *nature* of the signing act itself. The axiom `attestation_signed_by` asserts that an attestation class (`X`) is a process (`bfo:0000015`) that is signed by some person (`cco:Person`). The use of the existential quantifier ("some") here emphasizes the relationship between the process and the agent. It formalizes the `sdg:signedBy` property as the mechanism by which the attestation process is finalized and authenticated.

### 5.2 Distinction from Cardinality Constraints
It is important to distinguish this axiom from the minimum cardinality constraint in Section 1. While `attestation_min_one_signer` ensures the *capacity* for signatories exists (at least one), `attestation_signed_by` defines the *ontological nature* of the attestation as an event that necessitates a signature. This reinforces that an attestation is not merely a process that *can* be signed, but one that *is* signed by virtue of its completion. This distinction is crucial for logic reasoning engines that must infer the status of a process based on the presence or absence of a signature property.
