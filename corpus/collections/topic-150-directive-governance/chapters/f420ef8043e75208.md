### 4.1 Preamble and Scope

This chapter establishes the formal ontological framework for managing compliance artifacts within the Secure Data Governance (SDG) ecosystem. It delineates the structural relationships between attestation processes, audit trails, and specific regulatory directives, particularly those governing Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) adherence. The definitions provided herein serve as the authoritative schema for generating evidentiary records and ensuring traceability across governance domains. All personnel involved in the creation, maintenance, or auditing of compliance records must adhere to the entity-relationship constraints defined in the subsequent sections.

---

## 1. Attestation Processes and Signatory Authority

### 1.1 Ontological Definition of Attestation

In the context of formal governance, an attestation is defined not merely as a signature, but as a specific temporal process (`bfo:0000015`) that validates the integrity of a state of affairs. According to Axiom 1 (`attestation_signed_by`), any entity classified as an attestation process is ontologically a subclass of a process that necessitates action by a `cco:Person`. This relationship is captured through the object property `sdg:signedBy`.

The distinction between the process and the agent is critical. The attestation exists as a discrete event in time, while the signatory exists as an independent entity. The governance framework requires that for every instance of an attestation process, there must be a corresponding record linking it to a specific natural person. This ensures non-repudiation and establishes a clear chain of custody for all compliance artifacts.

### 1.2 Cardinality Constraints in Signatory Protocols

While the existence of a signatory is mandatory, governance frameworks often impose quantitative constraints on the signing process to prevent unilateral authorization of critical changes. Axiom 3 (`attestation_min_one_signer`) refines the general definition of attestation by imposing a minimum cardinality constraint. It asserts that any attestation process must be signed by at least one (`min 1`) `cco:Person`.

This axiom serves as a validation rule within the compliance database. It prohibits the creation of "orphan" attestation records—processes that have been initiated but lack completion via a signature. In practice, this constraint is enforced at the application layer, preventing the submission of compliance forms until the signatory field is populated with a valid personnel identifier.

## 2. Audit Execution and Personnel Accountability

### 2.1 The Audit Process as a Governance Event

Audits represent the primary mechanism for verifying compliance with established directives. Axiom 4 (`audit_conducted_by`) defines the audit as a distinct subclass of process (`bfo:0000015`), characterized specifically by the property `sdg:conductedBy`. Unlike attestation, which may be a declarative act, an audit implies an investigative or verification activity performed by an agent.

The ontology distinguishes between the *role* of an auditor and the *person* fulfilling that role. However, at the data layer, this is resolved by linking the audit process instance directly to a `cco:Person` identifier. This direct linkage allows the governance framework to query all audits conducted by a specific individual, facilitating conflict-of-interest checks and workload balancing.

### 2.2 Audit Trail Verification

To maintain the evidentiary value of an audit, the system must record the provenance of the audit event itself. The relational schema for audits mirrors that of attestations but utilizes the distinct `conductedBy` property to separate the acts of verification (auditing) from the acts of approval (attestation).

## 3. PCI DSS Directives and Requirement Mapping

### 3.1 Directive Structure and Equivalence

The complexity of regulatory compliance is managed through the use of Directives. Axiom 5 (`requirement_equiv_specifies_target`) provides a complex definition for a specific class of Directives (`cco:DirectiveICE`). This axiom establishes an equivalence class: a Directive is defined by its capacity to *specify* a requirement (`Y`) and *apply to* a target (`Z`).

This structure is foundational for the PCI DSS implementation. It dictates that a directive cannot exist in a vacuum; it must be intrinsically linked to a specific requirement (the "what") and a specific target system or process (the "where"). The relational schema enforces this by treating the requirement (`y`) as a foreign key referencing the PCI DSS requirement table, creating a tightly coupled graph of governance objects.

### 3.2 PCI DSS Requirement Integration

Axiom 2 (`pci_dss_requirement`) further specializes this structure. It posits that a specific class (`X`) is a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` which possesses the property `sdg:pciDssRequirement`. This property links the internal directive to an external regulatory standard (`Y`).

In the relational model, this creates a bi-directional referential integrity constraint. The `t_pci_dss_requirement` table links a Directive to a Requirement definition, while the `t_requirement_equiv_specifies_target` table links that same Requirement definition back to the Directive and its Target. This circular dependency ensures that a PCI DSS requirement is never defined without a corresponding directive, and a directive is never created without a valid requirement specification.
