# Chapter 4: Interoperability of Governance Directives and Control Frameworks

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the structural mapping between high-level governance directives and specific control frameworks, specifically focusing on the integration of ISO/IEC 27001 Annex A controls, PCI DSS requirements, and risk taxonomies. The objective is to provide a formalized ontology for aligning compliance artifacts—directives, requirements, and risk statements—within a unified governance model. By defining the precise relational constraints between these entities, organizations can achieve evidence-based audit trails and automated compliance verification. The following sections detail the axiomatic relationships that govern these mappings, supported by data schemas representative of a production-grade governance repository.

## 4.2 ISO 27001 Annex A Subclassing

In the context of information security management, directives are often abstract mandates that must be grounded in specific control objectives. The axiom `iso27001_annexa_subclass` formalizes the relationship where a governance directive (Class X) is defined as a subclass of the `cco:DirectiveICE` (Information Control Element) and specifically necessitates the implementation of an ISO/IEC 27001 Annex A control (Class Y).

This relationship ensures that a high-level policy, such as "Data Protection Governance," is not merely a statement of intent but is structurally bound to a specific control mechanism within the ISO framework, such as "A.10.1.1 Cryptographic controls." The linkage allows for the automated derivation of compliance gaps; if the directive exists, the Annex A control must be present and evidenced.

### 4.2.1 Relational Implementation

The implementation of this axiom relies on a table structure that captures the identity of the directive, the specific Annex A control it invokes, and the unique identifier of the record. Notably, the schema enforces a foreign key constraint linking the Annex A control (`y`) to a threat classification table, implying that controls are selected based on the specific threats they mitigate.

**Table 4-1: ISO 27001 Annex A Subclass Mapping**
This table embodies the `iso27001_annexa_subclass` axiom, representing the binding of directives to specific ISO 27001 controls. The primary key is `id`, while `y` serves as a foreign key referencing the threat class addressed by the control.

| id | x (Directive) | y (ISO 27001 Control) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| DIR-2023-001 | AccessControlPolicy | THREAT_CLASS_A |
| DIR-2023-002 | DataEncryptionStandard | THREAT_CLASS_B |

This structural dependency is critical for maintaining the integrity of the control framework; it prevents the existence of orphaned directives that do not map to a recognized threat landscape.

## 4.3 Control-Only Addresses Threat Class

While general directives may address multiple concerns, certain controls are designed with a singular, focused purpose: the mitigation of a specific threat class. The axiom `control_only_addresses_threat_class` defines a specialized subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` (Class X) that addresses a specific risk or threat (Class Y) to the exclusion of others.

This distinction is vital for granular risk assessment. When a control is classified under this axiom, auditors can infer that the evidence provided for this control applies exclusively to the identified threat. This prevents the "double-counting" of control efficacy across disparate risk categories. For instance, a specific firewall rule set might be defined to address *only* the threat of "Unauthorized Remote Access," rather than broader "Network Availability" issues.

### 4.3.1 Reciprocal Mapping and Constraints

The relational schema for this concept introduces a reciprocal dependency with the ISO 27001 Annex A table. The `y` slot (the threat) in this table references the `id` of the ISO Annex A table. This circular reference (`t_iso27001_annexa_subclass` references `t_control_only_addresses_threat_class` and vice versa) models a real-world governance scenario where a control is defined by the threat it addresses (`THREAT_CLASS_A`), and that threat is formally categorized within the ISO framework (`ISO_CTRL_A`).

**Table 4-2: Control-Only Addresses Threat Class**
This table embodies the `control_only_addresses_threat_class` axiom. It lists directives that are scoped to address specific risks exclusively. The column `y` references the ISO control ID, establishing the link between the threat definition and the control standard.

| id | x (Directive) | y (Risk/Threat) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| THREAT_CLASS_A | MalwareInjectionControl | ISO_CTRL_A |
| THREAT_CLASS_B | EavesdroppingMitigation | ISO_CTRL_B |
| THREAT_CLASS_C | TailgatingPrevention | ISO_CTRL_C |
| THREAT_CLASS_D | DataLeakageExfiltration | ISO_CTRL_D |

In Table 4-2, the entity `THREAT_CLASS_A` is identified as a directive (`MalwareInjectionControl`) that addresses a risk mapped to `ISO_CTRL_A`. This mapping confirms that the control defined in the ISO framework is the appropriate response to the specific threat classified here. The foreign key constraint ensures that every threat addressed is traceable back to a ratified control standard.

## 4.4 PCI DSS Requirement Integration

Organizations operating within the payment card industry must align their information security directives with the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS). The axiom `pci_dss_requirement` extends the `cco:DirectiveICE` by asserting that a given directive (Class X) satisfies a specific PCI DSS requirement (Class Y).

This mapping serves as the bridge between internal governance policies and external regulatory obligations. By populating this table, an organization demonstrates that its internal directives (e.g., "Wireless Access Policy") are sufficient to meet explicit PCI DSS requirements (e.g., "Req 1.2.3"). The axiom structure implies that the PCI requirement is satisfied through the implementation of a control that addresses a specific threat, linking the compliance requirement back to the risk-based approach defined in previous sections.

### 4.4.1 Compliance Verification

The schema for this axiom links the PCI requirement (`y`) to the threat class table. This implies that PCI compliance is achieved not just by stating a requirement, but by implementing a directive that successfully addresses the underlying threat class identified in the governance model.

**Table 4-3: PCI DSS Requirement Mapping**
This table embodies the `pci_dss_requirement` axiom. It maps internal directives to specific PCI DSS requirements. The `y` column references the threat class, indicating that the PCI requirement is fulfilled by addressing that specific threat.

| id | x (Directive) | y (PCI DSS Requirement) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| PCI-REQ-001 | FirewallConfigurationRule | THREAT_CLASS_A |
| PCI-REQ-002 | WirelessAccessPointControl | THREAT_CLASS_B |
| PCI-REQ-003 | CardDataEncryptionPolicy | THREAT_CLASS_D |
| PCI-REQ-004 | AntiVirusUpdateMechanism | THREAT_CLASS_A |

As shown in Table 4-3, the `FirewallConfigurationRule` directive is mapped to a PCI requirement associated with `THREAT_CLASS_A`. This indicates that the firewall configuration is the mechanism by which the organization meets the PCI standard while simultaneously mitigating the defined threat (e.g., Malware Injection). This multi-dimensional mapping allows for unified reporting where a single control artifact satisfies both internal risk management and external compliance mandates.

## 4.5 Requirement Equivalence: Specifies and Applies To

Complex governance environments often require equivalence classes where a requirement is defined not just by its existence, but by its specification and its target. The axiom `requirement_equiv_specifies_target` defines a Class X as equivalent to the intersection of two conditions: it specifies a particular directive (Class Y) and applies to a specific target system or asset class (Class Z).

This axiom is crucial for scope definition. It answers the question: "To which assets does this specific control specification apply?" For example, a directive specifying "Two-Factor Authentication" (Y) might apply only to the "Corporate Email System" (Z). This equivalence ensures that the control is not applied universally where it may not be appropriate, nor is it omitted where it is required.

### 4.5.1 Targeted Governance

The relational structure for this axiom enforces a mandatory link between the requirement and the ISO control it specifies (`y`), while simultaneously capturing the target (`z`). This creates a triple constraint: the requirement exists, it specifies a control, and it applies to a target.

**Table 4-4: Requirement Equivalence (Specifies/Target)**
This table embodies the `requirement_equiv_specifies_target` axiom. It defines requirements that are valid only when they specify a control and apply to a target. The `y` column references the ISO control, ensuring the requirement is grounded in the control framework.

| id | x (Requirement) | y (Specifies Control) | z (Applies To Target) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| REQ-EQ-001 | AuthSpecMandatory | ISO_CTRL_A | HR_Production_DB |
| REQ-EQ-002 | CryptoStandardTransit | ISO_CTRL_B | Customer_Portal_API |
| REQ-EQ-003 | PhysicalEntryLog | ISO_CTRL_C | DataCenter_Floor_2 |
| REQ-EQ-004 | MediaDisposalProc | ISO_CTRL_D | Backup_Site_Office |

In Table 4-4, `REQ-EQ-001` represents a requirement that specifies `ISO_CTRL_A` (likely an Access Control) and applies specifically to the `HR_Production_DB`. This level of granularity is essential for audit evidence, as it restricts the scope of the control assessment to the defined target, preventing scope creep and ensuring precise resource allocation during audits.

## 4.6 Control Addresses Risk

The final concept, `control_addresses_risk`, represents the general relationship between a directive and a risk. Unlike the "control-only" axiom, which implies exclusivity, this axiom (`control_addresses_risk`) asserts that a directive (Class X) addresses a risk (Class Y) as one of its potentially multiple functions.

This is the standard mapping for most defense-in-depth strategies. A single directive, such as a "Security Awareness Training" program, might address multiple risks, including "Social Engineering," "Phishing," and "Insider Threat." The schema links the risk back to the ISO Annex A subclass, ensuring that the risk being addressed is recognized within the overarching control framework.

This table captures the many-to-many relationships inherent in modern security postures. While the "control-only" table handles specific, singular mappings, this table aggregates the broader risk mitigation landscape.
