# Chapter 4: Governance Frameworks and Control Mapping in Multi-Regulatory Environments

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the structural relationships between information security directives, risk mitigation strategies, and regulatory compliance frameworks within a unified governance ontology. The primary objective is to establish a precise mapping between internal control directives and external regulatory standards, specifically ISO/IEC 27001 and the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS). The content herein defines the axiomatic structure of these relationships, providing a rigorous method for auditing control efficacy and verifying compliance through formal semantic constraints. Readers are assumed to be familiar with the foundational concepts of the Common Control Ontology (cco) and the Security Directive Graph (sdg).

## 4.2 ISO 27001 Annex A Control Subclassing

The foundational element of the governance framework is the classification of internal directives according to ISO/IEC 27001 Annex A controls. In this ontology, a directive is not merely a policy statement but a specific subclass of the `cco:DirectiveICE` (Information Control Element) that explicitly realizes a control objective defined in the ISO standard.

Formally, this relationship is defined by the axiom: `{X} is something that iso27001 annex a {Y}`. Here, `{X}` represents the specific internal directive or control class implemented by the organization, while `{Y}` represents the abstract control class defined in ISO 27001 Annex A. This subclassing ensures that any instance of `{X}` inherits the properties and obligations associated with the ISO standard, providing a traceable lineage from operational procedure to regulatory compliance.

To implement this axiomatically, the relational schema enforces that the ISO control reference (`y`) must correspond to a valid entry in the threat-addressing control registry. This constraint prevents the citation of obsolete or non-existent control classes and ensures that every ISO mapping is grounded in an active risk mitigation directive.

**Table 4.1: ISO 27001 Annex A Subclass Mappings**
The following table illustrates the instantiation of directives (`x`) that are subclassed under specific ISO 27001 Annex A controls (`y`). The `id` column provides the unique identifier for the directive class within the ontology.

| id | x (Directive Class) | y (ISO 27001 Annex A Control) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| dir_iso_001 | AccessControlPolicyDirective | Annex_A.9_Access_Control |
| dir_iso_002 | CryptographyControlDirective | Annex_A.10_Cryptography |
| dir_iso_003 | OperationsSecurityDirective | Annex_A.12_Operations_Security |
| dir_iso_004 | SystemAcquisitionDirective | Annex_A.14_System_Acquisition |
| dir_iso_005 | SupplierRelationshipsDirective | Annex_A.15_Supplier_Relationships |

In the context of an audit, Table 4.1 serves as the primary evidence of control coverage. For instance, the `AccessControlPolicyDirective` (id: dir_iso_001) is formally recognized as an implementation of `Annex_A.9_Access_Control`. This mapping allows auditors to query the ontology for all directives fulfilling a specific compliance obligation, thereby streamlining the evidence collection process.

## 4.3 Control-Only Risk Addressing

While the previous section establishes the link to standards, this section addresses the functional scope of a control. A critical distinction in governance is determining whether a control addresses a specific risk to the exclusion of all others. The ontology captures this through the axiom: `{X} is something that addresses risk {Y}`.

This axiom utilizes a universal restriction (`only`), implying that the directive `{X}` is constrained to address the risk class `{Y}` and no other. This is a stronger assertion than a general association; it defines the exclusive boundary of the control's operational scope. In the relational model, this creates a mutual dependency where the risk addressed (`y`) must refer back to an ISO-defined directive, reinforcing the tight coupling between the standard control and the specific threat it mitigates.

This structure is essential for "gap analysis." If a directive claims to address a risk but does not map back to a recognized ISO control class via the foreign key constraint, it indicates a "zombie" control— a mitigation effort that lacks grounding in the formal framework. Conversely, if a directive mapped to an ISO control fails to address the specific risk identified in this table, the implementation is non-compliant.

**Table 4.2: Exclusive Risk Addressing by Control**
The table below defines directives (`x`) that are constrained to address specific risks (`y`). The foreign key relationship mandates that the risk identifier (`y`) must exist as a valid ISO directive identifier.

| id | x (Directive) | y (Risk Addressed / ISO Directive Ref) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| ctrl_excl_001 | PhysicalEntryControl | dir_iso_001 |
| ctrl_excl_002 | TransmissionEncryptionProtocol | dir_iso_002 |
| ctrl_excl_003 | MalwareExfiltrationPrevention | dir_iso_003 |
| ctrl_excl_004 | SecureDevelopmentLifecycle | dir_iso_004 |
| ctrl_excl_005 | ThirdPartyDataProcessingAgreement | dir_iso_005 |

For example, `PhysicalEntryControl` (id: ctrl_excl_001) is axiomatically restricted to address the risk defined by `dir_iso_001`. This ensures that the control's scope is strictly limited to the domain of Access Control, preventing scope creep and ensuring precise resource allocation during risk treatment planning.

## 4.4 PCI DSS Requirement Mapping

In multi-regulatory environments, a single internal directive often satisfies multiple external frameworks. This section defines the mapping between internal directives and the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS). The governing axiom is: `{X} is something that pci dss requirement {Y}`.

This relationship asserts that the directive `{X}` fulfills the stipulations of PCI DSS requirement `{Y}`. The relational schema enforces that the PCI requirement (`y`) must be realized through a directive that is already defined as an exclusive risk-addressing control (referencing `t_control_only_addresses_threat_class`). This creates a hierarchy where PCI compliance is derived from the foundational risk mitigation controls defined in Section 4.3.

This dependency ensures that organizations cannot claim PCI DSS compliance via abstract policy statements alone. They must demonstrate that the PCI requirement is satisfied by a concrete control (`x`) that actively addresses a specific risk (`y`). This "evidence-anchored" approach is critical for passing ROC (Report on Compliance) reviews, as it provides a direct lineage from the high-level PCI requirement down to the technical control.

**Table 4.3: PCI DSS Requirement Satisfaction**
The following table maps internal directives (`x`) to specific PCI DSS requirements (`y`). The `y` column references the exclusive control directives established in Table 4.2.

| id | x (PCI Directive) | y (PCI DSS Requirement / Control Ref) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| pci_req_001 | RestrictAccessBasedOnNeedToKnow | ctrl_excl_001 |
| pci_req_002 | EncryptTransmissionOfCardholderData | ctrl_excl_002 |
| pci_req_003 | UseAntiVirusSoftware | ctrl_excl_003 |
| pci_req_004 | DevelopSecureSystemsAndApps | ctrl_excl_004 |
| pci_req_005 | VerifyAndManageThirdPartyProviders | ctrl_excl_005 |

Consider the entry `pci_req_002`. The directive `EncryptTransmissionOfCardholderData` satisfies the PCI requirement by referencing `ctrl_excl_002`. Tracing the lineage, `ctrl_excl_002` is the `TransmissionEncryptionProtocol`, which exclusively addresses the risk defined in `dir_iso_002` (Cryptography). This chain of evidence proves that the organization's PCI compliance is grounded in a robust, ISO-aligned cryptographic control framework.

## 4.5 Requirement Equivalence: Specification and Target

The complexity of modern governance requires that directives be decomposed into specific specifications (`sdg:specifies`) that apply to distinct targets (`sdg:appliesTo`). The axiom for this concept is: `{X} EquivalentTo: cco:DirectiveICE and (sdg:specifies some {Y}) and (sdg:appliesTo some {Z})`.

This equivalence class defines a requirement `{X}` not just by its existence, but by its semantic composition: it must specify a parameter or methodology `{Y}` and apply to a target entity `{Z}`. The schema constrains the specification (`y`) to be a valid ISO directive, ensuring that the detailed requirements are always extensions of the standard control classes.

This level of granularity is necessary for automated policy enforcement. By defining the target (`z`), the system can dynamically apply the requirement `{X}` only to relevant assets (e.g., "Cardholder Data Environment" or "Employee Database"). By linking the specification (`y`) to the ISO directive, the system ensures that the configuration parameters are aligned with industry best practices.

**Table 4.4: Requirement Specification and Target Application**
This table details the decomposition of requirements into specifications and targets. The `y` column links the specification to the ISO Annex A subclass.

| id | x (Requirement) | y (Specification / ISO Ref) | z (Target) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| req_spec_001 | PasswordComplexityRule | dir_iso_001 | ActiveDirectory_UserStore |
| req_spec_002 | TLS1.3Implementation | dir_iso_002 | PublicFacingWebGateway |
| req_spec_003 | LogRetentionPolicy | dir_iso_003 | SIEM_Collector |
| req_spec_004 | CodeReviewChecklist | dir_iso_004 | GitHub_Repository_Main |
| req_spec_005 | DataResidencyClause | dir_iso_005 | SaaS_Provider_Contract |

For instance, `req_spec_001` defines a `PasswordComplexityRule`. This rule specifies parameters derived from `dir_iso_001` (Access Control) and applies specifically to the `ActiveDirectory_UserStore`. This precise definition prevents the erroneous application of strict password policies to service accounts or low-risk systems, thereby optimizing security operations.

## 4.6 General Risk Addressing

Unlike the exclusive risk addressing defined in Section 4.3, general risk addressing acknowledges that some directives mitigate risks as part of a broader portfolio. The axiom is: `{X} is something that addresses risk {Y}`.

Here, the relationship is existential (`some`) rather than universal (`only`). This indicates that while `{X}` addresses risk `{Y}`, it may also address other risks or serve broader governance purposes. The schema links the risk (`y`) back to the ISO directive, maintaining the connection to the standard framework while allowing for flexibility in control implementation.

This category is typically used for overarching governance frameworks, such as an "Information Security Policy" that addresses multiple risks (Access, Cryptography, Operations) simultaneously. While the specific controls (Section 4.3) are the "hammers" hitting specific nails, these general directives are the "hands" guiding the overall strategy.

**Table 4.5: General Risk Addressing Mappings**
The table below lists directives (`x`) that address risks (`y`) as part of their broader operational mandate.

| id | x (General Directive) | y (Risk Addressed / ISO Ref) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| ctrl_gen_001 | EnterpriseSecurityAwarenessProgram | dir_iso_001 |
| ctrl_gen_002 | DataLossPreventionStrategy | dir_iso_002 |
| ctrl_gen_003 | IncidentResponsePlan | dir_iso_003 |
| ctrl_gen_004 | VendorRiskManagementProgram | dir_iso_005 |
| ctrl_gen_005 | BusinessContinuityPlan | dir_iso_003 |

In this table, `ctrl_gen_001` (Enterprise Security Awareness Program) addresses the risk associated with `dir_iso_001` (Access Control). However, unlike the exclusive control in Table 4.2, this program likely also addresses risks related to phishing (Operations Security) or physical security. The ontology captures this nuance through the `some` restriction, allowing for a more holistic view of governance interactions.
