# Chapter 4: Artifact Attribute Specification and Compliance Standards

This chapter establishes the protocols for defining and recording essential attributes of physical and digital artifacts within the enterprise data governance framework. It delineates the ontological relationships between artifacts and their commercial or geospatial properties, ensuring data integrity across supply chain management, financial auditing, and location-based services. Adherence to these specifications is mandatory for all entities submitting product or location data to the central registry to maintain compliance with ISO/IEC 8000 data quality standards.

## 1. Brand Attribution and Lineage Tracking

### 1.1 Regulatory Framework for Brand Identity

In the context of asset management and commercial compliance, the assignment of a brand to an artifact is not merely a descriptive exercise but a regulatory requirement for liability and warranty enforcement. The ontology defines a specific constraint wherein an Artifact (`X`) must be associated with a Brand (`Y`) through the `sdg:hasBrand` property. This relationship ensures that every tangible or intangible asset within the inventory can be traced back to a responsible legal entity or manufacturer.

For the purposes of audit readiness, the brand entity must be distinct from the artifact itself. The `cco:Artifact` class represents the physical item or software instance, while the brand represents the organizational or commercial signature. This distinction is critical in scenarios involving recalls, where the ability to filter artifacts by brand (`Y`) allows for rapid isolation of affected lots. Failure to maintain a strict one-to-many or many-to-many mapping between artifacts and brands in the registry results in non-compliance penalties under Section 4.2 of the Global Supply Chain Accord.

### 1.2 Implementation and Data Entry

Data stewards are required to verify the brand designation against the manufacturer's certificate of origin prior to entry. The system enforces this by validating that the `Y` value in the relationship corresponds to an existing entry in the authorized vendor registry. ## 2. Financial Valuation and Price Schema Compliance

### 2.1 Standardization of Price Attributes

The financial valuation of artifacts is governed by strict accounting principles requiring the separation of the object from its value. The ontology captures this through the `sdg:hasPrice` relationship, where an Artifact (`X`) possesses a Price (`Y`). In this schema, the price is treated as a distinct class or value object rather than a primitive data type, allowing for complex attributes such as currency codes, tax inclusivity flags, and effective date ranges to be associated with the price entity itself.

This abstraction facilitates multi-currency reporting and historical price tracking. When an artifact undergoes a valuation change, a new instance of the Price class (`Y`) is generated and linked to the Artifact (`X`), preserving the audit trail. This mechanism is essential for compliance with International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS), specifically regarding the valuation of inventory assets. It prevents the overwriting of historical financial data and ensures that price fluctuations are logged as discrete events with immutable timestamps.

### 2.2 Audit Verification Protocols

Auditors must verify that the price linked to an artifact reflects the current fair market value or the standardized list price as defined in the master pricing catalog. Any discrepancy between the `Y` value in the database and the invoiced amount must be flagged for review. ## 3. Locational Data Integrity and Postal Address Standardization

### 3.1 Geospatial and Postal Referencing

For artifacts that represent fixed locations or facilities, the integration of postal address data is a foundational requirement for logistics and regulatory reporting. The axiom `sdg:hasPostalAddress` defines a relationship where an Artifact (`X`), specifically a facility or site, is characterized by a Postal Address (`Y`). This relationship ensures that physical assets are anchored to verifiable real-world coordinates, facilitating jurisdictional determinations for tax and compliance purposes.

The Postal Address class (`Y`) must adhere to the format defined by the Universal Postal Union (UPU) S42 standard. This includes structured fields for street names, locality, region, and postal codes, but within the context of this ontology, the address is treated as a semantic class linked to the artifact. This allows a single artifact (e.g., a distribution center) to possess multiple address types (e.g., physical, billing, shipping) without altering the core artifact definition.

### 3.2 Validation and Error Handling

Data entry interfaces must validate that the `Y` value (Postal Address) is not a free-text string but a reference to a validated address object. This prevents "dirty data" issues such as inconsistent abbreviations or missing locality names. During site audits, inspectors will cross-reference the artifact ID (`X`) with the registered postal address (`Y`) to verify the physical location of the asset. Mismatches between the registered address and the site visit findings constitute a critical compliance failure.

## 4. Geospatial Coordinate Precision and Longitude Verification

### 4.1 Coordinate System Requirements

The precise geolocation of artifacts is essential for operational transparency, particularly in sectors governed by environmental and zoning regulations. The ontology specifies that an Artifact (`X`) must have a Longitude (`Y`), defined via the `sdg:hasLongitude` property. While latitude is also a necessary component of geolocation, this chapter isolates longitude to demonstrate the handling of scalar coordinate data as distinct classes within the schema.

The Longitude class (`Y`) encapsulates not only the numeric value but also the reference datum (typically WGS 84). By treating longitude as a class, the system can attach metadata regarding the precision of the measurement and the method of acquisition (e.g., GPS survey, geocoding interpolation). This level of granularity is required for high-stakes environments such as pipeline management or aviation, where minute errors in longitudinal positioning can result in significant regulatory breaches.

### 4.2 Precision and Tolerance Levels

Compliance protocols dictate that longitudinal values must be recorded to a minimum of six decimal places (approximately 0.11 meters of precision). The system will reject any entries for `Y` that do not meet this precision threshold or that fall outside the valid range of -180.00 to +180.00 degrees. Furthermore, the longitude value (`Y`) linked to an artifact (`X`) must be consistent with the centroid of the postal address provided in Section 3. Significant deviations between the stated address and the recorded longitude will trigger an automated data quality alert for review by the GIS specialist.
