# CHAPTER 7 — FORMAL ATTESTATION AND CONTROL MAPPING IN MULTI-REGULATORY ENVIRONMENTS

**DOCUMENT CONTROL**
**Reference:** TG-2024-07
**Issue Date:** October 24, 2024
**Authority:** Governance & Compliance Directorate
**Classification:** Internal Use Only

This chapter delineates the formal mechanisms by which organizational processes are mapped to regulatory directives, specifically within the intersecting domains of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the System and Organization Controls (SOC 2) framework. The text establishes a rigorous ontology for linking procedural attestations to specific legal articles and trust criteria, ensuring that all governance activities are traceable to a responsible human agent and supported by verifiable evidence artifacts. Readers are advised to consult the accompanying relational schemas to understand the deterministic links between process classes, information content entities, and audit protocols.

---

## 7.1 Attestation Processes and Personnel Accountability

In the context of formal governance, an attestation is not merely a signature but a distinct process subclass (`bfo:0000015`) that serves as the primary node of accountability. The axiom `attestation_signed_by` posits that for any attestation process to be valid, it must be executed by a recognized `cco:Person`. This relationship ensures that abstract compliance activities are anchored to specific legal or natural persons, thereby satisfying the "human-in-the-loop" requirements prevalent in modern audit trails.

The structural integrity of the governance framework relies on the unambiguous identification of the signatory. When an attestation is instantiated, the system records the specific class of the attestation process (e.g., `OfficerAttestation`, `AdministratorReview`) and links it to the unique identifier of the person executing the action. This linkage prevents the delegation of authority to non-accountable system agents and ensures that liability can be assessed in the event of a control failure.

The registration of these attestations is the prerequisite step for any subsequent compliance mapping. ## 7.2 GDPR Directive Subclassifications

The General Data Protection Regulation imposes specific obligations that must be operationalized within the enterprise architecture. These obligations are modeled as subclasses of `cco:DirectiveICE` (Information Content Entity). According to the `gdpr_article_subclass` axiom, a directive is defined by its reference to a specific GDPR article (slot `y`). For instance, a class such as `RightToErasureDirective` is semantically defined by its relationship to GDPR Article 17.

Crucially, the ontology enforces a foreign key constraint where the instantiation of a GDPR directive is dependent on a pre-existing attestation. This reflects the operational reality that a legal article is only "active" within the firm's governance framework once it has been acknowledged and signed off by a responsible officer. The `y` slot in the relational schema, while representing the Article class in the ontology, functionally links to the attestation record that validated it.

| id | x | y |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |

This mapping allows the organization to query specific regulatory obligations (e.g., "Show me all active Article 32 implementations") and immediately retrieve the responsible signatory. It transforms static legal text into dynamic, enforceable objects within the compliance database.

## 7.3 SOC2 Trust Criteria Integration

Parallel to the GDPR framework, the SOC 2 Trust Services Criteria provide a baseline for information security. The `soc2_trust_criterion` axiom functions similarly to the GDPR classification, defining a DirectiveICE as a manifestation of a specific SOC2 criterion (e.g., CC6.1 regarding logical access). These criteria are often mapped to the same control sets as GDPR requirements, creating a unified compliance surface.

The relational structure for SOC2 criteria mirrors that of the GDPR directives, requiring a linkage to an attestation record (`t_attestation_signed_by`). This design choice enforces the policy that all trust criteria, regardless of their origin framework, must be formally adopted and signed by a qualified stakeholder. It prevents the "shadow adoption" of controls where a technical team implements a security measure without formal governance review.

**Table 7.3: SOC2 Criteria Adoption Log**
*This table represents the `soc2_trust_criterion` axiom. The `y` column links the specific Trust Criterion Class to the Attestation ID, ensuring the criterion has been reviewed and signed.*

By maintaining separate tables for GDPR and SOC2 directives while linking them to a common attestation registry, the organization supports cross-walking analyses. For example, one can verify that the `DataSecurityDirective` (GDPR) and the `EncryptionCriterion` (SOC2) are both signed by the `SystemAdministrator`, ensuring consistency in control ownership.

## 7.4 Evidence Repositories and Attestation Support

A compliance framework is only as robust as its evidence. The `attestation_with_supporting_evidence` axiom asserts that any attestation process (`bfo:0000015`) must be substantiated by supporting evidence (`y`). This evidence typically consists of logs, screenshots, configuration exports, or audit reports that demonstrate the effective operation of a control.

In this ontology, the evidence is linked to the specific directive instance it supports. The foreign key constraint requires that the evidence (`y`) in `t_attestation_with_supporting_evidence` must reference a valid `t_gdpr_article_subclass` (id). This hierarchy implies that evidence is not merely attached to a person but is specifically validating the implementation of a legal article or trust criterion. It creates a granular chain of custody: *Person signs Attestation -> Attestation activates Directive -> Evidence validates Directive.*

The density and specificity of this data satisfy the rigorous standards of external auditors and regulatory bodies.

## 7.5 Audit Control Mechanisms

The final component of the governance ontology is the audit process itself. The `audit_subclass` axiom defines an audit as a process (`bfo:0000015`) that specifically targets `cco:DirectiveICE` entities. An audit is not a generic review; it is a structured examination of the directives, criteria, and evidence established in the previous sections.

While the relational schema for `t_audit_subclass` is structurally simple—containing only an ID and the Audit Class—its semantic role is pivotal. The audit process consumes the data generated by the other tables: it verifies that the attestations in Table 7.1 are current, that the directives in Tables 7.2 and 7.3 are active, and that the evidence in Table 7.4 is sufficient. The audit acts as the meta-control, validating the integrity of the entire governance web.

**Table 7.5: Audit Schedule and Classification**
*This table represents the `audit_subclass` axiom. It catalogs the various types of audit processes applied to the directive framework.*

| :--- | :--- |

The execution of an audit process typically results in the generation of new evidence entries (feeding back into Table 7.4) and potentially requiring new attestations (feeding back into Table 7.1), thereby closing the loop on the continuous improvement cycle. This formalized, data-driven approach ensures that governance is not a static artifact but a dynamic, verifiable state of organizational operations.
