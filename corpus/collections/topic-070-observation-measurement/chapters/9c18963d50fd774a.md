# Chapter 4: Ontological Structures for Regulatory Compliance and Attestation

This chapter delineates the formal ontological structures required to model the intersection of regulatory mandates, specifically the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the System and Organization Controls (SOC) 2 framework, within a unified governance architecture. It provides a rigorous examination of the axioms governing attestation processes, the classification of directive information content entities, and the evidentiary chains necessary for audit verification. The content is grounded in the Basic Formal Ontology (BFO) and the Common Core Ontologies (CCO), offering a schema for representing compliance artifacts as machine-readable data structures.

## 1. Attestation and Signatory Authority

In the context of governance frameworks, the validity of any compliance mechanism rests upon the verification of authority and the irrevocability of signatory commitments. The ontological construct for this is defined by the axiom that an attestation is a process (`bfo:0000015`) which necessitates an agent acting in the capacity of a signatory (`cco:Person`). This relationship moves beyond the mere storage of a signature; it formally defines the attestation as a temporal process wherein a person binds their identity to a specific assertion of truth or compliance.

The axiom `attestation_signed_by` asserts that the entity {X} is a subclass of a process that is signed by a person. This establishes the primary linkage between a governance artifact and the responsible natural person. In relational terms, this structure isolates the signatory event as a distinct entity, ensuring that the authority can be audited independently of the content being signed.

There are no foreign keys in this table; it serves as the parent entity for other directives that require signatory validation.

### 1.1 Operational Context of Signatory Processes
The implementation of `attestation_signed_by` within an enterprise resource planning (ERP) or Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC) system requires the strict enforcement of identity verification protocols. The process class {X} must be instantiated only after multi-factor authentication (MFA) confirms the identity of the `cco:Person`. Furthermore, the process must be temporally bounded; a signature is a discrete event with a start and end time, distinct from the persistence of the signed document.

### 1.2 Non-Repudiation and Audit Trails
From a forensic standpoint, the `attestation_signed_by` table provides the root of the chain of custody for compliance obligations. If a directive is contested, the audit trail must resolve to a specific row in this table, proving that a specific natural person executed the process. This prevents the diffusion of responsibility often found in complex corporate hierarchies.

## 2. GDPR Article Implementation

The General Data Protection Regulation introduces specific legal obligations that function as directives within information systems. The ontology models these as subclasses of `cco:DirectiveICE` (Information Content Entity), which are specifically characterized by their reference to a particular GDPR Article. The axiom `gdpr_article_subclass` formalizes this by stating that {X} is a DirectiveICE that references GDPR Article {Y}.

Crucially, the relational schema enforces that the GDPR Article {Y} must be an instance of an attestation process (`t_attestation_signed_by`). This reflects the legal reality that Articles of the GDPR (such as Article 28 regarding Processor contracts) are not merely text but are operationalized through specific attestation activities—contracts signed, notices acknowledged, or rights executed.

Column `x` represents the DirectiveICE class (e.g., a Right to Erasure directive), while column `y` is a foreign key referencing `t_attestation_signed_by(id)`, linking the legal article to the specific attestation process that activates it.

| id | x | y |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| GDPR-ART-17-01 | RightToErasureDirective | SIG-2023-002 |
| GDPR-ART-28-01 | ProcessorContractDirective | SIG-2023-001 |
| GDPR-ART-21-01 | ObjectionToProcessingDirective | SIG-2023-005 |
| GDPR-ART-15-01 | RightOfAccessDirective | SIG-2023-002 |
| GDPR-ART-32-01 | DataSecurityDirective | SIG-2023-004 |

### 2.1 Mapping Legal Text to Directives
The class {X} in this schema acts as the bridge between unstructured legal text and structured system behavior. For instance, the `RightToErasureDirective` is not the text of Article 17 itself, but the system-level command derived from it. The foreign key relationship ensures that every instance of a GDPR directive within the system is tethered to a valid, human-signed attestation, satisfying the requirement that data protection measures be "state of the art" and verifiable.

### 2.2 Granularity of Compliance
By treating each article as a potential subclass of `DirectiveICE`, the framework allows for granular compliance reporting. An organization may be fully compliant with `GDPR-ART-28-01` (Processor contracts) while deficient in `GDPR-ART-32-01` (Data security). The relational structure facilitates this differential analysis by isolating the attributes of each directive.

## 3. SOC2 Trust Criteria Integration

Parallel to the GDPR framework, the SOC2 Trust Services Criteria (TSC) provide a baseline for information security controls. The axiom `soc2_trust_criterion` posits that {X} is a `DirectiveICE` which incorporates a specific SOC2 Trust Criterion {Y}. Like the GDPR model, this structure links the abstract criterion to a concrete attestation process.

This mapping is essential for organizations undergoing SOC2 Type II audits. The criteria (e.g., CC6.1 regarding logical access) must be instantiated as directives within the control environment. The schema mandates that these criteria {Y} are realized through attestation processes, ensuring that the criteria are not merely theoretical but are actively signed off upon by responsible parties.

### 3.1 Cross-Framework Alignment
The structure of `t_soc2_trust_criterion` allows for direct alignment with `t_gdpr_article_subclass`. For example, `SOC2-CC6.6-01` (Data Disposal) and `GDPR-ART-17-01` (Right to Erasure) may reference distinct attestation processes but represent functionally similar control objectives. The ontology supports querying across these tables to identify overlapping controls, thereby reducing audit fatigue.

### 3.2 Criteria as Directives
Treating SOC2 criteria as `DirectiveICE` entities emphasizes their prescriptive nature. They are not passive measurements but active commands within the system architecture. The foreign key to the attestation table ensures that the implementation of these criteria is formally acknowledged by a person, satisfying the "assertion" component of the SOC2 attestation report.

## 4. Evidence and Supporting Documentation

A directive, whether derived from GDPR or SOC2, remains a theoretical construct without substantiating evidence. The axiom `attestation_with_supporting_evidence` addresses this by defining a relationship where an attestation process {X} is substantiated by supporting evidence {Y}. In this schema, the evidence {Y} is explicitly linked to a GDPR Article subclass, creating a chain of validity from the legal basis to the proof of execution.

This structure enforces the principle of "evidence-based compliance." It is insufficient to assert that a process occurred; the system must point to a specific artifact (a log file, a screenshot, a timestamped record) that proves the process was executed in accordance with the specific directive.

This table embodies the `attestation_with_supporting_evidence` axiom. The primary key is `id`, representing the evidence record. Column `x` represents the attestation process class, and column `y` is a foreign key referencing `t_gdpr_article_subclass(id)`, linking the evidence to the specific GDPR directive it supports.

| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| EVID-LOG-001 | ErasureRequestLog | GDPR-ART-17-01 |

| EVID-SEC-001 | AccessControl_AuditTrail | GDPR-ART-32-01 |

### 4.1 The Evidence Chain
The relational dependency here is critical. Evidence {Y} cannot exist in a vacuum; it must validate a specific directive instance (`t_gdpr_article_subclass`). This prevents the "general evidence" pitfall, where organizations provide generic security logs to answer specific legal questions. The schema enforces specificity: `ErasureRequestLog` is valid evidence only for `GDPR-ART-17-01` (Right to Erasure), not for `GDPR-ART-28-01` (Processor Contracts).

### 4.2 Integrity and Retention
As `bfo:0000015` processes, the generation of evidence must be tamper-evident. The ontology implies that while the attestation is the legal sign-off, the supporting evidence is the factual grounding. Systems governed by this schema must implement Write-Once-Read-Many (WORM) storage strategies for the entities referenced in column `x` to ensure the integrity of the audit trail.

## 5. Audit Subclass and Control Verification

The final component of this governance framework is the audit mechanism itself. The axiom `audit_subclass` defines {X} as a process that audits a `DirectiveICE`. This recursive capability—where a process (audit) examines other directives—creates the meta-layer of governance required for continuous improvement and regulatory adherence.

This axiom distinguishes an "audit" from a standard "attestation." While an attestation asserts that a directive is followed, an audit is the systematic investigation of that assertion. The schema treats the audit as a distinct process class, capable of targeting any `DirectiveICE` within the system, whether it originates from GDPR or SOC2 sources.

### 5.1 Audit as a Process
Classifying audits as `bfo:0000015` (Process) underscores that they are activities with duration, resource consumption, and defined workflows. An audit is not a static report but the investigation that generates the report. By linking these processes to `DirectiveICE` targets (conceptually, though the schema here lists the audit classes themselves), the organization can schedule and track audit coverage across its entire regulatory landscape.

### 5.2 Governance Feedback Loops
The `audit_subclass` provides the closure mechanism for the governance loop. Audits generate findings which lead to new directives, which require new attestations, which generate new evidence. The ontology supports this cyclical evolution of the compliance posture. The rows in `t_audit_subclass` represent the critical checkpoints where the organization verifies the integrity of the entire data structure described in this chapter.
