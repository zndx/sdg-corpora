# Chapter 4: Governance Structures and Evidence Correlation

This chapter delineates the formal relationships between governance directives, verification processes, and the evidentiary artifacts required to establish compliance within a managed environment. It establishes the ontological boundaries for attestation, verification, control implementation, and audit execution, providing a structured framework for mapping procedural classes to their respective outputs and risk mitigation targets. The content herein serves as a normative reference for system architects and compliance officers seeking to instantiate a directive governance framework, ensuring that all assertions are grounded in verifiable data structures and logically consistent axioms.

## 1.0 Attestation with Supporting Evidence

Attestation represents the formal assertion of truth regarding a specific state of compliance or operational functionality. Within the context of the governance framework, an attestation is not merely a declarative statement; it is a process that is ontologically dependent on the existence of corroborating data. The relationship between the attestation process and its supporting evidence is defined by the axiom that an attestation is a process that *with supporting evidence* includes some evidence class. This dependency ensures that every attestation is anchored to a tangible artifact, thereby preventing hollow assertions of compliance.

In practical terms, this means that an entity cannot formally attest to a control's effectiveness without referencing specific data points generated by that control. The evidence serves as the object to which the attestation subject refers. This structure enforces traceability, allowing auditors to move backward from the attestation statement to the underlying evidence, and forward from the evidence to the specific process that generated it.

### 1.1 Evidence Dependency and Integrity

The integrity of the attestation is directly proportional to the validity of the supporting evidence. The framework classifies both the attestation and the evidence as distinct classes, linked by a mandatory object property. This relationship implies that if the evidence class is invalidated or retracted, the attestation class enters a state of indeterminacy until new supporting evidence is provided.

## 2.0 Verification Produces Evidence

While attestation consumes evidence, the verification process is responsible for its genesis. Verification is defined as a process that *produces evidence* of some class. This distinction is critical for governance modeling: verification is the active mechanism—such as a scan, a review, or an automated check—that generates the artifacts subsequently used in attestations. The axiom posits a directional flow from the verification activity to the evidence output.

This relationship establishes the provenance of governance data. By linking the evidence class to a specific verification process, the framework creates an immutable chain of custody. It answers the question of origin for every artifact in the system. Without this linkage, evidence exists as orphaned data, lacking the context necessary to validate its authenticity or relevance to the control environment.

### 2.1 Process-to-Artifact Mapping

The verification process must be rigorously defined to ensure that the evidence it produces is of the correct type and granularity. For instance, a verification process designed to check network vulnerability must produce evidence classified as a vulnerability report, not a generic log file. The ontology enforces this coherence by restricting the range of the `producesEvidence` property to specific evidence classes compatible with the verification domain.

## 3.0 Control Only Addresses Threat Class

Controls are the directives or implemented mechanisms within a system designed to manage risk. The ontology defines a specific constraint regarding the scope of a control: a control is a directive that *addresses risk only* from a specific threat class. This axiom utilizes a universal quantification ("only") to restrict the domain of the control's applicability. It asserts that the control is designed and sanctioned to mitigate a specific category of threats and, by definition within this strict context, no others.

This constraint is vital for the classification and deployment of security controls. It prevents scope creep and ensures that a control is not evaluated against threats for which it was not designed. For example, a firewall control designed to address network intrusion threats should not be cited as the primary mitigation for physical theft threats. This axiom enforces a strict one-to-many mapping where a single control type is exclusively associated with a specific threat class.

### 3.1 Scope Restriction and Threat Alignment

The "only" restriction in the axiom serves as a validation rule during the control definition phase. When a control is instantiated, the governance framework must verify that the threat class it addresses falls within the sanctioned set for that control type. This alignment is crucial for accurate risk scoring and gap analysis. If a control claims to address a threat outside its defined class, the relationship is ontologically invalid.

The subsequent table encodes this restrictive relationship. It demonstrates that specific control directives are bound exclusively to particular threat classes. The schema ensures that the `y` column (Threat Class) acts as a strict filter for the applicability of the `x` column (Control).

## 4.0 Control Addresses Risk

While the previous axiom defines the *class* of threats a control is designed to address, this axiom addresses the *instance* of risk. A control is a directive that *addresses risk* of some specific risk instance. This is the operational realization of the control framework. Where the "threat class" axiom defines the design intent, the "addresses risk" axiom defines the operational deployment. It maps the control to the specific, identified risks within the risk register.

This relationship is the core of the risk mitigation strategy. It asserts that for a specific risk entry (e.g., "SQL Injection on Customer Database"), there exists a directive (e.g., "Input Validation Control") that serves as the countermeasure. Unlike the "only" constraint of the previous section, this is an existential assertion ("some"), indicating that a control may address one or more specific risk instances, provided those instances fall within the control's authorized threat class.

### 4.1 Mitigation Strategy and Risk Register

The linkage between controls and risk instances is dynamic. As new risks are identified, controls must be mapped to them. This axiom provides the structure for that mapping. It is the primary data source for generating risk heat maps and compliance gap reports. By querying this relationship, governance officers can identify unaddressed risks (risks with no incoming control links) or over-controlled environments (single risks with excessive redundant controls).

## 5.0 Audit with Findings

The audit process is the evaluative mechanism that assesses the effectiveness of controls and the accuracy of attestations. The ontology defines an audit as a process that *has findings* of some class. Findings are the discrete observations, deficiencies, or confirmations discovered during the audit execution. This relationship aggregates the granular details of an audit engagement into a coherent process record.

This axiom distinguishes the audit itself (the process, schedule, and scope) from the results (the findings). It allows for the temporal separation of the audit event and the reporting of its results. A finding may exist as a draft object before being formally linked to the audit process, but it only gains formal standing as an audit artifact when linked via this relationship.

### 5.1 Audit Lifecycle and Finding Aggregation

The lifecycle of an audit begins with planning and ends with the final report, but the value of the audit lies in the findings. This axiom supports the aggregation of multiple findings under a single audit identifier. It facilitates longitudinal tracking, allowing an organization to compare findings across different audit cycles (e.g., Q1 Audit vs. Q2 Audit) to measure the efficacy of remediation efforts.
