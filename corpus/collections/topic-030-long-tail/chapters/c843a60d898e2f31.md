# Chapter 7: Evidence Tracing and Regulatory Compliance in Data Governance Systems

## 7.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the formal mechanisms by which organizations establish verifiable links between system artifacts, procedural directives, and regulatory claims. Within the framework of modern data governance, the integrity of compliance reporting relies heavily on the ability to trace specific data processing activities back to authoritative sources and human signatories. The following sections introduce a structured ontology for mapping these relationships, focusing on the provenance of descriptive claims, the authority of attestations, and the technical validation of data lineage. By anchoring these concepts in rigorous relational schemas, auditors and compliance officers may ensure that governance frameworks are not merely theoretical but are supported by granular, machine-readable evidence.

## 7.2 Trace Support for Descriptive Claims

In the context of audit readiness, a "trace" is defined as a processual entity that substantiates a specific descriptive claim. According to the foundational axiom, a trace is a dependent continuant that functions as evidence for a Descriptive Information Content Entity (DescriptiveICE). This relationship is critical when an organization must demonstrate that a specific assertion—such as "all user access logs are immutable"—is grounded in reality. The trace itself must be a distinct, identifiable class of entity that captures the state or behavior of the system at a point in time.

The utility of a trace lies in its ability to transform a subjective assertion into an objective, auditable fact. For instance, a compliance officer might assert that data retention policies are enforced automatically. The supporting trace for this claim would be the system process or log entry that demonstrates the execution of the deletion script. Without this trace, the claim remains an unsubstantiated opinion. The ontology formalizes this by requiring that any valid trace must be a subclass of a process that specifically supports a descriptive claim.

## 7.3 Attestation and Signatory Authority

While system traces provide raw evidence, they lack legal weight without human authorization. An attestation is a formal process wherein a specific agent validates the accuracy or compliance of a procedure. The governing axiom here posits that an attestation is a process signed by a Person. This requirement ensures that there is always a responsible party accountable for the validity of a governance action. In regulatory frameworks such as SOC 2 or ISO 27001, the signature serves as the bridge between technical evidence and legal liability.

The signatory, represented as an instance of the `cco:Person` class, must possess the authority to bind the organization to the statement made in the attestation. This could be a Chief Information Security Officer (CISO) certifying that vulnerability scans have been completed, or a Data Protection Officer (DPO) confirming that data subject requests have been processed. The relational model captures this by linking the attestation process directly to the identity of the signer. This prevents the "orphaned process" problem, where automated tasks execute without clear ownership or oversight.

## 7.4 GDPR Lawful Basis and Directive Governance

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) mandates that any processing of personal data must be grounded in a specific lawful basis, such as contract performance, legal obligation, or legitimate interests. In this ontology, a Directive Information Content Entity (DirectiveICE)—such as a data processing policy—must be subclassed to include a specific lawful basis. This creates a formal, queryable link between high-level governance documents and the legal statutes that authorize them.

Crucially, the relational schema enforces a foreign key constraint between the lawful basis and the attestation table. This design choice reflects the operational reality that a lawful basis is not merely a static label but a validated decision. The selection of a lawful basis must be attested to by a responsible person (e.g., a DPO). Therefore, the lawful basis entry (`y`) in the directive table references the unique identifier of an attestation record (`id` in `t_attestation_signed_by`). This dependency ensures that no lawful basis is considered active within the system until it has been formally signed off, thereby closing the loop between legal requirement and human accountability.

## 7.5 Kernel Anomaly Detection as Evidence

As systems increase in complexity, automated security mechanisms must detect and report irregularities that indicate potential compromise or failure. A kernel anomaly is a specific class of Artifact that supports a claim regarding system integrity. Unlike high-level application logs, kernel artifacts operate at the lowest level of the operating system, recording interactions with hardware and memory management. These artifacts are indispensable for supporting claims about system stability or the absence of rootkits.

The axiom defines these anomalies as artifacts that support a DescriptiveICE. For example, a claim that "the system has not experienced unauthorized memory access" would be supported by kernel artifacts such as a clean `dmesg` output or the absence of entries in the kernel module loading log. In a forensic context, the presence of a kernel panic log or a syscall trace file serves as the definitive evidence to support a claim of system failure or intrusion. The ontology treats these artifacts as first-class citizens, ensuring they are cataloged with the same rigor as policy documents.

## 7.6 Column Lineage for Compliance Claims

In data warehousing and reporting, "column lineage" refers to the documented path of a data field from its source, through various transformations, to its final destination in a report. This lineage is an Artifact that supports a compliance claim, specifically regarding the accuracy and provenance of reported figures. For regulated industries, it is insufficient to simply present a final value; one must be able to trace that value back to the source transaction to prove it has not been altered improperly.

The ontology treats lineage metadata as a supporting artifact. For instance, if a compliance report claims that "all reported revenue is derived from verified sales transactions," the column lineage artifact serves as the proof. This artifact would map the `Revenue` column in the report back to the `Amount` column in the `Sales_Transactions` table, documenting any aggregation or currency conversion logic applied along the way. By formalizing lineage as a support mechanism for claims, the governance framework ensures that data transformation logic is transparent, auditable, and resistant to manipulation.
