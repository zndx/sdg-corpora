# Chapter 4: Ontological Mapping of Regulatory Directives and Evidence Chains

This chapter delineates the structural relationships between regulatory directives, specifically within the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), as formalized through a specific ontology of compliance artifacts. We examine the mapping of these requirements to attestation processes and safeguards, establishing a rigorous schema for audit evidence. The discussion proceeds from the definition of high-level directives to the granular implementation of technical, administrative, and physical safeguards, all anchored by a deterministic relational model that ensures referential integrity across compliance domains.

## 4.1 PCI DSS Requirements as Directives

The foundational unit of compliance within the Payment Card Industry ecosystem is the PCI DSS Requirement. In the defined ontology, this entity is not merely a textual statement but a formal class, `X`, which acts as a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` (Information and Communication Element). The axiom asserts that `{X}` is something that `pci dss requirement` `{Y}`, establishing a semantic link between the directive itself and the specific obligation it imposes. This relationship is critical for automated compliance reasoning, as it transforms static regulatory text into a machine-readable node within a governance graph.

The instantiation of this axiom requires that every PCI DSS requirement be uniquely identified and associated with a specific attestation record. This association ensures that no requirement exists in a vacuum; each directive must point to a process or artifact that validates its implementation. The relational projection of this axiom enforces a foreign key constraint, mandating that the target of the requirement (`y`) must exist as a valid attestation entity. This structure prevents orphaned requirements and ensures that the compliance framework is evidence-based.

## 4.2 Attestation with Supporting Evidence

To validate the adherence to directives, the ontology employs the concept of Attestation with Supporting Evidence. Formally, this is defined by the axiom where `{X}` is something that `with supporting evidence` `{Y}`. Here, `{X}` represents a process, specifically a subclass of `bfo:0000015` (Process), while `{Y}` represents the evidence or requirement being supported. This distinction is vital: the attestation is the *act* of verification (a temporal process), whereas the requirement is the *object* of verification.

In the context of audit trails and governance frameworks, the attestation process serves as the bridge between policy and execution. It captures the metadata of *who* verified the control, *when*, and *how*. The relational schema for this axiom introduces a circular dependency with the PCI DSS requirements table; the attestation process (`x`) references the requirement (`y`) as its supporting evidence, while the requirement table references the attestation process as its validation mechanism. This bidirectional linkage enforces a strict closure property: a requirement cannot be considered attested unless the attestation process explicitly cites that requirement as its evidence base.

## 4.3 HIPAA Administrative Safeguards

While PCI DSS focuses on payment card data, healthcare governance is driven by HIPAA, specifically through the implementation of safeguards. The first category of these is the Administrative Safeguard. The ontology defines this via the axiom: `{X}` is something that `hipaa safeguard` `{Y}`. In this context, `{X}` is a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE`, representing the administrative policy or procedure, while `{Y}` represents the attestation process that confirms the policy's enforcement.

Administrative safeguards relate to the actions, policies, and procedures to manage the selection, development, implementation, and maintenance of security measures to protect electronic health information (ePHI). These are often high-level governance controls, such as security training or contingency planning. In the relational model, the administrative safeguard (`x`) must be linked to an attestation process (`y`). This linkage ensures that administrative policies are not merely documented but are actively verified through a distinct process, satisfying the "addressable" and "required" implementation specifications of the HIPAA Security Rule.

## 4.4 HIPAA Technical Safeguards

Technical Safeguards constitute the automated mechanisms and processes used to control access to ePHI. The axiom `{X}` is something that `hipaa safeguard` `{Y}` applies here as well, where `{X}` is the technical directive (e.g., an encryption standard or access control mechanism) and `{Y}` is the attestation process. Unlike administrative safeguards, which involve human management, technical safeguards involve the technology and the policies governing its use.

The ontology treats technical safeguards as directives (`cco:DirectiveICE`) that must be instantiated and verified. The relational schema requires that the technical safeguard definition points to an attestation process. This is crucial for technical compliance, as it separates the configuration of a system (the directive) from the verification of that configuration (the attestation). For example, a directive requiring "Unique User Identification" is distinct from the "Access Control Audit" that verifies users are unique.

## 4.5 HIPAA Physical Safeguards

The final component of the HIPAA Security Rule addressed in this ontology is the Physical Safeguard. These safeguards are physical measures, policies, and procedures to protect electronic information systems and related buildings and equipment from natural and environmental hazards, and unauthorized intrusion. The axiom remains `{X}` is something that `hipaa safeguard` `{Y}`, where `{X}` is the physical directive (e.g., a facility access policy) and `{Y}` is the attestation process.

Physical safeguards often involve tangible assets: door locks, security badges, and workstation placement. In the ontology, these are treated with the same rigor as digital controls. The relational projection ensures that a physical policy, such as "Workstation Use," is not simply a posted notice but a directive that is actively attested via a process, such as "Facility Inspections." This formalization allows auditors to query physical security compliance with the same precision used for digital compliance.

The schema ensures that physical controls are audited and that the evidence is recorded within the same framework as administrative and technical controls.
