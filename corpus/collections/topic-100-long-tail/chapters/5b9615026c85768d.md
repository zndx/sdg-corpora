# Chapter 7: Regulatory Compliance Mapping and Evidence Verification

This chapter delineates the structural dependencies between regulatory directives, specifically the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), within a unified governance framework. It establishes the protocol for mapping specific requirements to evidentiary attestations, ensuring that administrative, technical, and physical safeguards are rigorously documented and verified. The following sections provide a detailed analysis of the ontology axioms governing these relationships, supported by relational data tables that instantiate the logical structure of compliance controls.

## 1. PCI DSS Requirements and Directives

The foundational element of the compliance framework is the PCI DSS Requirement, which functions as a specific type of directive within the Information and Communication Entity (ICE) hierarchy. In formal ontology terms, a PCI DSS Requirement is defined as a class that is a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` and holds a specific relationship (`sdg:pciDssRequirement`) to a target class. This relationship asserts that the subject entity (X) is something that imposes a PCI DSS requirement upon object (Y). In practical governance terms, this translates to a mandatory control or procedural step that an organization must implement to secure cardholder data.

The directive nature of these requirements necessitates a clear mapping to verification processes. A requirement is not merely a static statement of policy; it is an active directive that demands validation. The structure of this relationship ensures that every requirement is traceable to a specific attestation process, thereby closing the loop between policy definition and operational verification. This traceability is critical for audit readiness, as it allows compliance officers to quickly retrieve the evidence supporting any given directive.

## 2. Attestation with Supporting Evidence

While directives define what must be done, the attestation process defines how compliance is verified. An Attestation with Supporting Evidence is modeled as a process (`bfo:0000015`) that utilizes evidence to validate the implementation of a directive. The axiom defining this concept states that the attestation (X) is something that possesses supporting evidence (Y). In the context of this framework, the "supporting evidence" is the specific PCI DSS requirement that is being validated, creating a bidirectional link between the requirement and the verification process.

This relationship is critical for maintaining the integrity of the compliance audit trail. By defining the attestation as a process that is intrinsically linked to the requirement it verifies, the ontology ensures that no attestation exists in a vacuum. Every verification action must be anchored to a specific directive. This structure prevents the common audit failure of "orphaned evidence," where documentation exists but cannot be mapped to a specific control or requirement. Furthermore, it enforces the discipline that evidence must be contemporaneous and relevant to the specific directive being assessed.

## 3. HIPAA Administrative Safeguards

The HIPAA Security Rule mandates the implementation of Administrative Safeguards to manage the selection, development, implementation, and maintenance of security measures. These safeguards are formalized as directives (`cco:DirectiveICE`) that implement a HIPAA safeguard (`sdg:hipaaSafeguard`) regarding a specific administrative function (Y). The verbalization of this axiom—X is something that HIPAA safeguard Y—highlights the role of these directives as active enforcement mechanisms for administrative policies.

Administrative safeguards typically involve policies and procedures designed to clearly show how the entity will comply with the act. In the context of the relational mapping, these safeguards are linked to attestation processes. This linkage implies that the existence of a policy (the safeguard) is insufficient on its own; it must be validated through an attestation process. For example, a Security Management Process policy must be attested to via a risk analysis or risk management review. This mapping ensures that administrative controls are not merely theoretical but are subject to periodic review and validation.

## 4. HIPAA Technical Safeguards

Technical Safeguards refer to the technology and the policy and procedures for its use that protect electronic health information and control access to it. Under the governing axiom, a Technical Safeguard is a directive that enforces a specific technical control (Y). Unlike administrative safeguards, which focus on management and operational protocols, technical safeguards involve the automated mechanisms and hardware/software configurations used to protect data.

The integration of technical safeguards into the attestation framework is essential for verifying the efficacy of cybersecurity controls. For instance, a directive implementing Access Control must be attested by logs showing successful authentication mechanisms or audit trails of access denials. By mapping technical safeguards to attestations, the framework moves beyond a checklist of software features and instead focuses on the operational validation of those features. This ensures that encryption is active, that unique user identifiers are enforced, and that automatic log-off mechanisms are functioning as intended.

## 5. HIPAA Physical Safeguards

Physical Safeguards are measures to protect physical electronic information systems and related buildings and equipment from natural and environmental hazards, as well as unauthorized intrusion. The axiom defining these safeguards classifies them as directives that implement a physical control (Y). These controls are often the most visible aspect of compliance, encompassing facility access controls, workstation usage, and device and media controls.

Mapping physical safeguards to attestations provides a necessary layer of verification for environmental security. It is insufficient to simply state that a facility is locked; the attestation must provide evidence, such as visitor logs, maintenance records for security systems, or inventory reports for media disposal. This relationship ensures that physical security is treated with the same rigor as digital security. It mandates that the physical protection of electronic health information (ePHI) is regularly monitored, tested, and verified to be effective against unauthorized physical access.
