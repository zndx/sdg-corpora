# Chapter 4: Relational Mapping of Compliance Directives and Evidence Attestation

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the structural mapping of regulatory compliance concepts within a unified governance framework. It specifically addresses the intersection of Payment Card Industry Data Security Standards (PCI DSS) and Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) requirements as they pertain to information security directives. The discourse herein establishes a formal ontology for representing these requirements as classes of directives, substantiated by specific attestation processes. We examine the relational schema that binds these abstract regulatory mandates to tangible evidence, ensuring referential integrity across domains. The following sections present a detailed analysis of five core axioms, their verbalization in natural language, and their projection into relational database tables designed for high-fidelity audit trails.

## 4.2 PCI DSS Requirements and Directive ICE

The foundational unit of regulatory adherence within this framework is the PCI DSS Requirement. Formally defined as a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` (Information Control Element), this concept represents a specific mandate imposed by the PCI Security Standards Council. The axiom posits that a PCI DSS Requirement (`X`) is an entity that enforces a specific PCI DSS Requirement (`Y`). This recursive structure allows for the decomposition of high-level mandates into granular control objectives.

In practice, this means that every regulatory directive must be mapped to a specific, identifiable requirement within the standard. The relational projection of this axiom creates a dependency where the requirement itself must be validated by an external attestation process. This structure enforces the principle that no directive exists in isolation; it must be intrinsically linked to a verification mechanism.

## 4.3 Attestation with Supporting Evidence

To validate the directives outlined in the previous section, the framework employs a robust attestation model. The concept of `Attestation with Supporting Evidence` is defined as a subclass of `bfo:0000015` (Process). This axiom asserts that an attestation (`X`) is a process that possesses supporting evidence (`Y`). Within the context of this ontology, the "supporting evidence" is structurally defined as the specific PCI DSS requirement that the attestation process verifies.

This creates a closed-loop verification system. The attestation is not merely a document but a process (`bfo:Process`) that generates or references evidence proving compliance with a specific requirement. The relational schema for this axiom is critical for auditability, as it traces the flow of validation from the process back to the mandate.

This circular reference (Requirement points to Attestation; Attestation points to Requirement) enforces a strict 1:1 or 1:N mapping where every piece of evidence is explicitly tied to the regulatory text it satisfies.

## 4.4 HIPAA Administrative Safeguards

While PCI DSS focuses on payment card data, the governance framework must simultaneously accommodate healthcare regulations under HIPAA. The first category of these controls is the Administrative Safeguard. Defined as a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE`, an Administrative Safeguard (`X`) is an entity that implements a specific HIPAA safeguard (`Y`). These safeguards pertain to administrative actions, policies, and procedures to manage the selection, development, implementation, and maintenance of security measures.

The integration of HIPAA safeguards into the same relational model as PCI DSS requirements allows for a unified compliance dashboard. The structure mirrors the PCI model but applies to the specific administrative controls required by HIPAA, such as security management processes or assigned security responsibility.

## 4.5 HIPAA Technical Safeguards

The second category of HIPAA controls within this framework is the Technical Safeguard. This axiom defines a Technical Safeguard (`X`) as a `cco:DirectiveICE that hipaa safeguard {Y}`. Unlike administrative safeguards, technical safeguards involve the technology and the policy and procedures for its use that protect electronic health information (ePHI) and control access to it.

Examples include access control, audit controls, and integrity controls. The ontology treats these as distinct classes of directives, yet they rely on the same underlying attestation infrastructure for validation. This separation allows auditors to filter controls by domain (Technical vs. Administrative) while maintaining a consistent evidence trail.

## 4.6 HIPAA Physical Safeguards

The final component of the HIPAA triad in this chapter is the Physical Safeguard. This axiom defines a Physical Safeguard (`X`) as a `cco:DirectiveICE that hipaa safeguard {Y}`. These safeguards are physical measures to protect electronic information systems and related buildings and equipment from natural and environmental hazards, as well as unauthorized intrusion.

Physical safeguards often overlap with general corporate security but are codified here as specific regulatory directives. The relational model treats them with the same rigor as technical or administrative controls, requiring a foreign key link to an attestation record. This ensures that physical controls, such as facility access controls or workstation security, are not merely assumed but are actively verified and recorded.
