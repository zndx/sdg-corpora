# Chapter 4: Ontological Mapping of Artifacts and Compliance Directives

This chapter delineates the formal ontological relationships governing enterprise artifacts and their alignment with information security standards. It establishes the taxonomic linkage between tangible assets and their associated brand identities, alongside the hierarchical mapping of internal directives to ISO/IEC 27001 controls and clauses. The objective is to provide a rigorous framework for auditing asset provenance and verifying compliance coverage through structured data representations. Readers will examine the specific axioms that bind artifacts to brands and directives to regulatory clauses, ensuring that governance frameworks are anchored in verifiable, machine-readable evidence.

## 1. Artifact Classification and Brand Attribution

In the context of enterprise asset management, the precise identification of hardware and software components is a prerequisite for effective governance. The ontology defines an `Artifact` as any discrete physical or logical entity within the infrastructure inventory. To ensure traceability and supply chain accountability, every artifact must be associated with a specific brand or manufacturer. This relationship is not merely descriptive but serves as a control point for vendor risk management and warranty validation.

The axiom `schemaorg_product_brand` formalizes this relationship by asserting that a class of artifacts (`X`) necessarily possesses a brand property linking it to a specific brand entity (`Y`). This SubClassOf axiom ensures that any instance classified as `X` inherits the requirement to be associated with `Y`. In a relational database context, this is modeled to enforce referential integrity, preventing the entry of orphaned assets or undefined brands within the configuration management database (CMDB).

The following table instantiates this axiom, illustrating the correlation between specific asset classes and their respective brand entities. The primary key (`id`) serves as the unique identifier for the assertion, while `x` represents the artifact class and `y` represents the brand class.

| id | x | y |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| asrt_001 | SecureEntryGateway | CiscoSystems |
| asrt_002 | PhysicalServerNode | DellTechnologies |
| asrt_003 | MultifunctionPrinter | HPInc |
| asrt_004 | EndpointWorkstation | Lenovo |
| asrt_005 | NetworkStorageArray | NetApp |

**Table 1.1:** Relational projection of the `schemaorg_product_brand` axiom, demonstrating the binding of artifact classes to brand entities.

The data presented in Table 1.1 reflects a typical inventory snapshot where high-value infrastructure components are cataloged. The assertion that a `SecureEntryGateway` is a subclass of an artifact that has the brand `CiscoSystems` allows auditors to automatically verify that installed firmware matches the vendor's security advisories. Without this ontological constraint, the organization risks maintaining a fragmented inventory where asset lineage is obscured, thereby complicating incident response and patch management cycles.

## 2. Alignment of Directives with ISO 27001 Annex A Controls

Governance frameworks rely heavily on the translation of abstract policies into concrete technical controls. The `DirectiveICE` class represents internal mandates, such as "Password Management Policy" or "Access Control Standards." To demonstrate compliance with the ISO/IEC 27001 standard, these internal directives must be explicitly mapped to the controls listed in Annex A. This mapping provides the evidence trail required by external auditors to validate that the organization's Information Security Management System (ISMS) addresses the necessary control objectives.

The axiom `iso27001_annexa_subclass` posits that a directive class (`X`) is a subclass of `DirectiveICE` and is characterized by the property `sdg:iso27001AnnexA`, which links it to a specific Annex A control (`Y`). This structural definition ensures that compliance is not a manual, ad-hoc process but an inherent property of the directive's definition. By embedding the control reference within the ontology, the organization can generate automated compliance matrices that identify gaps between internal policies and the standard's requirements.

Table 2.1 provides a relational view of this axiom, mapping specific internal directive classes to their corresponding ISO 27001 Annex A control references.

| id | x | y |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| dir_101 | AccessControlPolicy | A.9.4.3 |
| dir_102 | CryptographicControlsDirective | A.10.1.1 |
| dir_103 | MalwareProtectionProtocol | A.12.2.1 |
| dir_104 | SecureDisposalProcedure | A.8.3.2 |
| dir_105 | SupplierSecurityPolicy | A.15.1.1 |

**Table 2.1:** Relational projection of the `iso27001_annexa_subclass` axiom, linking internal directives to ISO 27001 Annex A controls.

The mapping illustrated in Table 2.1 is critical for maintaining the ISMS certification. For instance, the `MalwareProtectionProtocol` is axiomatically tied to control A.12.2.1 (Malware protection). During an audit, the system can retrieve all instances of this directive, such as specific endpoint configuration rules, and present them as evidence of implementation. This deterministic relationship eliminates ambiguity regarding which policy satisfies which control, thereby reducing the audit burden and increasing the reliability of the compliance posture.

## 3. Mapping Directives to ISO 27001 Clauses

While Annex A provides the specific controls for information security, the clauses of ISO 27001 (e.g., clauses 4 through 10) establish the overarching requirements for the ISMS, such as leadership, planning, and performance evaluation. Internal directives must often satisfy multiple layers of the standard; they must implement specific controls (Annex A) while also adhering to the broader management system requirements (Clauses).

The axiom `iso27001_clause_subclass` defines that a directive class (`X`) is a subclass of `DirectiveICE` and possesses the property `sdg:iso27001Clause`, linking it to a specific clause (`Y`). This axiom facilitates the vertical integration of the governance framework, ensuring that operational directives are not only technically sound but also aligned with the strategic and procedural mandates of the standard. For example, a risk assessment methodology must align with Clause 6.1.2 (Information security risk assessment) while simultaneously informing various Annex A controls.

Table 3.1 exhibits the relational instantiation of this axiom, categorizing directives by their alignment with the high-level clauses of the standard.

| id | x | y |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| dir_201 | RiskAssessmentFramework | 6.1.2 |
| dir_202 | InformationSecurityTrainingProgram | 7.3 |
| dir_203 | InternalAuditProcedure | 9.2 |
| dir_204 | ManagementReviewDirective | 9.3 |
| dir_205 | ContinuityPlanningProtocol | 8.5 |

**Table 3.1:** Relational projection of the `iso27001_clause_subclass` axiom, associating directives with ISO 27001 clauses.

The data in Table 3.1 demonstrates the scope of the ISMS beyond technical controls. The `InformationSecurityTrainingProgram` is mapped to Clause 7.3 (Awareness, education, and training). This ontological linkage ensures that when the organization undergoes a management review, the system can automatically aggregate all directives relevant to Clause 9.3, providing management with a comprehensive view of the system's performance and adherence to strategic objectives. By formalizing these relationships, the organization ensures that its governance architecture is robust, auditable, and fully aligned with the hierarchical structure of the ISO 27001 standard.
