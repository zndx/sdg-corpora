# Chapter 4: Regulatory Frameworks and Compliance Directives

This chapter delineates the structural and functional requirements of major regulatory frameworks governing information security and privacy. It provides a rigorous examination of the directives mandated by the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS), and the ISO/IEC 27001 standard. The discussion focuses on the formalization of these directives into ontological structures, mapping specific classes of controls to their protected assets or required outcomes. Through this analysis, compliance officers and security architects can derive the necessary evidence artifacts to demonstrate adherence to statutory and contractual obligations.

## 1. HIPAA Physical Safeguards

The HIPAA Security Rule, codified at 45 CFR § 164.310, establishes Physical Safeguards as a critical line of defense for Electronic Protected Health Information (ePHI). These directives are designed to limit physical access to electronic information systems and the facility in which they are housed, while ensuring that authorized access is permitted. The Physical Safeguards are not merely recommendations but implementation specifications that must be addressed to prevent unauthorized intrusion, environmental hazards, and unauthorized disclosure of tangible media.

In the context of the ontology, the axiom `hipaa_safeguard_physical` asserts that a specific Directive class (X) functions as a mechanism to safeguard a specific Physical Asset or Location class (Y). This relationship is foundational for audit trails, as it links the policy document (the Directive) directly to the tangible object under protection.

### 1.1 Facility Access Controls and Media Disposal

Facility Access Controls are mandated to implement policies and procedures to limit physical access to its electronic information systems and the facility or facilities in which they are housed, while ensuring that properly authorized access is allowed. Contingency operations must be documented, ensuring that access to support systems (e.g., emergency power, ventilation) is also secured. Furthermore, the Device and Media Controls directive requires policies and procedures that govern the receipt and removal of hardware and electronic media into and out of a facility, including the disposal of these media.

## 2. HIPAA Technical Safeguards

While physical safeguards address the tangible environment, Technical Safeguards involve the technology and the policy and procedures for its use that protect electronic health information and control access to it. Defined under 45 CFR § 164.312, these directives are automatable mechanisms that control access to systems and data, ensure the integrity of ePHI, and protect against unauthorized access during transmission. The ontology captures this through the `hipaa_safeguard_technical` axiom, mapping a Technical Directive (X) to an Information Asset (Y).

### 2.1 Access Control and Audit Controls

The Access Control standard requires implementing technical policies and procedures that allow only authorized persons to access electronic protected health information. This includes the implementation of unique user identification, emergency access procedures, automatic logoff, and encryption and decryption mechanisms. Concurrently, Audit Controls necessitate hardware, software, and/or procedural mechanisms that record and examine activity in information systems that contain or use electronic health information.

## 3. HIPAA Administrative Safeguards

Administrative Safeguards are the administrative actions, policies, and procedures to manage the selection, development, implementation, and maintenance of security measures to protect electronic health information and to manage the conduct of the covered entity’s workforce in relation to the protection of that information. These are often the most comprehensive and costly components of the HIPAA Security Rule to implement, as they require a formalized management structure. The `hipaa_safeguard_admin` axiom formalizes the relationship where an Administrative Directive (X) safeguards an Organizational Process or Workforce Component (Y).

### 3.1 Security Management Process and Workforce Security

The Security Management Process requires covered entities to implement policies and procedures to prevent, detect, contain, and correct security violations. This includes risk analysis, risk management, sanction policies, and information system activity review. Workforce Security involves implementing policies and procedures to ensure that all members of the workforce have appropriate access to ePHI, contingent on their roles and responsibilities. This includes authorization and/or supervision, workforce clearance procedures, and termination procedures.

## 4. PCI DSS Requirements

The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) provides a robust framework for organizations that handle cardholder data. Unlike HIPAA, which is a statutory regulation, PCI DSS is a contractual standard enforced by payment brands. However, its requirements are technically prescriptive and rigorous. The standard is organized into 12 requirements, grouped into six control objectives. The ontology utilizes the `pci_dss_requirement` axiom to express that a specific PCI Directive (X) necessitates a specific Control Objective or Technical Implementation (Y).

### 4.1 Secure Network and System Configuration

The first two requirements of PCI DSS focus on building and maintaining a secure network. Requirement 1 mandates the installation and maintenance of firewall configurations to protect cardholder data. Requirement 2 requires the vendor-supplied defaults for system passwords and other security parameters to be changed before any systems are installed into the network. These are foundational controls that prevent easy entry points for attackers.

The `x` column represents the PCI Requirement class, and the `y` column represents the specific Control or Configuration class that is required. This mapping is essential for scoping assessments, ensuring that every requirement is mapped to a specific technical control within the Cardholder Data Environment (CDE).

## 5. ISO/IEC 27001 Annex A Controls

ISO/IEC 27001 is the international standard for Information Security Management Systems (ISMS). Annex A of the standard provides a list of information security controls (objectives and controls) that are considered to be best practice. Organizations implementing an ISMS are required to select controls from Annex A (or other sources) to address their specific risks. The `iso27001_annexa_subclass` axiom is used to assert that a specific Organizational Directive (X) is a subclass of or implements a specific ISO 27001 Annex A Control (Y).

### 5.1 Access Control and Operations Security

Annex A is divided into 93 controls (in the 2013 version) or 114 controls (in the 2022 version), organized into 4 themes: Organizational (5), People (6), Physical (7), and Technological (8). A.9 (Access Control) mandates that access to information and other associated assets is authorized and restricted according to business requirements. A.12 (Operations Security) aims to ensure the correct and secure operations of information processing facilities.

The final table demonstrates the `iso27001_annexa_subclass` schema. The `id` provides a unique reference for the control implementation. The `x` column represents the internal Organizational Directive class, while the `y` column represents the canonical ISO 27001 Annex A Control class. This structure allows organizations to map their internal policies directly to the international standard, facilitating certification audits.
