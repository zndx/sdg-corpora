# Chapter 4: Mapping Regulatory Directives to Information Security Controls

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the formal mapping of internal governance directives to external regulatory frameworks, specifically focusing on the intersection of the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS), and the ISO/IEC 27001 information security management standard. The objective is to provide a structured methodology for aligning organizational controls—categorized here as Information Control Elements (ICE)—with statutory and contractual obligations. The following sections define the ontological relationships between specific directive classes and their target safeguard objects, providing evidence-based examples of compliance implementation. This mapping serves as the foundational architecture for audit readiness and automated compliance verification within the enterprise governance framework.

## 4.2 HIPAA Physical Safeguards

The Security Rule under HIPAA mandates specific physical safeguards to protect electronic protected health information (ePHI) from unauthorized intrusion, environmental hazards, and physical tampering. Within the ontology, this relationship is modeled by the axiom `hipaa_safeguard_physical`, which asserts that a specific DirectiveICE class (X) functions as a mechanism to safeguard a physical entity class (Y). These directives are not merely suggestions but constitute enforceable standards under 45 C.F.R. § 164.310(a)(1). Implementation requires a granular assessment of the facility's physical topology, including data centers, emergency operations centers, and remote work sites.

Physical access controls must be validated to ensure that only authorized personnel can access areas where ePHI is stored or processed. This involves the deployment of facility access tokens, biometric scanners, and visitor logs. Furthermore, the directive extends to workstation usage, ensuring that screens are not visible to unauthorized individuals and that workstations are locked when unattended. The following table illustrates the instantiation of these directives, mapping specific policy classes to the physical assets they protect.

**Table 1: Implementation of HIPAA Physical Safeguard Directives**

The following table embodies the `hipaa_safeguard_physical` axiom. The primary key `id` uniquely identifies the directive instance, while columns `x` and `y` represent the specific directive class and the physical asset class being safeguarded, respectively.

| id | x | y |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| PHY-1001 | FacilityAccessControl | DataCenterServerRoom |
| PHY-1002 | WorkstationSecurity | NursingStationTerminal |
| PHY-1003 | DeviceAndMediaControl | PortableStorageDevice |
| PHY-1004 | FacilityAccessControl | EmergencyPowerGenerator |
| PHY-1005 | WorkstationSecurity | AdministrativeOfficePC |

In practice, the directive `FacilityAccessControl` (PHY-1001) enforces strict entry protocols for the `DataCenterServerRoom`, necessitating multi-factor authentication for all entry attempts. Similarly, `WorkstationSecurity` (PHY-1002) mandates the use of privacy filters and automatic session timeouts on `NursingStationTerminal` assets to prevent inadvertent disclosure of patient data.

## 4.3 HIPAA Technical Safeguards

While physical safeguards address the tangible environment, technical safeguards focus on the automated mechanisms protecting ePHI within information systems. Governed by 45 C.F.R. § 164.312, these directives require the implementation of technology and the policy and procedures for its use that protect electronic health information. The ontology captures this via the `hipaa_safeguard_technical` axiom, linking a DirectiveICE (X) to a technical information asset or process (Y).

The core components include access control, audit controls, integrity controls, and transmission security. Entities must implement unique user identification, emergency access procedures, automatic logoff, and encryption and decryption mechanisms. The complexity of these safeguards arises from the need to balance security with clinical workflow efficiency. For instance, encryption mechanisms must be robust enough to prevent data exfiltration yet transparent enough not to impede emergency medical interventions.

This table instantiates the `hipaa_safeguard_technical` axiom. The `id` column serves as the unique identifier for the control instance; `x` denotes the technical directive class, and `y` denotes the specific system or data class subject to the control.

| id | x | y |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| TEC-2001 | AccessControlMechanism | ElectronicMedicalRecord |
| TEC-2002 | AuditControlSystem | SystemActivityLog |
| TEC-2003 | IntegrityControl | DigitalRadiologyImage |
| TEC-2004 | TransmissionSecurity | HL7MessageInterface |
| TEC-2005 | AuthenticationMechanism | RemoteAccessVPN |

For example, row TEC-2003 illustrates the application of `IntegrityControl` to `DigitalRadiologyImage` assets. This directive ensures that digital imaging and communications in medicine (DICOM) data are not altered or destroyed in an unauthorized manner, typically employing cryptographic hashing and digital signatures to verify data integrity upon receipt and storage.

## 4.4 HIPAA Administrative Safeguards

Administrative safeguards are the overarching administrative actions, policies, and procedures to manage the selection, development, implementation, and maintenance of security measures. These safeguards are pivotal because they manage the conduct of the workforce in relation to the protection of ePHI. The `hipaa_safeguard_admin` axiom formalizes the relationship where a DirectiveICE (X) governs an administrative process or workforce entity (Y).

Key requirements include security management processes, assigned security responsibility, workforce security, information access management, security awareness and training, and contingency planning. Compliance in this domain is heavily documentation-dependent. Organizations must maintain records of risk analyses, sanction policies for non-compliance, and business associate agreements. The administrative layer effectively dictates the governance structure for the physical and technical layers described previously.

**Table 3: Administrative Directives and Workforce Processes**

This table operationalizes the `hipaa_safeguard_admin` axiom. The primary key is `id`; `x` represents the administrative policy class, and `y` represents the workforce or procedural entity governed by that policy.

| id | x | y |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| ADM-3001 | SecurityManagementProcess | RiskAssessmentReport |
| ADM-3002 | WorkforceSecurity | SanctionPolicy |
| ADM-3003 | InformationAccessManagement | ClearanceAuthorization |
| ADM-3004 | SecurityAwarenessTraining | NewHireOrientation |
| ADM-3005 | ContingencyPlan | DataBackupRecoveryProcedure |

Row ADM-3002, for instance, links the `WorkforceSecurity` directive to the `SanctionPolicy`. This ensures that there are formal, documented policies regarding the application of appropriate sanctions against workforce members who fail to comply with security policies. This linkage is critical for audit trails demonstrating that the organization enforces compliance rather than merely suggesting it.

## 4.5 PCI DSS Requirements

The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) provides a robust framework for organizations that handle cardholder data (CHD). Unlike HIPAA, which is sector-specific, PCI DSS applies to any entity that stores, processes, or transmits payment card data. The `pci_dss_requirement` axiom maps a DirectiveICE (X) to a specific PCI DSS requirement class (Y), ensuring that internal controls align with the 12 high-level requirements of the standard.

The standard emphasizes a defense-in-depth strategy, requiring network security, data protection, vulnerability management, access control, monitoring, and information security policy. A critical distinction in PCI DSS is the strict segmentation of the Cardholder Data Environment (CDE) from the rest of the corporate network. Directives must explicitly address the isolation of systems that store, process, or transmit CHD to prevent lateral movement by attackers.

**Table 4: PCI DSS Control Alignment**

This table reflects the `pci_dss_requirement` axiom. The `id` column uniquely identifies the control mapping; `x` is the internal directive class, and `y` is the specific PCI DSS requirement category addressed by the directive.

| id | x | y |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| PCI-4001 | NetworkSegmentationDirective | Requirement1:FirewallConfiguration |
| PCI-4002 | DataProtectionPolicy | Requirement3:ProtectStoredCHD |
| PCI-4003 | VulnerabilityManagementProgram | Requirement6:SecureDevelopment |
| PCI-4004 | AccessControlMechanism | Requirement7:RestrictAccess |
| PCI-4005 | LoggingMonitoringDirective | Requirement10:TrackAndMonitor |

In row PCI-4001, the `NetworkSegmentationDirective` is mapped to `Requirement1:FirewallConfiguration`. This directive enforces the internal rule that the CDE must be isolated via internal firewalls, ensuring that traffic flows are strictly controlled and monitored, a fundamental requirement for maintaining a compliant cardholder data environment.

## 4.6 ISO 27001 Annex A Controls

ISO/IEC 27001 is the international standard for Information Security Management Systems (ISMS). Annex A provides a list of information security control objectives and a comprehensive set of controls. The `iso27001_annexa_subclass` axiom establishes a relationship where a DirectiveICE (X) is a subclass of a specific Annex A control objective (Y). This mapping allows organizations to demonstrate that their specific governance directives satisfy the broad control objectives outlined in the international standard.

Annex A covers diverse areas including information security policies, organization of information security, human resource security, asset management, access control, cryptography, physical and environmental security, operations security, communications security, system acquisition, development and maintenance, supplier relationships, information security incident management, information security aspects of business continuity management, and compliance. The flexibility of ISO 27001 allows for the tailoring of these controls to the specific risk profile of the organization.

**Table 5: ISO 27001 Annex A Control Implementation**

This table demonstrates the `iso27001_annexa_subclass` axiom. The `id` column serves as the unique identifier for the control instance; `x` represents the organization's specific directive, and `y` represents the ISO 27001 Annex A control objective to which it conforms.

| id | x | y |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| ISO-5001 | AccessControlPolicy | A.9:AccessControl |
| ISO-5002 | CryptographicKeyManagement | A.10:Cryptography |
| ISO-5003 | SupplierSecurityAgreement | A.15:SupplierRelationships |
| ISO-5004 | InformationClassificationPolicy | A.8:AssetManagement |
| ISO-5005 | IncidentManagementProcedure | A.16:InformationSecurityIncidentMgmt |

Row ISO-5002 illustrates the alignment of the internal `CryptographicKeyManagement` directive with `A.10:Cryptography`. This ensures that the organization's policies regarding the generation, storage, archival, and destruction of cryptographic keys meet the rigorous standards set forth in Annex A, thereby securing data both at rest and in transit against unauthorized disclosure or modification.
