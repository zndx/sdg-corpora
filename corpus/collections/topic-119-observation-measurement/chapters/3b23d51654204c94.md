# Chapter 7: Ontological Mapping of Physical Artifacts and Descriptive Attributes in Regulatory Compliance

## 7.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the protocols for mapping physical artifacts to their descriptive attributes within a unified compliance framework. The primary objective is to establish a rigorous data lineage that links tangible assets—specifically facilities and sites—to their corresponding information content entities, such as geospatial coordinates and postal addresses. Adherence to these mappings ensures that regulatory submissions maintain referential integrity and that audit trails accurately reflect the physical reality of operational footprints. The following sections detail the axiomatic structure of these relationships, providing the necessary ontological grounding for data architects and compliance officers involved in asset verification and reporting.

## 7.2 The Descriptive Information Content Entity

### 7.2.1 Definition and Axiomatic Foundation

In the context of high-fidelity audit reporting, a Descriptive Information Content Entity (ICE) serves as the fundamental unit of data that asserts a fact about the world. Unlike a physical artifact, which occupies space and possesses material properties, a Descriptive ICE is a "realizable entity"—it is a claim, a measurement, or a record that exists independently of the object it describes.

The governing axiom for this concept is derived from the `outlier_claim_basic` template. The Manchester syntax for this axiom is defined as: `Class: {X:Class} SubClassOf: cco:DescriptiveICE`. In natural language, this posits that {X} is a descriptive information content entity. This classification is critical for compliance frameworks because it distinguishes raw data strings (which are merely syntactic) from semantically grounded claims that can be audited, verified, and contested. When an organization logs a coordinate set or a postal address string, it is instantiating a Descriptive ICE. This entity must be treated as a distinct object within the database, subject to its own lifecycle management, versioning, and retention policies.

### 7.2.2 Audit Implications and Data Management

The classification of an attribute as a Descriptive ICE triggers specific governance requirements. Because these entities are "descriptive," they function as the evidentiary basis for all subsequent assertions regarding physical artifacts. If a Descriptive ICE is found to be erroneous—for example, a transposed digit in a latitude coordinate—the error propagates to every physical artifact linked to it. Therefore, the `outlier_claim_basic` structure acts as a control point. Data integrity protocols must validate the format and logical consistency of these claims before they are entered into the registry.

## 7.3 Geospatial Coordinate Attribution

### 7.3.1 Linking Artifacts to Coordinates

Once a Descriptive ICE—specifically a geospatial coordinate—has been established, it must be rigorously linked to the physical artifact it describes. In regulatory frameworks, an "Artifact" is typically a site, a facility, or a piece of infrastructure. The relationship between the artifact and the coordinate is not merely a property of the artifact; it is a distinct association that must be recorded to satisfy geospatial compliance standards (such as those mandated by environmental agencies or zoning boards).

The axiom governing this relationship is `schemaorg_place_geocoord`. The Manchester syntax is: `Class: {X:Class} SubClassOf: cco:Artifact, sdg:hasGeoCoordinates some {Y:Class}`. Verbalized, this means: {X} is something that has geo coordinates {Y}. This structure enforces a separation of concerns: the physical site (X) is distinct from the coordinate data (Y), yet they are bound by a specific, verifiable relationship. This prevents the "hard-coding" of location data into site records, allowing for updates to location data without altering the core identity of the site artifact.

### 7.3.2 Verification and Validation Protocols

When assigning coordinates to an artifact, compliance officers must verify that the coordinate tuple (Y) exists within the `t_outlier_claim_basic` registry. This foreign key constraint is a critical control mechanism; it prohibits the association of a site with "orphan" or unverified coordinate data. Furthermore, the nature of the artifact (X) must be confirmed as a member of the `cco:Artifact` class. This distinction ensures that abstract concepts or legal entities (which are not artifacts) are not erroneously assigned physical GPS coordinates, a common error in legacy data systems that leads to compliance violations during site inspections.

The `id` is the primary key of the relationship record; `x` represents the Artifact (the place); and `y` is a foreign key referencing the `id` of a GeoCoordinate claim in `t_outlier_claim_basic`.

*Table 7-2 embodies Axiom 1 (`schemaorg_place_geocoord`), defining the relationship where an Artifact (X) possesses GeoCoordinates (Y). The `id` is the primary key of the attribution, `x` identifies the physical Artifact, and `y` is a foreign key referencing the Descriptive ICE (the coordinate) established in Table 7-1. Note that `AdministrativeCenter_Delta` shares coordinates with `ManufacturingPlant_Alpha`, illustrating a one-to-many mapping common in co-located facilities.*

## 7.4 Postal Address Attribution

### 7.4.1 Structuring Address Assertions

While geospatial coordinates provide precise planetary location, postal addresses provide the civic and logistical identifier necessary for legal correspondence, service of process, and supply chain operations. Similar to coordinates, a postal address is not an intrinsic physical property of a building but a Descriptive Information Content Entity—a structured string that conforms to postal standards.

The relevant axiom for this concept is `schemaorg_place_address`. The Manchester syntax is: `Class: {X:Class} SubClassOf: cco:Artifact, sdg:hasPostalAddress some {Y:Class}`. Verbalized, this asserts: {X} is something that has postal address {Y}. This axiom mirrors the structure of the geospatial attribution but utilizes a different object property (`sdg:hasPostalAddress`). This distinction is vital for systems that must distinguish between "where a thing is" (coordinates) and "where the mail goes" (address), as these frequently diverge in large campuses or PO Box arrangements.

### 7.4.2 Compliance and Discrepancy Resolution

In audit scenarios, discrepancies between the registered address and the physical coordinates are a major red flag. By treating the address as a separate entity (Y) linked to the artifact (X), auditors can query for artifacts where the distance between `Y` (address geocode) and the linked `Y` (coordinates) exceeds a defined threshold. The `schemaorg_place_address` structure facilitates this "sanity check" by maintaining the address as a first-class citizen within the database, rather than burying it as an unstructured text field in a site description.
