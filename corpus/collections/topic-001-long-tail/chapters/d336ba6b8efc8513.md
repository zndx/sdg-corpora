# Chapter 4: Governance Processes and Verification Protocols

## Scope and Preamble

This chapter establishes the formal ontological definitions for core governance processes within the directive governance framework. It delineates the structural and functional characteristics of Verification, Audit, and Attestation processes as prescribed by the underlying ontology. The definitions provided herein are binding for all compliance reporting, system auditing, and regulatory documentation. Specifically, this text examines the classification of these entities as processes, the constraints regarding evidence production, and the necessity of agent involvement in attestation. The following sections provide detailed axioms, relational schemas, and implementation registers necessary for maintaining data integrity and semantic consistency across governance systems.

## 1. Basic Verification Processes

### 1.1 Definition and Ontological Status

Pursuant to the foundational axioms of the directive governance family, the entity class `Verification` is formally defined as a subclass of `bfo:0000015` (Process). This classification asserts that any instance of Verification is not a static object or a quality, but a temporally bounded entity that unfolds over time. In the context of governance, a Verification process represents the systematic examination or inspection of a system, claim, or data point to establish its truth or accuracy.

The ontological commitment here is fundamental: Verification is an activity. It possesses a start point and an end point, and during its interval, it occupies a distinct temporal region. This distinction separates Verification from *Information Entities* (which represent data) or *Material Entities* (which represent physical objects). When a system records a "Verification," it is referencing the occurrence of this specific processual event.

### 1.2 Operational Context

In practical terms, the Basic Verification process serves as the atomic unit of compliance checking. It does not, in its basic form, necessitate the generation of a persistent evidence artifact within the ontology, nor does it strictly require the intervention of a human agent. It may be purely computational or mechanical. However, it is the prerequisite building block upon which more complex governance structures, such as Evidence Production and Attestation, are constructed.

The registry of Basic Verification processes maintained by the Governance Office includes all process instances that have been initiated and concluded within the current fiscal year. These processes are tracked via unique identifiers to ensure temporal traceability.

## 2. Verification with Evidence Production

### 2.1 Axiomatic Constraints and Evidence Generation

While the Basic Verification defines the activity itself, the governance framework imposes a stricter constraint for high-compliance environments. The axiom `verification_min_one_evidence` stipulates that a Verification process must be associated with the production of at least one Evidence artifact. This relationship is modeled through the object property `sdg:producesEvidence`.

This constraint elevates the Verification process from a transient activity to a documented event. The "Evidence" (denoted as {Y}) is typically an Information Entity, such as a log file, a digital certificate, a scan report, or a timestamped hash. The ontology mandates that for a Verification to satisfy this specific subclass definition, the link between the process (X) and the output (Y) must be explicit and persistent. This ensures that any claim of verification is auditable and falsifiable based on the retained artifacts.

### 2.2 Relational Integrity and Schema

The relational schema projected by this axiom enforces a `NOT NULL` constraint on the evidence column (`y`). This database constraint mirrors the ontological axiom of "min 1." It is technically impermissible for a record in this table to exist without a corresponding evidence identifier. This design prevents "orphan" verification processes where the activity is claimed to have occurred but no supporting artifact is referenced.

## 3. Basic Audit Processes

### 3.1 Definition and Scope

The Audit process is defined axiomatically as a subclass of `bfo:0000015` (Process). While distinct from Verification in its scope—Verification typically targets a specific claim or data point, whereas an Audit targets a system, a process flow, or an organization over a broader timeframe—both share the same fundamental ontological parentage.

An Audit is a systematic, independent, and documented process for obtaining evidence and evaluating it objectively to determine the extent to which criteria are met. Within the ontology, the classification of an entity as an Audit process grants it specific properties and capabilities within the governance framework, distinguishing it from routine monitoring activities. It implies a formal scope of work, a defined set of criteria, and a conclusion.

## 4. Basic Attestation Processes

### 4.1 Definition and Functionality

Attestation, within the context of this framework, is formally defined as a Process (`bfo:0000015`). It represents the act of witnessing the signing of a document or the verification of a digital identity, and subsequently issuing a certificate or vouching for the validity of that information. Unlike an Audit, which is investigative, Attestation is confirmatory. It validates that a specific assertion has been made or that a specific event has occurred according to established rules.

The Basic Attestation process focuses on the event of attestation itself—the procedural act of validation. It serves as the mechanism by which trust is transferred from a trusted authority (the attestor) to a subject (the claimant). In its basic form, the ontology focuses on the processual nature of this act, ensuring it is recorded as a distinct temporal event with defined boundaries.

## 5. Attestation with Signatory Requirements

### 5.1 Agent Involvement and Constraints

The most rigorous form of attestation defined in this framework is governed by the axiom `attestation_min_one_signer`. This axiom retains the classification of Attestation as a Process but adds a critical existential constraint: the process must be signed by at least one `cco:Person`. This introduces the concept of agency and accountability into the governance model.

By mandating the involvement of a `cco:Person` (a human agent), the ontology ensures that attestation is not merely an automated system output but a legally and ethically grounded act. The "signer" is the individual who assumes responsibility for the truth of the attestation. This constraint is vital for regulatory frameworks where automated assertions are insufficient without human liability, such as in financial certifications, safety inspections, or legal depositions.

### 5.2 Relational Schema and Accountability

The schema for this concept links the Attestation process directly to the role of the Signer. While the table structure below focuses on the process instance (X), the underlying ontology enforces a join to a Person registry via the `sdg:signedBy` property. This ensures that every row in the registry of such attestations can be traced back to a specific human identity.
