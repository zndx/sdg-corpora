# Chapter 3: Directive Governance and Verification Protocols

This chapter delineates the formal ontological structures governing verification, audit, and attestation processes within the regulatory framework. It establishes the necessary conditions for process validity, evidence generation, and signatory authorization, ensuring that all governance activities are anchored in auditable data structures. The definitions provided herein are binding for all compliance reporting and system documentation.

## 1. Basic Verification Processes

In accordance with the foundational ontology of directive governance, a verification process is formally defined as any activity that falls under the class of processes (`bfo:Process`). This classification is the primary unit of operational analysis, serving as the superclass for all specific verification activities. A basic verification process does not, in itself, imply the production of artifacts or the requirement of human oversight; rather, it establishes the occurrence of a procedural event. For the purposes of regulatory compliance, every distinct verification activity must be cataloged as an instance of this class to ensure traceability.

Consider the routine operational checks performed by the Ashland Water and Sewer Commission. The act of calling a meeting to order or recording the presence of members constitutes a process. These activities are discrete, temporal events that must be logged in the governance record. While they may not immediately produce a physical artifact, they are valid processes under the axiom `verification_basic`.

The primary key `id` serves as the unique identifier for the specific process instance, while the column `x` represents the class or name of the process being instantiated. There are no foreign key constraints in this basic schema, as it defines the existence of the process rather than its relationships.

## 2. Evidence-Producing Verification

While basic verification establishes the occurrence of an event, regulatory compliance mandates that specific verification activities produce tangible evidence. This concept is captured by the axiom `verification_min_one_evidence`, which refines the definition of a verification process. Under this directive, a process is not merely an event but a generative activity that results in the creation of at least one distinct evidence artifact. This evidence must be persistent, retrievable, and sufficient to substantiate the claims made by the verification process.

The relationship between the process and the evidence is causal and mandatory. For instance, in the context of a "Declaration of Conformity" such as that issued by Flyability SA, the process of conformity assessment is meaningless without the resulting declaration document or test report. The process (assessment) produces the evidence (declaration). The ontology requires that for every instance of this process class, there exists a corresponding instance of an evidence class.

The column `x` denotes the process, and `y` denotes the evidence produced. The `NOT NULL` constraint on `y` enforces the axiom's requirement that at least one evidence item must be present.

## 3. Basic Audit Processes

Distinct from routine verification, an audit is a formalized process of systematic examination. Under the axiom `audit_basic`, an audit is defined strictly as a process (`bfo:Process`). However, in the context of governance frameworks, the term "audit" implies a higher level of rigor, scope, and formality than standard verification. It typically involves the review of existing evidence, verification of compliance against standards, and the generation of findings.

The audit process serves as the mechanism by which the integrity of the entire governance system is validated. Whether it is an internal review of billing information or an external financial examination, the activity is classified fundamentally as a process. The ontology treats the audit as a container for various verification steps, but at the highest level of abstraction, it remains an instance of a process class.

The following table embodies Axiom 4 (`audit_basic`), which asserts that {X} (the Audit) is a process. The primary key `id` provides the unique audit identifier, often corresponding to a reference number in official documentation. The column `x` contains the name or type of the audit process. This schema allows for the categorization of audits without presupposing their specific outcomes or associated evidence, which may be handled by subordinate tables.

## 4. Attestation Fundamentals

Attestation represents the formal act of validation or certification. As defined by Axiom 5 (`attestation_basic`), attestation is a process (`bfo:Process`). It is the mechanism by which an entity asserts that a set of conditions has been met. Unlike verification, which may be automated or mechanical, attestation inherently involves the assertion of truth or validity, often bridging the gap between technical evidence and legal or administrative compliance.

In practical terms, attestation is the "sign-off" phase. It is the action taken by a principal contact or authorized representative confirming that the data provided in an application or report is accurate. For example, the submission of a "New Customer Account Application" is incomplete until the attestation process—the act of submitting and confirming the data—is finalized. This process transforms a collection of data into a formal, binding record.

The primary key `id` identifies the specific attestation event, while `x` describes the nature of the attestation. This structure isolates the act of attestation as a distinct process entity, separate from the person performing it or the evidence being attested.

## 5. Attestation with Signatory Requirements

The validity of an attestation is contingent upon agency; an attestation cannot exist in a vacuum. Axiom 3 (`attestation_min_one_signer`) imposes a critical constraint on the attestation process: it must be signed by at least one person (`cco:Person`). This axiom introduces the agent into the process structure, ensuring that every formal validation is traceable to a responsible human actor. This is the cornerstone of accountability in governance frameworks.

This requirement aligns with standard regulatory practices where declarations of conformity or applications must be associated with a specific individual. The "Principal Contact" or "Billing Contact" listed in a form is not merely informational; they are the agent fulfilling the `signedBy` requirement. The ontology enforces that an attestation process instance is invalid if it lacks a link to a person instance.
