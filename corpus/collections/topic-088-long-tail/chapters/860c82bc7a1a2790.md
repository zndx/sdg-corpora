# Chapter 7: Directive Governance and Attestation Mechanisms

This chapter delineates the structural components of the Institutional Compliance Framework (ICF), specifically focusing on the ontological relationships between directives, attestation processes, and evidentiary requirements. The text establishes the formal definitions for governance artifacts, ensuring that all compliance activities are traceable, authorized, and substantiated by empirical data. Readers are advised to consult the accompanying relational schemas to understand the data dependencies between attestation instances, their supporting evidence, and the governing directives.

## 7.1 Attestation with Supporting Evidence

In the context of institutional governance, an attestation is defined not merely as a declaration of fact, but as a processual entity (bfo:0000015) that is intrinsically dependent on the existence of supporting artifacts. The axiom `attestation_with_supporting_evidence` posits that for an attestation {X} to be valid, it must possess a direct object property linking it to a specific evidence class {Y}. This relationship ensures that governance assertions are never abstract; they are always anchored to documentation, logs, or physical records that can be audited.

From a compliance perspective, this binding prevents "orphaned" attestations—claims made without basis. When a governing body, such as the Board of Regents or a Compliance Committee, reviews a status report, they are verifying the integrity of the link between the attestation process and the evidence. If the evidence {Y} is deemed insufficient, the attestation {X} fails its ontological validity test. This structure is critical for audit trails, where the provenance of a claim is just as important as the claim itself.

## 7.2 Directive Specification and Target Application

Requirements within the governance framework are modeled as a specific subclass of directives. The axiom `requirement_equiv_specifies_target` provides a complex equivalence definition: a requirement {X} is equivalent to a `cco:DirectiveICE` that both specifies a particular action or state {Y} and applies to a specific target entity or class {Z}. This tripartite structure is essential for granular governance. It distinguishes a generic policy (a directive without a specific target) from a requirement, which is actionable and enforceable against a specific department, individual, or system.

This axiom facilitates the automated enforcement of governance rules. By querying the ontology for instances of {X} that apply to a specific target {Z}, compliance officers can instantly generate a checklist of active requirements. For instance, if {Z} is the "Bursar's Office," the system retrieves all requirements {X} that specify actions {Y} relevant to that office. This mapping eliminates ambiguity regarding responsibility and scope, ensuring that directives are not merely suggestions but binding obligations with defined subjects and objects.

## 7.3 Attestation Periodicity

Governance processes are temporally bounded; an attestation is only valid within a specific timeframe. The axiom `attestation_at_period` asserts that an attestation process {X} must be associated with an attestation period {Y}. This temporal indexing is crucial for maintaining the currency of compliance status. In a dynamic regulatory environment, a verification performed three years ago is generally insufficient to demonstrate current compliance. Therefore, the period {Y} acts as a validity constraint on the process {X}.

This relationship allows governance frameworks to automate the lifecycle of compliance. By defining the duration of {Y}, the system can trigger alerts when an attestation is nearing expiration. It supports historical analysis by allowing auditors to reconstruct the state of compliance at any specific point in time. For example, during an audit of the 2023 fiscal year, one would query only for attestations linked to the "FY2023" period, filtering out subsequent or prior attestations that are not relevant to the audit scope.

## 7.4 Evidence Requirements within Controls

While attestations consume evidence, controls generate the requirement for it. The axiom `control_with_evidence_requirement` defines a control {X} as a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` that necessitates the production or retention of specific evidence {Y}. This distinction is vital for the design of control environments. A control is not merely a passive rule; it is an active directive that mandates the creation of an artifact. Without the generation of {Y}, the control {X} cannot be said to have been executed.

In practice, this relationship guides the operational deployment of compliance measures. When implementing a new control, such as a "Visitor Log," the governance framework immediately identifies the required evidence: the "Visitor Log Entries." This linkage ensures that resources are allocated for the capture and storage of the necessary data. It also informs audit planning; an auditor reviewing the effectiveness of control {X} knows precisely which evidence artifact {Y} to request for verification.

## 7.5 Attestation Authorization

The validity of an attestation process is ultimately contingent upon the authority of the signatory. The axiom `attestation_signed_by` establishes that an attestation {X} must be signed by a person (`cco:Person`). This relationship injects the concept of agency and accountability into the governance framework. It ensures that attestation is not an automated system event but a conscious ratification by a responsible individual with the standing to verify the claims being made.

This axiom serves as the final node in the chain of custody for compliance information. While a control may generate evidence, and an attestation may link to that evidence, the signature authenticates the human review of that linkage. It allows the organization to attribute specific attestations to specific officers (e.g., the Chief Financial Officer, the Headmistress, the Compliance Officer), creating a clear line of responsibility. In the event of non-compliance, this structure identifies the individuals who explicitly vouched for the integrity of the process.
