# Chapter 7: Formal Ontology of Governance Directives and Attestation Mechanisms

## 7.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the formal ontological structures governing the relationship between internal directives, control mechanisms, and the evidentiary artifacts required to substantiate compliance. In the context of enterprise governance, the integrity of audit trails relies not merely on the collection of data but on the precise logical linkage between regulatory requirements (Directives), the operational controls enacted to satisfy them, and the attestations that verify their execution. The following sections define five core axioms derived from the Basic Formal Ontology (BFO) and the Common Core Ontologies (CCO), providing a schema for mapping compliance activities to verifiable evidence. These definitions are essential for establishing a "single source of truth" within automated governance, risk, and compliance (GRC) systems.

## 7.2 Attestation with Supporting Evidence

### 7.2.1 Conceptual Definition
In formal governance, an attestation is defined as a process (specifically, a `bfo:0000015` process) that serves as a declarative statement of fact regarding the state of a control or system. However, for an attestation to possess probative value, it must be intrinsically linked to supporting evidence. The axiom `attestation_with_supporting_evidence` posits that an attestation (Class X) is a subclass of process that necessarily possesses the property `sdg:withSupportingEvidence`, which relates to some evidentiary artifact (Class Y).

This relationship ensures that every claim made within a governance framework is anchored to a specific, retrievable artifact. Without this linkage, attestations are rendered hollow assertions, susceptible to audit failure. The supporting evidence (Y) acts as the dependent variable in this equation, providing the empirical basis for the attestation (X).

### 7.2.2 Implementation and Data Structure
To implement this axiom within a relational database, one must map the attestation instance to its corresponding evidence. The primary key is the attestation identifier, while the foreign key (conceptually) points to the evidence repository. ## 7.3 Requirement Equivalence and Target Specification

### 7.3.1 Conceptual Definition
Governance frameworks are built upon requirements, which are formally modeled as directives. The axiom `requirement_equiv_specifies_target` establishes a complex equivalence: a Requirement (Class X) is equivalent to the intersection of a `cco:DirectiveICE`, a specification of some parameter (Class Y), and an application to a specific target scope (Class Z).

This tripartite structure is critical for precision. A requirement is not merely a suggestion; it is a directive that *specifies* exactly what must be done (Y) and *applies to* exactly whom or what (Z). This axiom prevents ambiguity in compliance mandates. For instance, a data encryption policy is not a vague concept; it is a directive that specifies "AES-256 encryption" and applies to "Customer PII Databases." The formalization of this relationship allows for automated reasoning engines to determine compliance status by checking if the specified action has been performed on the defined target.

### 7.3.2 Implementation and Data Structure
The relational projection of this axiom requires a table structure that captures the requirement identity alongside its specification and target. This allows auditors to query all requirements applicable to a specific target or to verify the specifications mandated by a particular directive.

| REQ-CTRL-002 | Physical Access Control | Biometric Authentication Protocol | Server Room Entry Points (Zone A) |

## 7.4 Attestation at Period

### 7.4.1 Conceptual Definition
Compliance is not a static state but a temporal process. The axiom `attestation_at_period` formalizes the temporal dimension of governance assertions. It defines an Attestation (Class X) as a subclass of process that occurs `sdg:atAttestationPeriod` some defined timeframe (Class Y).

This axiom is fundamental for recurring compliance cycles, such as quarterly reviews, annual certifications, or monthly risk assessments. It binds the act of attestation to a specific temporal context, ensuring that the assertion is valid only for that duration. In audit scenarios, this allows for the precise retrieval of attestations relevant to a specific reporting period, thereby maintaining the integrity of historical records and facilitating trend analysis over time.

## 7.5 Control with Evidence Requirement

### 7.5.1 Conceptual Definition
While Section 7.2 defined the link between an attestation and its evidence, this axiom defines the link between the *control* itself and the evidence it mandates. The axiom `control_with_evidence_requirement` asserts that a Control (Class X), which is a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE`, has the property `sdg:requiresEvidence` relating to some Evidence Class (Y).

This distinction is vital for operational governance. It dictates that for a control to be considered "implemented" or "effective," it must generate specific evidence. For example, a "Password Rotation Control" is not merely a policy statement; it is a directive that *requires* the evidence of "System Password Change Logs." This axiom shifts the focus from the existence of a policy document to the generation of operational artifacts, thereby enforcing a culture of continuous compliance monitoring.

## 7.6 Attestation Signed By

### 7.6.1 Conceptual Definition
The final axiom addresses the matter of authority and accountability. The axiom `attestation_signed_by` defines an Attestation (Class X) as a process that is `sdg:signedBy` a `cco:Person`. This introduces the agent into the ontological structure. An attestation without a signatory lacks legal and procedural standing; it is an orphaned data point.

By linking the attestation process to a specific person (via their role or identity), the organization establishes a chain of custody and assigns liability. This is the cornerstone of "sign-off" procedures in governance frameworks. It ensures that a human agent has reviewed the underlying evidence and accepts responsibility for the accuracy of the attestation.
