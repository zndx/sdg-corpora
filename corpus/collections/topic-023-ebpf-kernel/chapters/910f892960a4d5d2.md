# Chapter 4: Evidence Lifecycle and Directive Governance in Compliance Frameworks

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the structural relationships governing the generation, consumption, and evaluation of evidence within directive governance frameworks. In regulated environments, the mere existence of a control or a process is insufficient; compliance is predicated on the demonstrable linkage between governance artifacts and the evidentiary data that supports them. The following sections explore four critical axioms that define this linkage: the dependency of attestation on supporting evidence, the generative capacity of verification processes, the temporal evaluation of constraints, and the evidentiary requirements of controls. These concepts are foundational to constructing audit-ready information systems where every directive is anchored to verifiable data artifacts.

## 4.2 Attestation with Supporting Evidence

### 4.2.1 Conceptual Definition

In the ontology of governance, an attestation is not merely a statement of fact but a specific type of process (subsumed under `bfo:0000015`, a processual entity) that validates the state of a system or record against a declared standard. The axiom `attestation_with_supporting_evidence` posits that for any attestation `{X}` to be valid, it must be substantiated by a specific class of evidence `{Y}`. This relationship ensures that attestations are not self-referential or hollow; they must draw their validity from external, persistent data artifacts.

This construct is critical in high-assurance environments such as financial reporting or safety-critical systems. An attestation acts as a logical bridge between a human or automated agent's assertion and the raw data that grounds that assertion in reality. Without the `withSupportingEvidence` object property, the attestation lacks the necessary semantic density to withstand audit scrutiny.

### 4.2.2 Operational Context

Consider the operational workflow of a quarterly financial closure. The Chief Financial Officer (CFO) must sign off on the accuracy of the ledger. In this ontology, the "CFO Sign-Off" is the attestation `{X}`. However, this sign-off is procedurally null unless it references the "General Ledger Audit Trail" `{Y}`. The system must enforce that the link between `{X}` and `{Y}` is explicit and typed. If the audit trail is missing or corrupted, the attestation process cannot complete, thereby enforcing data integrity at the governance layer.

Furthermore, the classification of `{Y}` is significant. The evidence must be of a type defined as acceptable within the governance framework. For instance, a system log file may serve as evidence for a system availability attestation, but it would not suffice for a legal compliance attestation requiring a signed contract. The ontology captures this by typing `{Y}` as a specific class, ensuring that the nature of the evidence matches the nature of the claim.

This strict typing prevents the "rubber stamping" of compliance activities where sign-offs occur without underlying data verification.

## 4.3 Verification Produces Evidence

### 4.3.1 Conceptual Definition

While attestation consumes evidence, verification produces it. The axiom `verification_produces_evidence` describes a processual relationship wherein the execution of a verification activity `{X}` necessarily results in the creation of an evidence artifact `{Y}`. This distinction is vital for audit trails: it shifts the focus from static data to dynamic process execution. Verification is an active process (`bfo:0000015`) that interrogates the state of a system or the correctness of a data set, and the byproduct of this interrogation is a record that proves the verification occurred.

This axiom underpins the concept of "continuous monitoring" in modern governance frameworks. Rather than relying on point-in-time snapshots, systems are designed to execute verification routines (e.g., checksum validation, configuration drift detection) that automatically generate evidence. This evidence `{Y}` serves as the historical record of the verification process `{X}`, allowing auditors to reconstruct the timeline of compliance checks long after the fact.

### 4.3.2 Operational Context

In the context of software supply chain security, verification processes are paramount. When a build server compiles a production artifact, it often executes a code signing verification process. This process `{X}` takes the binary and a public key as inputs, but crucially, it produces a signature verification log `{Y}`. This log is the evidence that the verification was performed and that the integrity of the artifact was confirmed at the time of the build.

The relationship is deterministic. If the verification process `{X}` is executed, the evidence `{Y}` must exist. Conversely, if the evidence `{Y}` is missing, one can infer that the verification process `{X}` either failed to execute or was not recorded. This causal link is what allows automated compliance engines to flag gaps in governance coverage. For example, if a `SystemIntegrityCheck` is scheduled to run daily, and the `HashValidationReport` for a specific day is absent from the repository, the system has objectively violated the `verification_produces_evidence` constraint.

It is the physical (or digital) manifestation of the process. In a regulatory audit, the presence of this file is proof that the process occurred. The ontology ensures that the type of evidence produced is explicitly linked to the process, preventing a scenario where, for example, a manual checklist is submitted as proof of an automated penetration test.

## 4.4 Constraint Evaluated During

### 4.4.1 Conceptual Definition

Constraints are conditional logic statements that restrict the behavior or state of a system. The axiom `constraint_evaluated_during` defines the temporal and procedural scope of these constraints. It asserts that a constraint `{X}` (a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE`) is evaluated specifically during the execution of a process or phase `{Y}`. This axiom introduces the dimension of time and lifecycle into the governance model. Constraints are not universal constants that exist in a vacuum; they are active rules that must be triggered at precise moments in a workflow.

This concept is central to the implementation of "policy-as-code." In this paradigm, governance rules are encoded as software functions that are invoked at specific points in a development or operational pipeline. The axiom formalizes this by linking the constraint (the rule) to the evaluation context (the phase). It answers the question: "When does this rule apply?"

### 4.4.2 Operational Context

Consider a data governance workflow regarding personally identifiable information (PII). A constraint might be defined as "PII data must be encrypted at rest." The axiom `constraint_evaluated_during` requires that we specify *when* this check happens. Is it checked during data ingestion? During storage? Or during data retrieval? The axiom links the `EncryptionConstraint` `{X}` to the `DataIngestionPhase` `{Y}`.

This linkage prevents ambiguity in compliance enforcement. If a constraint is evaluated during the wrong phase, it may be ineffective. For example, checking a password complexity constraint *after* a user has successfully logged in is futile. The constraint must be evaluated *during* the `PasswordCreationWorkflow`. By explicitly modeling the `{Y}` (the evaluation phase), the ontology ensures that governance controls are embedded within the correct operational context, thereby enforcing "preventative" rather than "detective" controls where appropriate.

The following table maps specific constraint classes to the operational phases during which they are evaluated. This demonstrates the temporal binding of directives to the processes they govern.

In row `CON-DAT-02`, the `DataSovereigntyFilter` is the constraint `{X}`. This rule ensures that data does not cross geopolitical borders in violation of local laws. The axiom specifies that this is evaluated during the `DataIngestionPhase` `{Y}`. This means that the logic enforcing the filter must be active at the exact moment data enters the system. If the filter were evaluated during a nightly batch job (a different phase), the system would temporarily be in a non-compliant state during the day. The ontology captures this requirement for synchronous evaluation, ensuring that the constraint is active precisely when it is needed to mitigate risk.

## 4.5 Control with Evidence Requirement

### 4.5.1 Conceptual Definition

The final axiom, `control_with_evidence_requirement`, bridges the gap between the implementation of a control and the proof of its operation. It asserts that a control `{X}` (a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE`) requires evidence `{Y}`. This is distinct from the verification process; while verification *produces* evidence, a control *requires* it to be considered implemented or effective. This axiom is the mechanism by which abstract governance policies are grounded in observable reality.

In audit terminology, this addresses the "assertion of existence." An organization may claim to have a control in place (e.g., "We monitor database access"), but without the required evidence (e.g., "Database access logs"), the control is considered deficient. The axiom formalizes this dependency: the existence of `{X}` is contingent upon the presence of `{Y}`.

### 4.5.2 Operational Context

A common control in information security is "Change Management." The control requires that no changes are made to production systems without authorization. The `ChangeManagementControl` `{X}` is the directive. However, for this control to be satisfied, it requires `ChangeTicketRecord` `{Y}`. The control does not generate the ticket; the user does. But the control *requires* the ticket to exist as a condition of validity.

This relationship is essential for gap analysis. When auditing a system, one can query the ontology for all instances of `{X}` (Controls) and check if the corresponding `{Y}` (Evidence) is present. If a `FirewallConfigurationControl` is defined but no `FirewallConfigurationBackup` (the required evidence) exists, the control is flagged as non-compliant. This axiom facilitates automated compliance checking by defining the "must-have" artifacts for every governance control.

The table below enumerates controls and the specific evidence classes they require. This mapping serves as a checklist for auditors and compliance officers to verify that the necessary documentation and logs are maintained to support the control framework.

The absence of this certificate, or its expiration, directly impacts the validity of the control. By defining this relationship in the ontology, the system can automatically alert administrators if the required evidence for a critical control is missing or invalid, thereby maintaining the integrity of the governance posture.
