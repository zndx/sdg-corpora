# Chapter 7: Evidence Lifecycle and Directive Governance in Compliance Frameworks

This chapter delineates the structural relationships governing the generation, consumption, and evaluation of evidence within a formalized compliance ontology. It establishes the axiomatic links between directive processes, verification mechanisms, and the evidentiary artifacts required to substantiate organizational governance. The scope is restricted to the formal definition of four primary relational structures: attestation with supporting evidence, verification producing evidence, constraint evaluation timing, and evidence requirements for controls.

## 1. Attestation with Supporting Evidence

In the context of governance frameworks, an attestation is formally defined not merely as a statement of fact, but as a process that is ontologically dependent on pre-existing artifacts. The axiom `attestation_with_supporting_evidence` posits that for an attestation process (Class X) to be valid, it must possess a specific relationship to a supporting evidence class (Class Y). This relationship ensures that all assertions of compliance are anchored to tangible data artifacts, thereby preventing vacuous claims of control effectiveness.

The attestation process acts as a consumer of evidence. It does not generate the artifact; rather, it validates or certifies the veracity of the artifact. From an audit perspective, this distinction is critical. The auditor (the agent performing the attestation) must examine the artifact (the supporting evidence) to render a judgment. If the artifact is missing, the attestation process cannot be instantiated, resulting in a control failure.

### 1.1. Structural Dependency and Provenance

The dependency modeled here is unidirectional. The supporting evidence exists independently of the attestation, often generated by operational systems or logging mechanisms. The attestation serves as the bridge between raw data and governance assurance. For example, consider a "Quarterly Financial Review." This process is an instance of an attestation. It cannot occur without the "General Ledger Extract." The ledger extract is the supporting evidence. The ontology maps this by classifying the review as a subclass of `bfo:Process` that necessitates the presence of the ledger extract via the `sdg:withSupportingEvidence` object property.

This mapping enforces a rigorous chain of custody. By defining the attestation as a process that *has* supporting evidence, the framework implicitly demands that the evidence be retrievable, readable, and authentic before the attestation can be signed off. This prevents retroactive attestation where a process is claimed to have been completed without the underlying data to support it.

## 2. Verification Produces Evidence

While attestation consumes evidence, verification is a generative process. The axiom `verification_produces_evidence` describes a relationship wherein a verification process (Class X) actively creates an evidence artifact (Class Y). This distinction is vital for automated compliance systems, where the act of checking a system state results in the creation of a log, hash, or status report that serves as proof of the check itself.

Verification processes are often mechanical or algorithmic. They involve the execution of a script, a sensor scan, or a mathematical validation. The output of this execution is the evidence. This creates a closed loop for compliance: the existence of the evidence proves that the verification occurred. If the evidence is missing, one can infer that the verification process either failed or was never executed.

### 2.1. Generative Mechanisms and Integrity

The ontology classifies verification as a subclass of `bfo:Process`. The `sdg:producesEvidence` property links this process to the artifact it generates. This is particularly relevant for continuous monitoring environments. For instance, a "CryptographicHashVerification" process will produce a "HashMatchCertificate." The certificate is the evidence; it is created *by* the verification. This differs from the attestation model in that the evidence does not exist prior to the process.

This generative capability allows organizations to automate evidence collection. Rather than manually gathering logs, the governance framework can point to the output of the verification process as the required proof. This reduces the risk of human error in evidence aggregation and ensures the integrity of the compliance data, as the evidence is a direct byproduct of the control execution.

## 3. Constraint Evaluated During

Directives within a governance framework are not static; they are evaluated within specific temporal or procedural contexts. The axiom `constraint_evaluated_during` formalizes this by linking a directive or constraint (Class X) to the phase or event (Class Y) during which the evaluation occurs. This ensures that controls are applied at the correct juncture in a workflow, rather than arbitrarily or post-hoc.

The subject of this axiom is a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` (Information Control Element). These directives represent rules, requirements, or constraints that the system must satisfy. The object, Class Y, represents the evaluation context. This could be a specific phase in a software development lifecycle (e.g., "CommitPhase"), a stage in a procurement process (e.g., "VendorSelection"), or a recurring maintenance window.

### 3.1. Temporal and Procedural Binding

Binding constraints to evaluation phases prevents scope creep and ensures that controls are relevant to the current state of the asset or process. For example, a "CodeQualityConstraint" is only relevant when code is being written or committed. Evaluating it during the "DeploymentPhase" would be inefficient and likely too late to prevent defects. The ontology captures this by stating that the constraint is evaluated *during* the specific phase.

This binding also facilitates the mapping of controls to the MITRE ATT&CK framework or similar lifecycle models. By explicitly defining the "during" relationship, the governance framework can automate the triggering of evaluation scripts. When the system enters the "VendorSelection" phase, the "VendorSolvencyConstraint" is automatically triggered for evaluation.

Similarly, `CON-HR-103` ensures that "BackgroundScreeningRequirement" is evaluated specifically during the "CandidateOnboarding" phase, anchoring the control to the relevant business process.

## 4. Control with Evidence Requirement

The final axiom, `control_with_evidence_requirement`, defines the prescriptive aspect of governance. It asserts that a control (Class X), classified as a `cco:DirectiveICE`, necessitates the presence of a specific type of evidence (Class Y) to demonstrate compliance. This is the mandate that drives the collection of artifacts throughout the organization.

Unlike verification, which *produces* evidence, or attestation, which *consumes* it, a control *requires* it. This is a declarative relationship. The control defines the rules of engagement; the evidence is the proof that those rules were followed. This axiom is the bridge between the theoretical governance framework and the practical audit trail.

### 4.1. Compliance Mandates and Artifact Specification

The specificity of this relationship is what distinguishes a mature governance framework from a generic checklist. Instead of merely stating "keep records," the axiom allows the framework to state "Control X requires Evidence Y." For instance, a "PhysicalAccessControl" does not just require "some logs"; it requires a "BadgeSwipeRecord." This specificity reduces ambiguity during audits and ensures that the data collected is fit for purpose.

This requirement also drives the design of IT systems. If a governance framework dictates that a "ChangeManagementControl" requires a "SignedChangeRequest," the IT service management (ITSM) software must be configured to capture and store that specific artifact type. The ontology thus serves as a functional requirement for the system architecture.
