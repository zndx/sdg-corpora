# Chapter 4: Evidence Lifecycle and Control Verification in Directive Governance

### 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the formal relationships governing the generation, requirement, and utilization of evidence within the context of directive governance frameworks. It establishes the ontological boundaries for attestation processes, verification protocols, and the temporal evaluation of constraints, ensuring that control mechanisms are anchored to verifiable data artifacts. The definitions provided herein are essential for auditors, compliance officers, and system architects who must map procedural assertions to objective proof points. By rigorously defining these links, the framework ensures that governance directives are not merely theoretical but are operationally bound to specific evidence streams.

## 1. Attestation with Supporting Evidence

### 1.1 Conceptual Definition

In the context of formal governance, an attestation is defined as a process by which an entity asserts the validity of a specific state or condition. However, per the axiom `attestation_with_supporting_evidence`, such a process is ontologically incomplete unless it is substantiated by supporting evidence. The Manchester syntax expression `Class: {X:Class} SubClassOf: bfo:0000015, sdg:withSupportingEvidence some {Y:Class}` formalizes this by asserting that any attestation process (X) must possess a specific relationship to an evidence class (Y). This relationship ensures that the act of attestation is not a vacuous declaration but is grounded in objective reality.

The verbalization "{X} is something that with supporting evidence {Y}" implies a dependency. The integrity of the attestation process is directly proportional to the quality and relevance of the supporting evidence. In audit practice, this means that a signed compliance report (the attestation) must reference specific logs, configuration snapshots, or transaction records that prove the reported state is true. Without this linkage, the attestation fails to meet the criteria of `bfo:0000015` (a process occurring in time) as a valid governance event.

### 1.2 Implementation and Audit Trails

When implementing this axiom within a governance database, it is critical to maintain referential integrity between the process definition and the evidence artifacts. The primary key of the attestation record serves as the anchor for the audit trail, while the foreign key pointing to the evidence class ensures that only recognized, valid forms of proof are accepted. This structure prevents the submission of unauthorized or irrelevant documentation as proof of compliance.

For instance, consider a "System Availability Attestation." To satisfy the ontology, this attestation cannot stand alone; it must be linked to an evidence class such as "Uptime Monitoring Logs." Similarly, a "Data Privacy Attestation" requires linkage to "Access Control Registers." ## 2. Verification Produces Evidence

### 2.1 Conceptual Definition

While attestation consumes evidence to support a claim, the verification process is generative; it creates evidence as an output of its execution. The axiom `verification_produces_evidence` is expressed in Manchester syntax as `Class: {X:Class} SubClassOf: bfo:0000015, sdg:producesEvidence some {Y:Class}`. Here, {X} represents the verification activity, and {Y} represents the evidence artifact generated by that activity.

The verbalization "{X} is something that produces evidence {Y}" distinguishes verification from passive observation. A verification is an active process—such as a diagnostic scan, a hash comparison, or a logical test—that results in a tangible output. This output serves as the proof that the verification actually occurred and what its result was. In a forensic or regulatory context, the absence of this generated evidence implies that the verification process either did not take place or was not recorded, constituting a significant compliance failure.

### 2.2 The Generative Loop of Compliance

The distinction between supporting evidence and produced evidence is vital for constructing a robust audit chain. Produced evidence often becomes the supporting evidence for a higher-level attestation. For example, a "Vulnerability Scan" (verification) produces a "Scan Report" (evidence). This report is then used as supporting evidence for a "Cybersecurity Posture Attestation." This hierarchy allows complex governance frameworks to decompose high-level requirements into executable, verifiable steps.

The relational schema for this concept treats the verification event as the parent entity. The `id` column tracks the specific instance of the verification execution, while `x` defines the type of verification performed and `y` categorizes the resulting artifact. This ensures that every evidence artifact in the system can be traced back to the specific process that generated it, establishing non-repudiation.

## 3. Constraint Evaluation Timing

### 3.1 Conceptual Definition

Directives and constraints within a governance framework do not exist in a vacuum; they must be evaluated at specific points in a lifecycle or workflow. The axiom `constraint_evaluated_during` formalizes this temporal binding. The Manchester syntax `Class: {X:Class} SubClassOf: cco:DirectiveICE, sdg:evaluatedDuring some {Y:Class}` asserts that a constraint or directive (X) is assessed during a specific phase or event (Y).

The verbalization "{X} is something that evaluated during {Y}" highlights the context-dependency of compliance controls. A constraint regarding "Data Sovereignty," for example, is irrelevant if evaluated after data has already been transferred across borders; it must be evaluated during the "Data Ingestion Phase." This axiom allows governance architects to map controls to the specific operational windows where they are applicable, reducing false positives and ensuring that checks are performed at the moment of risk.

### 3.2 Temporal Binding and Phase Gates

In practice, this axiom facilitates the creation of "phase gates" in software development, procurement, or data processing pipelines. By associating a constraint (`cco:DirectiveICE`) with a specific phase (`Y`), the system can enforce blocking behaviors. If the constraint is not satisfied during the designated phase, the workflow cannot proceed. This transforms compliance from a retrospective exercise into a preventative control mechanism.

The relational structure for this axiom links the directive to the phase. The `id` serves as the unique constraint identifier, `x` is the specific rule or directive being applied, and `y` is the operational phase. This mapping is crucial for automated compliance engines that need to trigger rule sets based on the current state of the underlying process.

## 4. Control Evidence Requirements

### 4.1 Conceptual Definition

The final axiom addresses the prescriptive nature of governance controls. A control is not merely a suggestion; it is a mandate that carries specific evidentiary burdens. The axiom `control_with_evidence_requirement` is defined as `Class: {X:Class} SubClassOf: cco:DirectiveICE, sdg:requiresEvidence some {Y:Class}`. This asserts that a control (X) necessitates the presence of a specific type of evidence (Y).

The verbalization "{X} is something that requires evidence {Y}" establishes the criteria for audit readiness. When a control is implemented—such as a "Multi-Factor Authentication" policy—the axiom dictates that mere implementation is insufficient. There must be a requirement to generate and retain evidence, such as "MFA Token Logs," to demonstrate that the control is functioning. This bridges the gap between policy definition and operational reality.

### 4.2 Defining the Burden of Proof

For auditors, this axiom provides a checklist for validation. When reviewing a control, the auditor queries the system for the evidence type specified in the requirement. If the control requires "Quarterly Review Sign-offs," the absence of such documents constitutes a direct violation of the control's definition. This formalization prevents ambiguity where a control might be considered "implemented" despite a lack of proof.

The relational schema ensures that every control defined in the system is explicitly linked to an evidence requirement. The `id` uniquely identifies the control, `x` names the control, and `y` specifies the mandatory evidence class. This structure is fundamental for generating gap analysis reports, as it immediately highlights controls where the required evidence is missing or expired.
