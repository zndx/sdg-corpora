# Chapter 4: Evidence Lifecycle and Directive Governance in Compliance Frameworks

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the structural relationships governing the generation, consumption, and evaluation of evidence within directive governance frameworks. It establishes the ontological boundaries for compliance processes, specifically focusing on the interaction between attestation mechanisms, verification protocols, and the constraints imposed by internal controls. The content herein is intended to provide a rigorous reference for auditors and compliance officers who must map procedural artifacts to regulatory requirements. We define the precise conditions under which a process is validated by supporting evidence, how verification activities generate new evidence artifacts, and the temporal or logical phases during which specific constraints are evaluated. The following sections adhere to the formal axioms defined in the governance ontology, ensuring that all relational mappings are deterministic and auditable.

## 4.2 Attestation with Supporting Evidence

### 4.2.1 Definition and Ontological Basis

In the context of governance, an attestation is formally defined as a process (bfo:0000015) that validates the state of a system or process through the direct reference to pre-existing artifacts. According to the axiom `attestation_with_supporting_evidence`, an attestation cannot exist in a vacuum; it is structurally dependent on the existence of a supporting evidence class. This relationship ensures that every compliance claim is anchored to a tangible artifact, thereby preventing baseless assertions of control effectiveness.

The verbalization of this axiom—"{X} is something that with supporting evidence {Y}"—implies a directional dependency. The attestation (X) derives its validity from the evidence (Y). In a relational schema, this is modeled as a foreign key dependency where the evidence record must exist and be accessible at the time the attestation is recorded. This distinction is critical for audit trails: if the supporting evidence is subsequently deleted or modified, the integrity of the attestation is fundamentally compromised.

### 4.2.2 Evidence Dependency and Audit Validity

When constructing an audit report, the practitioner must identify the specific evidence class that supports each attestation statement. For instance, a "System Availability Attestation" is meaningless without a corresponding "Uptime Monitor Log." The ontology requires that the class of the evidence matches the expectation of the attestation process.

The following table illustrates specific instances of this relationship. It maps the attestation process (X) to the specific class of supporting evidence (Y) required to validate it. This structure mirrors the itemized specificity found in technical supply lists, ensuring that every requirement is accounted for with a corresponding resource.

| :--- | :--- | :--- |

### 4.2.3 Implementation Guidelines

Organizations must implement version control for all evidence classes referenced in Table 4-1. Because the attestation is a process occurring at a specific point in time, the supporting evidence must be immutable as of that timestamp. Any modification to the evidence class post-attestation invalidates the relational integrity defined in the schema. Therefore, governance frameworks must enforce write-once, read-many (WORM) storage policies for all artifacts listed in the `y` column.

## 4.3 Verification Produces Evidence

### 4.3.1 Definition and Ontological Basis

While attestation consumes evidence, verification produces it. The axiom `verification_produces_evidence` defines a class of processes (X) that result in the generation of a new evidence artifact (Y). This distinction is vital for understanding the flow of information within a compliance management system. Verification is an active process—such as a diagnostic scan, a reconciliation script, or a physical inspection—that creates an output record serving as proof of the activity's execution.

The verbalization "{X} is something that produces evidence {Y}" establishes a generative relationship. Unlike the dependency seen in attestation, here the process is the antecedent. In terms of audit readiness, this means that the absence of evidence (Y) serves as positive proof that the verification process (X) did not occur, or failed to capture its output.

### 4.3.2 Evidence Generation and Provenance

The provenance of compliance data relies heavily on the correct application of this axiom. When a verification process runs, it must populate a specific evidence class. For example, a "Configuration Verification" process should produce a "Configuration Drift Report." The schema for this relationship enforces that every verification instance is linked to exactly one produced evidence instance.

### 4.3.3 Automation and Integrity

To ensure the non-repudiation of the evidence produced, the verification processes (X) should be automated wherever possible. Manual verification processes often introduce latency and potential errors in evidence generation (Y). The governance framework mandates that any entry in Table 4-2 involving high-frequency processes (e.g., `NetworkSegmentationCheck`) must be executed via automated scripts that write directly to the immutable evidence repository.

## 4.4 Constraint Evaluated During

### 4.3.1 Definition and Ontological Basis

Constraints are specific types of directives (cco:DirectiveICE) that enforce boundaries on system behavior or data handling. The axiom `constraint_evaluated_during` posits that a constraint (X) is not static in its application but is actively assessed during a specific phase, event, or temporal window (Y). This temporal binding ensures that constraints are relevant to the context in which they are applied.

The verbalization "{X} is something that evaluated during {Y}" introduces the concept of the "Evaluation Window." A constraint may be valid in perpetuity, but its *evaluation*—the actual checking of compliance against that constraint—occurs at discrete intervals. This mirrors the structure of educational curricula where specific learning objectives are targeted during specific terms; here, specific compliance constraints are targeted during specific operational phases.

### 4.3.3 Phase-Based Compliance Logic

It is insufficient to merely define the constraint (X); the governance framework must explicitly define the trigger for phase (Y). For example, the `ChangeManagementFreezeConstraint` is only evaluated during the `ProductionReleaseWindow`. If an attempt is made to deploy code outside of this window, the constraint evaluation logic must block the action. This tight coupling between the constraint and the evaluation phase is enforced by the schema to prevent "scope creep" in compliance enforcement.

## 4.5 Control with Evidence Requirement

### 4.5.1 Definition and Ontological Basis

Controls are the operational implementations of governance policies. The axiom `control_with_evidence_requirement` defines a control (X) as a directive that necessitates the collection of a specific type of evidence (Y). This is distinct from verification producing evidence; rather, this axiom defines the *demand* side of the equation. The control exists to enforce a behavior, and the proof of that behavior's existence is the required evidence.

The verbalization "{X} is something that requires evidence {Y}" establishes a mandatory collection protocol. If a control is implemented, the evidence class (Y) must be populated. Failure to produce the evidence constitutes a failure of the control itself. This relationship is foundational for "continuous control monitoring" (CCM) systems, which automate the checking of evidence availability for every active control.
