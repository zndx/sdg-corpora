# Chapter 4: Governance Frameworks and Evidence Provenance

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the structural relationships governing the generation, requirement, and utilization of evidence within a formalized compliance environment. It establishes the ontological boundaries for attestation processes, control directives, and verification mechanisms, ensuring that all governance artifacts are traceable to specific risk mitigation strategies and evidentiary requirements. The definitions provided herein align with the Birmingham City Council Financial Procedures Manual and the standards set forth by the Information Governance Committee, serving as the authoritative reference for auditing personnel and compliance officers. All entities described are grounded in formal axioms that map specific governance classes to their evidentiary counterparts, facilitating rigorous data integrity and automated verification.

## 4.2 Attestation with Supporting Evidence

### 4.2.1 Conceptual Definition

Attestation constitutes a formal process by which an authorized entity validates the veracity of a specific state or operation. Within the context of information governance, an attestation is not merely a declaration but a processual entity (`bfo:0000015`) that is intrinsically dependent on the existence of supporting artifacts. The integrity of an attestation is null if the evidentiary chain is broken; therefore, the relationship between the attesting process and its evidence is one of strict existential dependency. This ensures that any claim of compliance is anchored in tangible, auditable data.

### 4.2.2 Implementation and Compliance

In practice, the attestation process acts as a wrapper around evidence collection. For instance, a "Quarterly Access Review" attestation is only valid when supported by "System Access Logs." The governance framework mandates that the linkage between the attestation and the evidence be explicitly recorded in the central registry. This allows auditors to trace the lineage of a compliance claim back to the source data, ensuring that the attestation is not a standalone assertion but a derivative of verified records.

## 4.3 Control with Evidence Requirement

### 4.3.1 Directive Governance

Controls, defined as directives (`cco:DirectiveICE`), function as the primary mechanism for enforcing organizational policy. Unlike passive guidelines, controls possess an active property: they require evidence. This requirement is not optional but constitutive of the control itself. A control that does not explicitly specify the evidence necessary to demonstrate its operation is considered incomplete within the governance framework. This axiom ensures that every control implemented within the infrastructure is auditable by design.

### 4.3.2 Evidence Specification

The requirement for evidence transforms a control from a theoretical rule into an enforceable procedure. For example, a "Password Rotation Control" is ineffective unless it produces evidence of rotation, such as "Password Change Logs." The framework requires that the specific evidence type be cataloged for each control. This granularity allows compliance teams to automate evidence collection, as the system knows precisely which artifacts to fetch for a given control check.

## 4.4 Requirement Specification and Targeting

### 4.4.1 Equivalence and Specificity

A requirement is a specialized form of directive that is defined by three concurrent characteristics: it is a directive, it specifies a particular action or state, and it applies to a specific target. This tripartite equivalence ensures that requirements are never vague. A requirement must explicitly state *what* is to be achieved (the specification) and *where* or *whom* it applies to (the target). This structure eliminates ambiguity in regulatory adherence and facilitates precise mapping between organizational mandates and technical implementations.

### 4.4.2 Target Application

The application of a requirement to a target is critical for scope management. For instance, a "Data Retention Requirement" might specify "7-Year Storage" and apply to "Transaction Databases." By decoupling the specification from the target, the framework allows for the reuse of requirements across different domains. A single specification can be applied to multiple targets, or a single target can be subject to multiple specifications, creating a matrix of compliance obligations that can be queried and analyzed efficiently.

## 4.5 Verification and Evidence Production

### 4.5.1 The Verification Process

Verification is distinct from attestation in that it is an active process of inquiry or testing that results in the creation of new artifacts. While attestation relies on existing evidence, verification generates evidence (`sdg:producesEvidence`). This process is fundamental to the audit lifecycle, as it provides the raw data that subsequent attestations will consume. Verification activities include automated scans, manual inspections, and consistency checks, all of which yield outputs that serve as proof of the system's state at a specific point in time.

### 4.5.2 Evidence Generation

The output of a verification process must be standardized to be useful for governance. A "Vulnerability Scan" (the process) produces a "Scan Report" (the evidence). The governance framework mandates that the format and metadata of this evidence are standardized to allow for automated ingestion. This ensures that the evidence produced is not only admissible but also machine-readable, enabling continuous monitoring and real-time compliance dashboards.

## 4.6 Control-Threat Alignment

### 4.6.1 Risk Addressance

Controls are implemented specifically to mitigate risks. The ontology defines that a control addresses risk (`sdg:addressesRisk`) *only* if that risk belongs to a specific class. This restriction is vital for ensuring that controls are not over-specified or applied vaguely. By constraining the relationship to a specific threat class, the framework forces a precise mapping between the safeguard and the danger it is intended to neutralize. This precision is necessary for risk assessment methodologies that calculate residual risk based on the efficacy of applied controls.

### 4.6.2 Threat Classification

The classification of threats allows for the grouping of controls. For example, a "Malware Signature Control" addresses the threat class "Malicious Software Infection." It does not address "Physical Theft." This clear demarcation prevents gaps in coverage where a threat might be assumed mitigated by a control that does not actually apply to it. The governance framework utilizes this mapping to visualize the organization's security posture and identify unmitigated threat classes.
