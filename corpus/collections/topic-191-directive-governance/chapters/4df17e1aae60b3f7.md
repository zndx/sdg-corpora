# Chapter 4: Governance Directives and Evidence Management Frameworks

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the structural relationships between governance directives, evidence artifacts, and risk mitigation strategies within a formal compliance framework. The content herein defines the ontological axioms that bind control mechanisms to their evidentiary requirements and specifies the procedural verification of these artifacts. It is intended for systems architects, compliance officers, and audit professionals who require a precise mapping of directive classes to their corresponding data and process outputs. The following sections establish the necessary logical constraints for implementing a robust governance, risk, and compliance (GRC) database schema, ensuring that all regulatory obligations are traced to verifiable proof points.

## 4.2 Attestation with Supporting Evidence

### 4.2.1 Conceptual Definition
In the context of formal governance, an attestation is not merely a declarative statement but a distinct process (bfo:Process) that validates the state of compliance against a specified standard. According to the axiom `attestation_with_supporting_evidence`, an attestation is axiomatically dependent on the existence of supporting evidence. This relationship ensures that any attestation instance is structurally invalid unless it is backed by a specific evidentiary artifact. The evidence serves as the factual basis for the attestation, transforming it from a subjective claim into an auditable record.

### 4.2.2 Implementation and Audit Implications
When modeling this within a relational database, the integrity of the compliance framework relies on the enforcement of the foreign key constraint linking the attestation to its evidence. Auditors must verify that for every record of attestation, a corresponding evidence record exists. This prevents "orphan" attestations—claims of compliance that lack substantiation. For instance, a "System Security Attestation" cannot exist without a corresponding "Penetration Test Report" or "Vulnerability Scan Summary." The schema enforces this by defining the evidence slot as mandatory (NOT NULL).

## 4.3 Control Evidence Requirements

### 4.3.1 Conceptual Definition
Directives within the governance framework, specifically those classified as `cco:DirectiveICE`, function as operational controls. The axiom `control_with_evidence_requirement` posits that for a control to be effective, it must explicitly require the generation or collection of evidence. This moves beyond the passive existence of a control and focuses on its active demand for proof. A control that does not mandate evidence is functionally opaque and cannot be measured for efficacy.

### 4.3.2 Operational Directives
The requirement for evidence is a critical component of the control design phase. When a control is instantiated—such as a "Change Management Procedure"—it must inherently specify the artifacts it demands, such as "Change Request Forms" or "Approval Minutes." This linkage allows compliance officers to query the system for all evidence required by a specific control, facilitating gap analysis during audit preparation. The schema treats the control as the parent entity and the required evidence as the dependent child, ensuring that the control definition is never devoid of compliance metrics.

## 4.4 Requirement Specification and Target Application

### 4.4.1 Conceptual Definition
The definition of a requirement within this ontology is complex, involving an equivalence to a directive that simultaneously specifies a condition and applies to a target. The axiom `requirement_equiv_specifies_target` formalizes this by stating that a requirement (X) is equivalent to the intersection of being a `cco:DirectiveICE`, specifying a particular parameter or rule (Y), and applying to a specific entity or scope (Z). This tripartite structure ensures that requirements are never vague; they must define *what* is specified and *who* or *what* it applies to.

### 4.4.2 Governance Frameworks
This structure is essential for granular governance. It allows an organization to distinguish between a general policy and a specific, actionable requirement. For example, a "Data Retention Requirement" is not merely a concept; it is a directive that specifies a "Retention Schedule" and applies to "Customer Databases." This precise mapping eliminates ambiguity during enforcement. If a target (Z) is decommissioned, the requirement can be reviewed for relevance; if the specification (Y) changes, the impact on the requirement is immediate.

## 4.5 Verification and Evidence Production

### 4.5.1 Conceptual Definition
While attestation is the validation of a state, verification is the active process (bfo:Process) of investigating and producing the evidence itself. The axiom `verification_produces_evidence` distinguishes the *act* of verification from the *artifact* it creates. Verification is the mechanism by which raw data is transformed into auditable evidence. Without the verification process, evidence does not formally enter the compliance ecosystem; it remains merely data.

### 4.5.2 Procedural Generation
This concept emphasizes the "supply chain" of compliance. Verification activities—such as automated scripts, manual inspections, or third-party audits—must be modeled as productive processes. The output of these processes is the evidence consumed by the controls and attestations defined in previous sections. By tracking the verification process, organizations can audit the *provenance* of their evidence, ensuring that it has not been fabricated or altered outside of sanctioned procedures.

## 4.6 Control and Risk Addressing

### 4.6.1 Conceptual Definition
The ultimate purpose of a governance directive is to mitigate risk. The axiom `control_only_addresses_threat_class` formalizes the objective of a control by restricting its scope to addressing specific threat classes (Y). This axiom utilizes a "only" (universal) restriction, implying that the control is designed exclusively to address the specified threat. This precise categorization prevents "scope creep" in control implementation and ensures that controls are evaluated against the specific risks they were designed to mitigate.

### 4.6.2 Threat Mitigation Strategies
In practice, this means that a "Phishing Filter Control" is axiomatically linked to the "Social Engineering Threat" class. It does not address "Physical Intrusion." This strict typing allows for risk-based auditing. If an organization identifies a new threat vector, it can query the ontology to determine which existing controls are capable of addressing it. Conversely, it can identify gaps where threat classes exist without corresponding controls. The schema supports this by linking the control directly to the threat class.
