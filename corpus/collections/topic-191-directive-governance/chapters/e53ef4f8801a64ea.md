# Chapter 4: Governance Directives and Evidence Lifecycle Management

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the structural relationships between governance directives, procedural attestations, and the evidentiary artifacts required to maintain a defensible compliance posture. It provides a formal ontology for mapping control objectives to specific evidence types, defining the scope of requirements, and verifying the mitigation of threat classes. The principles outlined herein are intended for use by compliance officers, internal auditors, and information security governance teams to establish a rigorous, auditable framework for directive management and evidence collection.

## 4.2 Attestation with Supporting Evidence

### 4.2.1 Conceptual Definition

Within the governance framework, an attestation is formally defined not merely as a declaration, but as a process (`bfo:Process`) that is intrinsically dependent on the existence and validation of specific supporting artifacts. The axiom `attestation_with_supporting_evidence` establishes that for an attestation process to be valid, it must possess a relationship with a supporting evidence class. This relationship ensures that assertions made by management or system operators are grounded in verifiable data rather than subjective opinion. The integrity of the attestation is directly proportional to the robustness of the evidence class to which it refers.

### 4.2.2 Implementation and Procedural Context

In practice, this means that an auditor or compliance officer cannot validate a control's effectiveness without referencing the specific artifact class generated by that control. For instance, a "System Access Review" is an attestation process that is invalid unless it is supported by "Access Log Extracts." The process is the act of review; the evidence is the data reviewed. The ontology mandates that every attestation instance must be linked to a defined evidence class to satisfy the `sdg:withSupportingEvidence` object property.

## 4.3 Control with Evidence Requirement

### 4.3.1 Conceptual Definition

A control, modeled as a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` (Information Control Element), functions as a directive that necessitates the creation or retention of specific data artifacts. The axiom `control_with_evidence_requirement` formalizes the obligation that a control imposes on the system environment: it `sdg:requiresEvidence` of a specific class. This distinguishes a theoretical policy from an implemented control; the latter must produce a tangible output to be considered operational during an audit.

### 4.3.2 Directive Obligations

The requirement for evidence is the mechanism by which governance frameworks transition from abstract rules to observable states. When a control is instantiated, it must be configured to trigger the generation of the specified evidence class. Failure to produce this evidence constitutes a control failure or a finding of deficiency. For example, an "Access Control Directive" is only effective if it requires "Authentication Logs" as proof of execution.

## 4.4 Requirement Specification and Targeting

### 4.4.1 Conceptual Definition

Complex governance requirements are often compound constructs that specify an action while simultaneously defining the scope of application. The axiom `requirement_equiv_specifies_target` captures this tripartite structure. A requirement (`X`) is equivalent to a directive that `sdg:specifies` a particular action or state (`Y`) and `sdg:appliesTo` a specific target entity or class (`Z`). This axiom prevents ambiguity in compliance mandates by explicitly binding the "what" (specification) to the "who/what" (target).

### 4.4.2 Scope and Specification Analysis

This formalism is critical for audit scoping. A requirement such as "Users must rotate passwords every 90 days" is decomposed into: the Requirement itself (`X`), the Specification (`Y`) of "90-day rotation," and the Target (`Z`) of "User Account." By structuring requirements in this manner, automated governance tools can validate that the specification is met across the defined target population without misapplying the rule to unrelated entities (e.g., service accounts which may have different policies).

## 4.5 Verification Produces Evidence

### 4.5.1 Conceptual Definition

Verification is a distinct process from attestation. While attestation consumes evidence to validate a state, verification (`X`) is a proactive process (`bfo:Process`) that `sdg:producesEvidence` (`Y`). This axiom covers the activities of auditors, automated scanners, and quality assurance teams. The output of a verification process is a new artifact—often a report, a scan result, or a certification—that serves as proof that the verification activity occurred.

### 4.5.2 The Verification Lifecycle

In the context of a governance framework, verification processes are the primary generators of high-fidelity evidence. They are the tools used to test the effectiveness of controls. The relationship defined here is causal: the execution of the verification process (e.g., a penetration test) causes the creation of the evidence (e.g., the penetration test report). This evidence is subsequently consumed by attestation processes to certify the overall security posture.

## 4.6 Control Addresses Risk Class

### 4.6.1 Conceptual Definition

The ultimate purpose of any control directive is to mitigate risk. The axiom `control_only_addresses_threat_class` formalizes the relationship between a control (`X`) and the specific threat class (`Y`) it is designed to address. The use of the "only" restriction (`only {Y:Class}`) in the ontology implies a precise mapping: the control is intended to address *only* that specific threat class (or its subclasses). This precision prevents "scope creep" in control implementation and ensures that controls are evaluated against the specific risks they were designed to mitigate.

### 4.6.2 Risk-Control Alignment

Aligning controls to threat classes is a fundamental activity in Risk Management Frameworks (RMF). A misalignment here—where a control addresses a threat class different from the one identified in the risk assessment—results in "residual risk." For example, a "Data Loss Prevention (DLP) Control" addresses the threat class of "Data Exfiltration." It does not directly address "Insider Threat" (though they may overlap) or "Physical Theft." This axiom enforces a strict categorization to facilitate accurate risk scoring.
