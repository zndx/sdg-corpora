# Chapter 7: Formal Ontology of Evidence and Belief Structures in Compliance Frameworks

## 7.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the formalization of evidentiary structures within automated compliance governance systems, establishing a rigorous axiomatic foundation for the representation of belief and verification processes. It addresses the integration of descriptive information content entities (ICE) with procedural attestations, utilizing a modified Dempster-Shafer theoretical approach to quantify confidence levels over specific frames of discernment. The objective is to provide a deterministic schema for mapping subjective audit assertions to objective, verifiable data trails, ensuring that regulatory claims are anchored in logically consistent and evidence-backed structures. The following sections detail the specific ontology axioms governing mass functions, attestation protocols, refutation mechanics, and the persistence of non-disconfirmed claims.

## 7.2 Mass Functions over Frames of Discernment

In the context of high-assurance compliance frameworks, a "mass function" serves as a mathematical formalization of belief allocation, distinct from classical probability theory by its ability to handle uncertainty explicitly. Within the ontology, a Mass Function Subclass is defined as a specific type of Descriptive Information Content Entity (ICE) that distributes a "mass" (or basic belief assignment) over a specific Frame of Discernment. The Frame of Discernment represents the set of mutually exclusive propositions or hypotheses that are considered possible within a given audit context.

The structural integrity of this concept relies on the recursive relationship between the belief distribution and the process it evaluates. Specifically, the Frame of Discernment (Y) is not merely a passive set of possibilities but is structurally bound to an active Attestation Process. This linkage ensures that the belief distribution is always scoped to a verifiable governance activity. Without this linkage, the belief exists in a vacuum, rendering it auditable but non-verifiable. By constraining the Frame of Discernment to an attestation process, the ontology mandates that all subjective confidence levels must be traceable to an objective, documented activity.

## 7.3 Attestation with Supporting Evidence

While the mass function defines the scope and distribution of belief, the Attestation with Supporting Evidence axiom defines the mechanism by which that belief is substantiated. An attestation is formally categorized as a Process (specifically, a `bfo:0000015` Processual Entity), representing the active execution of a verification protocol. The critical distinction in this axiom is the requirement that the process must possess "supporting evidence." In this ontological framework, the supporting evidence (Y) is explicitly defined as a Mass Function.

This creates a rigorous evidentiary loop: the Attestation Process provides the context (the Frame of Discernment) for the Mass Function, while the Mass Function provides the evidentiary weight (the Supporting Evidence) for the Attestation. This reciprocal dependency prevents circular logic in manual audits by enforcing that the "evidence" for a process is itself a formally defined belief structure that has been previously scoped to that same process. The relational schema for this axiom enforces that the evidence cited (`y`) must be a validly instantiated mass function.

This shifts the compliance focus from "task completion" to "evidentiary value generation," a paradigm shift essential for continuous control monitoring (CCM) environments.

## 7.4 Claims Refuted by Evidence

A necessary component of any governance framework is the ability to formally negate false or non-compliant assertions. The Claim Refuted by Evidence axiom provides the structure for this negation. Here, a Claim (X) is a Descriptive ICE that is explicitly invalidated by a specific body of Evidence (Y). The ontology stipulates that the refuting evidence must be an Attestation Process.

This structure ensures that refutation is not arbitrary or based on hearsay. A claim is only considered refuted when a formal process (e.g., a forensic audit, a contradictory log analysis, or a penetration test) generates results that directly negate the claim's validity. The use of the Attestation Process as the refuting agent (`y`) enforces a high bar of proof; it requires procedural rigor to disprove a regulatory assertion, rather than simple observation.

For example, `ref_001` records the refutation of the "ClaimOfOpenPort443." The evidence cited is `attestation_01` (the Q3 Vulnerability Scan). In practice, this means the vulnerability scan process executed a set of probes (the Frame of Discernment) and produced a mass function (`mf_001`) indicating a high probability of security integrity, thereby refuting the claim that the port was open or vulnerable. This linkage allows auditors to trace not only why a system is compliant but also precisely why specific allegations of non-compliance were determined to be false.

## 7.5 Claims Not Disconfirmed

In logic and governance, the absence of evidence is not necessarily evidence of absence. The Claim Not Disconfirmed axiom addresses the "Open World Assumption," wherein a claim (X) persists as valid simply because it has not been proven to be a specific negation (Y). Unlike the previous axiom, which actively refutes a claim using evidence, this structure asserts that a claim is *not* equivalent to a disconfirmed state.

This is particularly relevant for "negative audit" findings or ongoing investigations where a violation has not been detected, but total certainty is impossible. Formally, the axiom states that Class X is not Class Y. In a compliance context, this might be used to assert that a "UserAccessClaim" is not a "UnauthorizedAccessClass." This does not prove the access was authorized; it merely proves the access was not *unauthorized* based on the current classification. This distinction is vital for maintaining accurate risk registers without falsely inflating assurance levels.

This axiom provides a logical safety net for compliance arguments where positive proof is computationally or procedurally infeasible to obtain, but negative proof (ruling out the forbidden) is readily available.
