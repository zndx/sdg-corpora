# Chapter 4: Evidence Lineage and Attestation Frameworks for Compliance Claims

This chapter establishes the formal ontological structures required to validate compliance claims within a regulated digital environment. It delineates the relationships between descriptive information content entities, the artifacts that support them, and the processes that attest to their validity. The provisions herein detail the necessary data schema and logical constraints to ensure that all governance assertions are anchored in verifiable evidence and traceable lineage, consistent with the requirements of the Data Governance Act and associated auditing standards.

## 1. Trace Support for Claims

### 1.1 Definition and Scope
Pursuant to the foundational axioms of the compliance ontology, a "Trace" is defined as a process-dependent entity that substantiates a specific descriptive claim. Formally, this is expressed as a subclass of `bfo:0000015` (a process) that bears the relationship `sdg:supportsClaim` toward a `cco:DescriptiveICE` (Information Content Entity). In practical terms, this means that no assertion regarding system state or data integrity may exist without a corresponding process trace that acts as its logical progenitor.

The regulatory framework mandates that these traces be immutable and temporally anchored. When a claim is generated—for instance, "User access was logged"—the system must produce a trace event (e.g., the writing of a log entry) that validates the claim. This relationship prevents the fabrication of retrospective claims without evidentiary backing.

## 2. Evidence Requirements for Claims

### 2.1 Minimum Cardinality Constraints
To ensure the robustness of compliance reporting, the ontology enforces a strict cardinality constraint on the relationship between claims and evidence. The axiom `claim_min_one_evidence` posits that any valid `cco:DescriptiveICE` (the Claim) must be supported by a minimum of one evidence entity (`Y`). This constraint eliminates the possibility of "orphan claims"—assertions that are stated but not substantiated by the system's operational records.

In the context of an audit, this translates to a validation rule: if a compliance report contains a row asserting a specific control was active, the database must reflect at least one join to an evidence table. The evidence entity (`Y`) must be a valid instance of a trace, as defined in the previous section.

### 2.2 Relational Dependencies
The relational schema for this concept relies on a foreign key constraint that physically enforces the logical dependency. The column `y` in the claims table must reference a valid `id` from the trace support registry. This structure ensures referential integrity; a claim cannot be entered into the database unless it points to an existing trace.

## 3. Column Lineage for Compliance Claims

### 3.1 Data Granularity and Provenance
While high-level process traces provide a broad evidentiary layer, granular compliance often requires tracking the transformation of specific data artifacts. The axiom `column_lineage_for_compliance_claim` addresses this by defining a subclass of `cco:Artifact` that supports a claim. Specifically, this applies to column-level lineage within data warehouses or ETL pipelines.

For example, a compliance claim might state that "Personally Identifiable Information (PII) has been hashed." The evidence for this claim is not merely a log file (a process trace), but the data artifact itself—the specific column in the database table containing the hashed values. This artifact supports the claim by its very existence and state.

## 4. Kernel Anomalies as Supporting Artifacts

### 4.1 Anomaly Detection in Governance
Modern governance frameworks increasingly rely on automated monitoring to detect deviations from expected behavior. The axiom `kernel_anomaly_supports_claim` formalizes the role of these deviations. A "kernel anomaly" (an artifact representing a statistical or structural outlier) is treated as a valid support for a descriptive claim.

This is particularly relevant for claims regarding system security or operational resilience. For instance, a claim such as "The system detected and logged an intrusion attempt" is supported by the artifact representing the anomaly itself (e.g., a spike in traffic or an unauthorized port scan). By classifying these anomalies as `cco:Artifact` subclasses, the ontology integrates security monitoring directly into the compliance evidence chain.

## 5. Attestation Frameworks

### 5.1 Formal Attestation Processes
The final step in the compliance chain is the human or automated verification of the claims and their evidence. The axiom `attestation_about_compliance_claim` defines an attestation as a process (`bfo:0000015`) that attests to the validity of a `cco:DescriptiveICE`. This is distinct from the raw evidence (traces or artifacts); it is the meta-process of review and sign-off.

Attestations are the mechanism by which an organization formally declares, "We have reviewed the evidence, and the claim is valid." This often involves a certification authority, an auditor, or an automated governance engine running a validation script. The ontology treats this attestation as a first-class process entity, ensuring that the act of verification itself is recorded and traceable.
