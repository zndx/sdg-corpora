# Chapter 7: Governance Frameworks and Evidence-Based Compliance Verification

## 7.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the structural architecture of compliance governance, specifically focusing on the formal relationships between regulatory directives, technical controls, and evidentiary artifacts. In the context of modern audit frameworks, the mere existence of a policy is insufficient; rather, compliance is demonstrated through a verifiable chain of custody linking high-level directives to specific, implemented controls and the evidence generated by their execution. The following sections introduce five foundational ontology concepts that govern this hierarchy. These concepts define how attestations are substantiated, how lawful bases are established under regulations such as the GDPR, and how verification mechanisms produce the necessary evidence to satisfy audit requirements. The discussion is grounded in a relational data model that enforces referential integrity, ensuring that every claim of compliance is anchored to a valid, authorized control or legal basis.

## 7.2 Attestation with Supporting Evidence

In governance frameworks, an attestation represents a formal declaration that a specific process or set of controls has been executed as intended. However, for an attestation to hold weight during an audit or regulatory review, it must be substantiated by supporting evidence. The ontology defines this relationship through the axiom that an attestation is a process that possesses supporting evidence. This implies that the act of attesting is not merely a self-contained assertion but is intrinsically linked to external data points or artifacts that validate the claim.

From a data modeling perspective, this relationship ensures that no attestation can exist within the system without a corresponding evidentiary anchor. This prevents "orphan" attestations—claims made without proof—which are a significant liability in compliance reporting. The supporting evidence typically takes the form of logs, screenshots, or processed records that demonstrate the operational reality of the directive.

## 7.3 GDPR Lawful Basis

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) mandates that any processing of personal data must be conducted on the basis of a legitimate legal justification. This concept is captured in the ontology by the assertion that a directive or processing activity has a lawful basis. This lawful basis serves as the cornerstone of compliance; without it, the processing activity is considered unlawful. In the context of the relational schema, the lawful basis acts as a bridge between the abstract directive (the policy or rule) and the concrete technical control that enforces it.

It is critical to understand that the lawful basis is not merely a static label but a dynamic entity that must be mapped to the controls which mitigate associated risks. The schema enforces a strict dependency where the lawful basis (`y`) must reference a control that addresses a specific threat class. This creates a closed loop of accountability: a directive cites a lawful basis, and that basis is validated by a control designed to manage the specific risks inherent in the processing activity.

## 7.4 Verification Produces Evidence

Verification is the systematic process of checking whether a control is functioning correctly and whether a directive is being followed. In a technical compliance environment, verification is a generative process; it yields artifacts—logs, reports, or system states—that serve as evidence. The ontology posits that verification is a process that produces evidence. This evidence is the factual output consumed by auditors and compliance officers to validate the state of the system.

The distinction between "supporting evidence" (Section 7.2) and "produced evidence" (Section 7.4) is subtle but important. Supporting evidence is the corpus of data required to back an attestation, whereas produced evidence is the specific output generated by a verification activity. In practice, a verification script might run a check on user permissions; the output of that script (the evidence) is then attached to an attestation to support it.

## 7.5 Control Only Addresses Threat Class

Risk management in information security relies on the precise mapping of controls to threats. A control is a safeguard or countermeasure designed to mitigate specific risks. The ontology axiom here is restrictive: a control addresses *only* a specific threat class. This exclusivity is a key design principle in governance frameworks, preventing "scope creep" where a single control is vaguely applied to multiple, unrelated risks. By enforcing a "only" relationship, the schema demands precision in definition and implementation.

This restriction has significant implications for audit trails. If a control is defined as addressing "Threat A," but evidence suggests it is mitigating "Threat B," a misalignment is flagged. This ensures that the control library is not only populated but is accurately semantically mapped to the threat landscape. The relational structure enforces this by linking the control to a threat class, which in this schema is identified via the lawful basis table, creating a tight coupling between the risk, the legal justification for managing it, and the technical solution.

## 7.6 Control with Evidence Requirement

For a control to be considered effective within a formal governance framework, it must be observable and verifiable. This means that the control cannot be a "black box"; it must generate or require evidence that demonstrates its operation. The ontology defines this by stating that a control requires evidence. This requirement drives the operational design of security tools and administrative processes; they must be configured to output logs, status reports, or other artifacts that can be ingested by the compliance system.

This concept closes the loop on the governance lifecycle. A directive establishes a need for a lawful basis; a control is implemented to address the associated threat; that control is required to produce evidence; verification processes collect that evidence; and finally, an attestation is made supported by that evidence. The "evidence requirement" is thus the functional specification that transforms a theoretical control into an auditable asset.
