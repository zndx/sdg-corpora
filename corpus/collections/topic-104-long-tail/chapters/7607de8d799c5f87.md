# Chapter 4: Evidence-Based Governance and Regulatory Compliance Frameworks

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the structural dependencies between governance directives, verification processes, and evidence generation within a formal compliance framework. It provides a rigorous examination of how attestation protocols rely upon lawful bases established under GDPR, and how verification mechanisms function as the primary generators of audit trails. The text further defines the relational constraints between control implementations and the specific threat classes they are designed to mitigate, emphasizing the necessity of artifact generation for regulatory adherence. The concepts presented herein are foundational for establishing a defensible audit posture and ensuring that information security controls are not merely theoretical implementations but are evidenced, verifiable, and legally grounded processes.

## 4.2 Attestation with Supporting Evidence

Attestation represents a formal assertion, often by a senior officer or designated authority, regarding the operational state of a specific process or control. Within the context of the ontology, an attestation is defined as a process (bfo:Process) that possesses the property of `withSupportingEvidence`. This relationship is axiomatic: for an attestation to be valid, it must be substantiated by a specific, referenced evidence artifact. The evidence acts as the anchor for the claim, transforming a subjective declaration into an objective, auditable data point.

In practice, this means that a self-assessment questionnaire regarding data privacy cannot exist in a vacuum. The assertion that "data is encrypted at rest" is the attestation (the *X* class), while the specific cryptographic configuration export or the third-party penetration test report constitutes the supporting evidence (the *Y* class). The integrity of the governance framework relies on the one-to-many mapping of attestations to their evidentiary backing; an attestation lacking a linked evidence object is considered null and void in an audit scenario.

## 4.3 GDPR Lawful Basis

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) mandates that any processing of personal data must be grounded in a specific lawful basis. In this framework, the lawful basis is modeled as a subclass of a Directive (cco:DirectiveICE) which possesses the property `hasLawfulBasis`. This axiom establishes that a directive or processing activity is valid only if it points to a specific, authorized legal justification. This is not merely a metadata tag but a structural requirement for the validity of the processing activity itself.

The lawful basis serves as the bridge between the technical implementation of controls and the legal rationale for their existence. For instance, a control implementing encryption (the *X* class) is valid because it supports a lawful basis such as "Contractual Necessity" (the *Y* class). The ontology dictates that the lawful basis slot must be populated by a reference to a control that addresses a specific threat class, creating a chain of validity from legal requirement down to technical mitigation.

This relationship ensures that compliance is not abstract. By linking the lawful basis to a control that addresses a threat, the organization demonstrates that the legal requirement is actively being managed through security measures. The table below details the directives and their corresponding lawful bases, which are in turn implemented by specific threat-addressing controls.

| DIR-MKT-001 | Customer Analytics Processing | LB-VOP-01 | Legitimate Interests / Vital Ops |
| DIR-SEC-001 | Access Control Logging | LB-LEG-01 | Legal Obligation (Audit Trail) |

## 4.4 Verification Produces Evidence

Verification is the active process of testing, inspecting, or validating a control to determine its operational effectiveness. Unlike attestation, which is a declaration of state, verification is an investigative process. The axiom `verification_produces_evidence` defines this process (bfo:Process) as one that generates an output artifact (the *Y* class). This output is the evidence required by the attestation layer described in Section 4.1.

This concept is critical for maintaining the "audit trail" over time. A control may be designed correctly, but without regular verification, its continued efficacy is unknown. The verification process—whether it is an automated script checking for open ports or a manual review of user logs—must produce a tangible output. This output is then linked to the GDPR lawful basis, closing the loop between technical verification and legal compliance.

For example, a verification process might involve running a monthly vulnerability scan. The scan itself is the process; the resulting PDF report is the evidence produced. This report is then used to support the attestation that "systems are patched against known vulnerabilities." The density of this relationship requires that every verification event be timestamped and cataloged with a unique identifier referencing the lawful basis it supports.

## 4.5 Control Addresses Threat Class

Security controls are implemented to mitigate specific risks. The axiom `control_only_addresses_threat_class` formalizes this by defining a control (cco:DirectiveICE) as an entity that addresses a risk (*Y* class) exclusively. This "only" restriction (using the `only` constructor in OWL) implies that if a control is present, it must be associated with a threat class, and conversely, the control's scope is limited to the threats it is designed to address.

This specificity prevents "scope creep" in compliance mappings. A firewall designed to block unauthorized network traffic (the threat class) cannot be claimed to satisfy a requirement for "data encryption at rest" (a different threat class). The relational schema enforces this by linking the control to a threat class entry, which in turn references the GDPR lawful basis.

The circular dependency here is intentional: the lawful basis requires a control, and the control addresses a threat that justifies the lawful basis. This creates a robust chain of custody for compliance logic. When an auditor reviews a control, they can trace it up to the threat it mitigates and down to the legal basis it satisfies.

## 4.6 Control with Evidence Requirement

A control that cannot be proven to be operational is effectively non-existent in a compliance context. The final axiom, `control_with_evidence_requirement`, stipulates that a control (cco:DirectiveICE) must require evidence (*Y* class) of its execution. This moves beyond the design of the control to its ongoing operation and maintenance.

This requirement dictates that for every control implemented, there must be a corresponding evidence-gathering mechanism. If a control requires "monthly review," the evidence requirement is the "sign-off sheet" or "review log." If the control is "automatic failover," the evidence requirement is the "system failover logs." This axiom ensures that the control is not just a static configuration but a living part of the governance framework.

The integration of this concept with the previous sections completes the audit lifecycle. The control addresses a threat (Section 4.5), its operation is verified producing evidence (Section 4.4), that evidence supports a lawful basis (Section 4.3), and the entire package is summarized in an attestation (Section 4.1).
