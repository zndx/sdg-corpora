# Chapter 7: Formal Ontology of PCI DSS Governance and Audit Evidence

This chapter establishes the formal ontological framework governing the definition, implementation, and verification of Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) controls within an enterprise information environment. It delineates the structural relationships between regulatory directives, personnel attestations, audit traceability, and threat mitigation strategies. The discourse herein is anchored in the rigorous application of the Web Ontology Language (OWL) to ensure semantic interoperability between compliance artifacts and technical governance frameworks. By mapping these abstract axioms to concrete relational schemas, auditors and governance officers may derive deterministic evidence of compliance posture.

## 7.1 PCI DSS Requirements as Directive Information Content Entities

The foundational unit of governance within the PCI DSS framework is the requirement itself, which is ontologically modeled as a subclass of the *Directive Information Content Entity* (ICE). A Directive ICE represents a prescriptive information artifact that mandates specific action or configuration. In the context of the axiom `pci_dss_requirement`, we define a class hierarchy wherein a specific directive (X) is semantically bound to a requirement category (Y). This relationship asserts that the directive is not merely a suggestion but a formal instantiation of a PCI DSS requirement.

For a governance framework to be operationally effective, these directives must be cataloged with high precision. The verbalization "{X} is something that pci dss requirement {Y}" implies that every governance object within the system must be traceable to a specific article of the compliance standard. This mapping prevents the abstraction of requirements into vague policy statements, ensuring that every directive is anchored to a specific regulatory control point.

## 7.2 Attestation Processes and Personnel Authority

Governance frameworks rely heavily on the concept of attestation—the formal act of signing a document to validate its accuracy or execution. Ontologically, attestation is modeled as a process (specifically, a subclass of `bfo:0000015`, denoting a processual entity) that necessitates the involvement of a responsible agent. The axiom `attestation_signed_by` formalizes this by asserting that an attestation process (X) must be signed by a person (`cco:Person`).

This distinction is critical for audit trails. It separates the mechanical process of generating an attestation report from the legal and ethical responsibility of the human agent validating it. In regulatory terms, an automated script generating a compliance report is insufficient without the corresponding "signed by" relationship that establishes accountability. The axiom ensures that the process is not orphaned; it is inextricably linked to a human actor with the authority to certify the state of compliance.

The relational schema for this axiom focuses on the process entity itself. ## 7.3 Audit Traces and Claim Support

The validity of any compliance claim rests upon the existence of supporting evidence. In this ontology, evidence is formalized through the axiom `trace_supports_claim`. Here, a trace (X)—defined as a process occurring over time—acts as the foundational support for a *Descriptive Information Content Entity* (ICE). A Descriptive ICE represents a proposition, statement, or claim about the state of the world (e.g., "The firewall is configured correctly").

The verbalization "{X} is something that supports claim descriptive information content entity" establishes a directional flow of evidence. The trace (the log, the audit record, the system output) provides the necessary support to substantiate the descriptive claim (the compliance assertion). Without this linkage, compliance claims are merely unsubstantiated assertions. This axiom is the semantic backbone of audit readiness, ensuring that every declarative statement in a report is backed by a processual artifact.

## 7.4 Requirement Equivalence: Specification and Application

To achieve a granular level of governance, requirements must be decomposed into their constituent specifications and targets. The axiom `requirement_equiv_specifies_target` provides a complex definition: a requirement (X) is equivalent to a Directive ICE that *specifies* some artifact (Y) and *applies to* some entity (Z). This tripartite structure allows the ontology to distinguish between the rule itself, the specific control mechanism being invoked, and the system component subject to that control.

For example, a PCI DSS requirement regarding encryption is not a monolithic concept; it is a directive that specifies an encryption protocol (Y) and applies to a specific data store (Z). This equivalence axiom enables precise querying, such as "Show me all directives that apply to the Customer Database." It transforms high-level regulatory text into machine-readable logic that can be enforced by configuration management databases.

## 7.5 Control Implementation and Threat Mitigation

The ultimate purpose of a governance framework is risk mitigation. The axiom `control_only_addresses_threat_class` formalizes the deployment of controls. It asserts that a control (X), which is a subclass of Directive ICE, addresses a risk (Y) restricted to a specific threat class. The use of the "only" (`only`) restriction in the Manchester syntax is semantically significant; it implies that if the control is active, it *must* be addressing a threat of type Y, and conversely, it cannot address threats outside this class.

This constraint prevents scope creep in control implementation. It ensures that a specific control, such as a packet filter, is strictly defined and utilized for the threat class it was designed to mitigate (e.g., unauthorized network ingress). By anchoring the control to a threat class, the ontology facilitates risk-based auditing, allowing stakeholders to verify that the deployed controls are sufficient to cover the identified threat landscape.
