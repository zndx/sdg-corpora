# Governance Frameworks: Compliance, Attestation, and Risk Control in PCI Environments

## 1.0 Preamble and Scope

This chapter delineates the structural and semantic underpinnings of compliance governance within the context of the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS). The objective is to provide a rigorous, formalized mapping between abstract ontology axioms and the concrete relational data structures employed in audit and reporting mechanisms. The discussion herein is restricted to the formal definition of requirements, the processes of attestation and traceability, and the implementation of controls addressing specific threat classes. Readers are assumed to be familiar with the foundational principles of information security governance and the logical modeling of regulatory frameworks.

## 2.0 PCI DSS Requirements and Directive Structures

### 2.1 Formal Definition of Requirements

In the ontology of compliance governance, a PCI DSS requirement is not merely a textual statement but a distinct information entity with specific logical properties. Formally, we define a requirement as a subclass of a Directive Information Content Entity (ICE). This classification asserts that the requirement carries prescriptive weight—it directs action or mandates a state of affairs. Furthermore, the axiom establishes that such a requirement must necessarily satisfy a `pciDssRequirement` relationship with another class. This relationship implies that the requirement is an instantiation or specific realization of a broader compliance category or specification.

The verbalization of this axiom posits that "{X} is something that pci dss requirement {Y}". In practical terms, this means that for every specific compliance mandate (X), there exists a defined specification or class (Y) which it fulfills or instantiates within the PCI DSS framework. This structure allows auditors to trace specific control implementations back to their abstract regulatory definitions.

### 2.2 Relational Mapping of Requirements

To implement this axiom within a relational database schema, we utilize the `t_pci_dss_requirement` table. This table captures the instantiation of specific requirements (X) and their association to the broader specification classes (Y). The primary key ensures unique identification of each requirement instance, while the foreign key constraint on column `y` enforces referential integrity to the equivalence table defined in subsequent sections. This structure prevents orphan requirements and ensures that every mandate is anchored to a valid specification target.

The following table presents instances of PCI DSS requirements mapped to their respective equivalence classes. Note that the `y` column references identifiers found in the equivalence table, establishing the necessary dependency chain.

## 3.0 Requirement Equivalence and Target Specification

### 3.1 The Equivalence Axiom

The governance framework further refines the definition of a requirement through an equivalence axiom. This axiom states that a requirement is equivalent to the intersection of three conditions: it is a Directive ICE, it specifies a particular target (Y), and it applies to a specific object or scope (Z). This tripartite definition ensures that a requirement is not defined in a vacuum; it must have a subject (what it specifies), a scope (what it applies to), and the nature of a directive.

The verbalization of this structure is implicit in the logical conjunction: "{X} EquivalentTo DirectiveICE and (specifies some {Y}) and (appliesTo some {Z})". This rigorous definition prevents ambiguity in compliance reporting. For example, a requirement is not simply "encrypt data"; it is "a directive that specifies encryption protocols and applies to cardholder data".

### 3.2 Relational Mapping of Equivalence

The `t_requirement_equiv_specifies_target` table embodies this axiom. The schema includes columns for the requirement class (X), the specification target (Y), and the application scope (Z). Both `y` and `z` are mandatory (NOT NULL), enforcing the axiom's requirement that these properties must be present for the equivalence to hold. Crucially, the schema includes a foreign key constraint where `y` references the `t_pci_dss_requirement` table. This creates a recursive relationship where the specification target of an equivalence requirement must itself be a valid PCI DSS requirement instance, as defined in Section 2.0.

## 4.0 Attestation and Signatory Authority

### 4.1 The Process of Attestation

Attestation represents the formal validation of compliance activities. Within the ontology, attestation is modeled as a subclass of a process (specifically, `bfo:0000015`, representing a processual entity). The critical characteristic of this process is that it must be signed by a person (`cco:Person`). This axiom introduces the agency of human accountability into the automated governance framework. It posits that "{X} is something that signed by person". Without this linkage to a natural person, the attestation is merely a system event and lacks legal or regulatory standing.

This distinction is vital in audit contexts. An automated scan may generate a finding, but it is not an attestation until a qualified individual (e.g., a QSA or internal auditor) executes the signing process. The ontology captures this by linking the attestation class directly to the agent performing the action.

### 4.2 Relational Mapping of Attestation

The `t_attestation_signed_by` table provides the relational structure for tracking these processes. The schema is streamlined, consisting of an identifier and the class of the attestation process (X). While the ontology axiom references the `cco:Person` class, the relational schema provided focuses on the attestation entity itself (`x`). In a full implementation, the linkage to the specific person would be resolved through secondary junction tables or application logic; however, this table serves as the registry of attestation events that require such signatory validation.

## 5.0 Audit Trails and Claim Support

### 5.1 Traceability of Evidence

A fundamental component of any compliance regime is the ability to support descriptive claims with evidentiary traces. The axiom governing this concept defines a class (X) as a subclass of a process that supports a claim, where the claim is a Descriptive Information Content Entity (ICE). The verbalization, "{X} is something that supports claim descriptive information content entity," highlights the function of the trace: it provides the factual basis for a statement of compliance or state.

For instance, if a claim asserts that "all systems are patched," the trace supporting this claim might be a log file generated during a patch management cycle. The ontology formalizes the relationship between the evidentiary artifact (the process generating the trace) and the descriptive assertion (the claim).

### 5.2 Relational Mapping of Traces

The `t_trace_supports_claim` table catalogs these evidentiary processes. The schema records the identifier of the trace and the class of the process (X) that generates or holds the evidence. By treating the trace as a process, the model acknowledges that evidence is often the result of an activity (logging, monitoring, sampling) rather than a static object.

## 6.0 Control Implementation and Threat Mitigation

### 6.1 Addressing Risk Classes

The final concept addressed in this chapter pertains to the operationalization of controls. A control is defined as a subclass of a Directive ICE that addresses a specific risk class. The axiom uses the "only" restriction (`sdg:addressesRisk only {Y:Class}`), which implies that the control {X} is restricted to addressing the risk {Y} and no other type of risk in this context. The verbalization is "{X} is something that addresses risk {Y}".

This restriction is critical for the precision of the governance framework. It ensures that controls are not vaguely defined but are tightly coupled to the specific threat vectors they are designed to mitigate. For example, a firewall control addresses the risk of "Unauthorized Network Access." It does not, in this specific axiomatic context, address the risk of "Physical Theft."

### 6.2 Relational Mapping of Controls and Risks

The `t_control_only_addresses_threat_class` table implements this relationship. It maps a specific control class (X) to the threat class (Y) it addresses. The schema enforces a foreign key constraint linking the threat class (`y`) back to the `t_pci_dss_requirement` table. This linkage suggests that the definition of the threat or risk is formally codified within the requirement structure established in Section 2.0, ensuring that controls are addressing recognized, standardized risks rather than ad-hoc concerns.
