# Governance Frameworks for PCI DSS: Ontological Formalization and Compliance Artifacts

This chapter establishes the formal ontological definitions for PCI DSS governance artifacts, focusing on the structural relationships between directives, requirements, and enforcement mechanisms. It provides a rigorous examination of the axioms governing Requirement Equivalence, PCI DSS Requirement Classification, Policy Jurisdiction, Attestation Processes, and Policy Enforcement. The content is designed to support auditors and compliance officers in mapping regulatory mandates to executable system controls.

## 1. Requirement Equivalence and Target Specification

The foundational axiom of the governance framework defines the equivalence class for a Requirement. In this context, a Requirement is not merely a textual statement but a formalized Directive Information Content Entity (ICE) that must satisfy three necessary and sufficient conditions: it must specify a PCI DSS requirement, it must apply to a specific target, and it must exist within the domain of directives. This structure ensures that every compliance obligation is traceable to both a regulatory source and a tangible system component.

Formally, the axiom is expressed as: Class: {X:Class} EquivalentTo: cco:DirectiveICE and (sdg:specifies some {Y:Class}) and (sdg:appliesTo some {Z:Class}). This definition prevents ambiguity in compliance scoping by mandating that a directive cannot exist in isolation; it must be anchored to a specific requirement class and a target class.

**1.1 Implementation and Compliance Mapping**

When implementing this axiom, administrators must verify that the directive explicitly references the requirement ID and the target infrastructure. Failure to maintain this tripartite linkage results in non-compliance with the governance framework. The following table illustrates the instantiation of this axiom, demonstrating how specific directives map to PCI DSS requirements and their respective targets.

The table below embodies Axiom 1 (`requirement_equiv_specifies_target`). The primary key is the `id` of the requirement equivalent class. The foreign key `y` references the specific PCI DSS requirement class defined in Axiom 2, establishing a dependency between the governance directive and the regulatory standard.

| id | x | y | z |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| REQ-001 | TransmissionEncryptionDirective | PCI-001 | PublicNetwork |

**Action:** Ensure that all governance directives are cataloged with the corresponding `y` and `z` values as shown in the registry above. Any directive lacking a specified target or PCI reference must be flagged for review.

## 2. PCI DSS Requirement Classification

Building upon the general definition of a requirement, this section details the specific classification of PCI DSS requirements. The axiom asserts that a PCI DSS Requirement is a subclass of the Directive ICE and possesses the property of being a PCI DSS requirement of a specific class. This creates a recursive relationship where the specific requirement class is defined by its relationship to the broader requirement equivalence defined in the previous section.

The Manchester syntax for this axiom is: Class: {X:Class} SubClassOf: cco:DirectiveICE, sdg:pciDssRequirement some {Y:Class}. In natural language, {X} is something that pci dss requirement {Y}. This classification is critical for audit trails, as it segregates general organizational policies from those specifically mandated by the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard.

**2.1 Regulatory Hierarchy and Dependency**

The dependency structure here is significant. The classification of a PCI DSS requirement relies on the existence of a Requirement Equivalent (Axiom 1). This circular dependency enforces integrity: a PCI requirement cannot exist without a directive that specifies it, and a directive cannot be valid unless it specifies a PCI requirement.

The following table represents the `t_pci_dss_requirement` schema. The primary key is `id`, representing the unique identifier for the PCI requirement class. The column `y` acts as a foreign key referencing the `t_requirement_equiv_specifies_target` table, specifically linking the regulatory requirement back to the directive that enforces it.

| id | x | y |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| PCI-001 | DataEncryptionStandard | REQ-001 |
| PCI-002 | AccessControlMechanism | REQ-002 |
| PCI-003 | VulnerabilityManagementProgram | REQ-003 |
| PCI-004 | SecureNetworkArchitecture | REQ-004 |

**Action:** Validate that for every entry in the PCI DSS requirement register, there is a corresponding entry in the Requirement Equivalent table. Discrepancies indicate orphaned policies or unregulated directives.

## 3. Policy Jurisdiction and Scope Definition

A Policy, within this ontology, is defined as a Directive ICE that exercises jurisdiction over a specific requirement and governs a particular scope. The axiom is formalized as: Class: {X:Class} EquivalentTo: cco:DirectiveICE and (sdg:hasJurisdiction some {Y:Class}) and (sdg:governsScope some {Z:Class}). This distinction is vital for multi-jurisdictional organizations where a policy may apply to specific legal frameworks or operational units.

The "Jurisdiction" slot (`y`) is constrained by the schema to reference a PCI DSS Requirement. This implies that the policy's authority is derived directly from its mandate to enforce a specific PCI requirement. The "Scope" (`z`) defines the boundary of this enforcement, such as the Cardholder Data Environment (CDE) or specific corporate directories.

**3.1 Scope Delineation and Enforcement**

Improper scoping is a common cause of audit failure. If a policy governing scope `z` does not correctly align with the jurisdiction `y`, the policy may be deemed unenforceable. Administrators must ensure that the scope is neither too broad (impeding operations) nor too narrow (failing to protect data).

The table below embodies Axiom 3 (`policy_equiv_jurisdiction_and_scope`). The primary key is `id`. The foreign key `y` references the `t_pci_dss_requirement` table, linking the policy's jurisdictional authority to the specific PCI requirement it upholds.

| id | x | y | z |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| POL-J-001 | CorporateEncryptionPolicy | PCI-001 | CardholderDataEnvironment |
| POL-J-002 | IdentityManagementPolicy | PCI-002 | CorporateDirectory |
| POL-J-003 | SystemHardeningPolicy | PCI-003 | ProductionServers |

**Action:** Review the `z` column for all policies. Ensure that the scope description matches the technical segmentation defined in the network architecture diagrams.

## 4. Attestation and Signatory Authority

Attestation is the process by which a qualified individual validates the implementation of controls. Ontologically, an Attestation is a subclass of a `bfo:Process` (Process) that is signed by a Person. The axiom is: Class: {X:Class} SubClassOf: bfo:0000015, sdg:signedBy some cco:Person. This elevates attestation from a administrative task to a formal process event with a designated responsible agent.

The verbalization "{X} is something that signed by person" emphasizes the agency involved. In the context of PCI DSS, this usually refers to the Qualified Security Assessor (QSA) or an internal officer signing the Report on Compliance (ROC) or Attestation of Compliance (AOC).

**4.1 Process Integrity and Non-Repudiation**

The integrity of the attestation process relies on the immutable link between the process record and the person. The schema for `t_attestation_signed_by` is straightforward, containing only the `id` and the class `x`. This simplicity reflects the singular focus of the axiom: establishing the existence of the signed process.

The table below embodies Axiom 4 (`attestation_signed_by`). The primary key is `id`, which serves as the unique reference for the attestation event. There are no foreign keys in this specific table, as it defines the existence of the process class itself.

| id | x |
| :--- | :--- |
| ATT-S-001 | QuarterlyComplianceReview |
| ATT-S-002 | AnnualRiskAssessmentAttestation |
| ATT-S-003 | PenetrationTestSignOff |

**Action:** Verify that all entries in the attestation log correspond to a valid personnel record in the HR system. Unsigned or anonymous process entries are invalid.

## 5. Policy Enforcement Mechanisms

The final axiom addresses the operationalization of policy through enforcement. A Policy is defined as a Directive ICE that is enforced by a minimum of one entity. The axiom is: Class: {X:Class} SubClassOf: cco:DirectiveICE, sdg:enforcedBy min 1 {Y:Class}. This ensures that policies are not merely aspirational statements but possess active enforcement mechanisms.

The schema constraint requires that the enforcing agent (`y`) references a PCI DSS Requirement. This structural choice suggests that enforcement is executed *through* the lens of the requirement. In other words, the policy is enforced by the application or adherence to the specific PCI control.

**5.2 Enforcement Validation**

During an audit, the enforcement link is tested by checking if the mechanism described in the PCI requirement is actively monitoring or controlling the policy subject. If the enforcement entity (`y`) is inactive, the policy (`x`) is considered violated.

The table below embodies Axiom 5 (`policy_min_one_enforcer`). The primary key is `id`, identifying the specific policy enforcement record. The foreign key `y` references the `t_pci_dss_requirement` table, indicating which specific PCI requirement acts as the enforcement agent for the policy.

| id | x | y |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| POL-E-001 | KeyManagementPolicy | PCI-001 |
| POL-E-002 | PrivilegedAccessPolicy | PCI-002 |
| POL-E-003 | PatchManagementPolicy | PCI-003 |

**Action:** Conduct a gap analysis between the policies listed in column `x` and the technical controls implemented for the requirements in column `y`. If a policy exists without an active enforcing requirement, the policy must be withdrawn or technical controls must be deployed.
