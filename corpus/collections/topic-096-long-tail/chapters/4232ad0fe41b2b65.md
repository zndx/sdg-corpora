# Governance Frameworks and PCI DSS Compliance Directives

This chapter delineates the structural and functional components of governance frameworks as they pertain to Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) compliance. It provides a rigorous examination of the ontological relationships between compliance directives, requirement specifications, jurisdictional scopes, and enforcement mechanisms. The definitions herein are grounded in formal axioms and are intended to serve as a reference for auditors, compliance officers, and governance professionals implementing internal controls.

## 1. Requirement Specification and Targeting

In the context of information security governance, a directive is rarely a monolithic entity; rather, it is a composite construct defined by its specific intent and its target of application. The foundational axiom for this concept establishes that a Requirement is equivalent to a Directive Information and Content Entity (ICE) that specifies a particular control objective and applies to a specific target environment. This tripartite structure—Directive, Specification, and Target—ensures that governance artifacts are not merely abstract statements of intent but are operationally bound to the assets they protect.

Formally, this relationship is expressed as the equivalence of a Requirement Class to the intersection of a Directive, a specification property, and an application property. This structure prevents ambiguity in compliance mapping. For instance, a directive regarding "encryption" is only valid as a requirement if it specifies "AES-256 encryption" and applies to "Cardholder Data." Without these slots populated, the directive lacks the granularity necessary for audit verification.

## 2. PCI DSS Requirement Classification

Building upon the general structure of requirements, it is necessary to understand the specific classification of directives that fall under the purview of the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS). The governing axiom here posits that a PCI DSS Requirement is a subclass of the broader Directive ICE, characterized specifically by the property of being a PCI DSS requirement. This classification is not merely taxonomic; it confers specific regulatory obligations and validation criteria upon the entity.

The verbalization of this axiom—that "X is something that PCI DSS requirement Y"—highlights the dependency of the specific requirement instance on the general framework. In practice, this means that for a directive to be recognized as a PCI DSS requirement, it must be explicitly mapped to the standard's published control set. This mapping creates a rigid chain of evidence required during a Level 1 On-Site Assessment or a Level 2 Report on Compliance (ROC).

## 3. Jurisdictional Scope and Governance

While technical requirements define the "what" of compliance, jurisdictional axioms define the "where" and "who." A Policy, in this ontological framework, is defined as a Directive ICE that possesses both a jurisdiction and a governed scope. This distinction is vital for multinational organizations that must navigate the complex interplay between global standards like PCI DSS and regional regulations such as GDPR, CCPA, or local data sovereignty laws.

The axiom `policy_equiv_jurisdiction_and_scope` asserts that a policy cannot exist in a vacuum; it must be anchored to a legal or organizational jurisdiction (e.g., the European Union, the State of California, or a specific corporate entity) and must explicitly define the scope of assets or personnel it governs. This formalization allows governance engines to automatically determine applicability: if a system resides in the "EU Jurisdiction," the "EU Data Privacy Policy" is triggered, and its scope is limited to "EU Resident Data."

The relational schema for this concept links the policy directly to the PCI DSS requirement table. This linkage implies that the policy is the instrument by which the technical requirement is enforced within a specific jurisdiction. ## 4. Attestation and Signatory Authority

Compliance is a lifecycle that culminates in attestation. From an ontological perspective, Attestation is a process—a temporal entity defined by the Basic Formal Ontology (BFO) as an occurrent—that is necessarily signed by a person. This axiom distinguishes the *act* of compliance verification from the *state* of being compliant. The attestation is the discrete event where liability is accepted and the accuracy of the compliance report is validated.

The requirement that the attestation be "signed by a Person" (cco:Person) introduces the element of individual accountability. In governance frameworks, this ensures that there is always a natural human party responsible for the veracity of the report, rather than an automated system or a faceless corporate entity. This is consistent with SEC regulations and PCI DSS standards that require sign-off by qualified security assessors (QSAs) or executive officers.

## 5. Enforcement Mechanisms

A directive without enforcement is merely advice. The final axiom covered in this chapter addresses the necessity of enforcement. A Policy is defined as a Directive ICE that is enforced by a minimum of one entity. This "min 1" quantifier ensures that there are no orphaned policies within the governance framework; every policy must have an assigned agent or body responsible for its execution and monitoring.

This axiom bridges the gap between governance documentation and operational reality. The enforcer can be a specific role (e.g., Chief Information Security Officer), a committee (e.g., Audit Committee), or an automated system (e.g., SIEM alerting logic). By linking the policy to the enforcer, the ontology facilitates the automated routing of compliance tasks and the escalation of violations.

The relational schema provided for this axiom links the policy to the PCI DSS requirement table.
