# Chapter 4: Governance Frameworks and Directive Compliance Structures

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the formal ontology governing compliance directives, specifically within the context of Payment Card Industry Data Security Standards (PCI DSS) and broader information security governance. It establishes the structural definitions for Requirements, Policies, and Attestations, mapping these conceptual entities to their logical relational schemas. The objective is to provide a rigorous, evidence-anchored framework for audit professionals to verify that directive information content entities (ICE) are correctly classified, specified, and enforced within enterprise systems. Administrators are advised to ensure that local data models align with the primary key and foreign key structures defined herein to maintain referential integrity across compliance repositories.

## 4.2 Directive Requirements and Specification Targets

The foundational unit of governance is the Requirement, formally defined as a subclass of the Directive Information Content Entity (`cco:DirectiveICE`). In compliance ontologies, a Requirement is not merely a static text string but a complex equivalence class that asserts specific control objectives. According to the governing axiom, a Requirement (`X`) is equivalent to a Directive that specifies a particular control mechanism (`Y`) and applies to a specific target scope (`Z`). This tripartite structure ensures that every mandate is actionable and bounded.

The relational projection of this axiom creates a normalized structure where the requirement itself is linked to the specific PCI DSS requirement it fulfills. This linkage is critical for audit trails, as it allows governance officers to trace a high-level organizational requirement down to the specific regulatory control it addresses.

The following table embodies the `requirement_equiv_specifies_target` axiom. It illustrates the mapping where a general governance requirement (Column `x`) specifies a specific PCI DSS control (Column `y`) and applies to a defined environment (Column `z`). The primary key is the unique identifier for the requirement, while the foreign key enforces the relationship to the specific PCI control definition.

| id | x | y | z |
|---|---|---|---|
| REQ-ADM-01 | AccessControlRequirement | PCI-8.1.1 | CardholderDataEnvironment |
| REQ-ADM-02 | TransmissionEncryptionReq | PCI-4.1 | PublicNetwork |
| REQ-ADM-03 | MalwareProtectionReq | PCI-5.1 | EndUserWorkstation |
| REQ-ADM-04 | LoggingMechanismReq | PCI-10.2.1 | SystemAuditTrail |
| REQ-ADM-05 | PhysicalAccessReq | PCI-9.1 | ServerRoom |

### 4.2.1 Implementation Guidelines
When populating the `t_requirement_equiv_specifies_target` table, practitioners must ensure that the `y` column references a valid, existing PCI DSS requirement identifier. The `z` column must utilize standardized controlled vocabulary for target scopes to facilitate automated querying. Failure to maintain the foreign key constraint between `y` and the PCI requirement table will result in orphaned requirements that cannot be validated against the standard.

## 4.3 PCI DSS Requirement Subclasses

While the previous section defines the general structure of a requirement, this section addresses the specific classification of PCI DSS requirements. The axiom `pci_dss_requirement` posits that a specific class (`X`) is a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` and possesses a `pciDssRequirement` relationship with another class (`Y`). This structure allows the ontology to model the hierarchical nature of compliance, where a specific technical control (the subclass) fulfills a broader mandate.

This relationship is reciprocal and circular in the relational schema to enforce strict validation: a PCI Requirement entry must point back to a General Requirement entry, which in turn must point to the PCI Requirement. This circular dependency ensures that there are no "dangling" PCI references without a corresponding organizational requirement, and no organizational requirements without a grounding in the specific PCI text.

The table below represents the `pci_dss_requirement` axiom. It lists specific PCI DSS control classes (Column `x`) and links them back to the general organizational requirement identifiers (Column `y`) established in the previous section. The primary key is the PCI requirement ID, and the foreign key validates the link to the general requirement table.

| id | x | y |
|---|---|---|
| PCI-8.1.1 | TwoFactorAuthenticationControl | REQ-ADM-01 |
| PCI-4.1 | SecureTransmissionProtocol | REQ-ADM-02 |
| PCI-5.1 | AntiMalwareSolution | REQ-ADM-03 |
| PCI-10.2.1 | AuditLogRecord | REQ-ADM-04 |
| PCI-9.1 | PhysicalEntryControl | REQ-ADM-05 |

### 4.3.1 Audit Verification
Auditors should verify that for every entry in `t_pci_dss_requirement`, the `y` value exists as a primary key in `t_requirement_equiv_specifies_target`. Furthermore, the `x` value must correspond to the official nomenclature used in the PCI DSS standard (e.g., "TwoFactorAuthenticationControl"). Discrepancies in naming conventions often indicate manual data entry errors and require remediation.

## 4.4 Policy Jurisdiction and Governance Scope

Policies differ from requirements in that they encompass a broader set of rules governed by a specific legal or organizational jurisdiction and applied to a comprehensive scope. The axiom `policy_equiv_jurisdiction_and_scope` defines a Policy (`X`) as a Directive that has a Jurisdiction (`Y`) and governs a Scope (`Z`). This distinction is vital for multinational organizations where a single policy may apply to multiple jurisdictions but must be scoped differently based on regional regulations.

In the relational model, the Jurisdiction slot (`y`) is structurally linked to the PCI DSS requirement table. This implies that the jurisdiction's authority is derived from or enforced by specific compliance requirements. For instance, the jurisdiction of "EU Data Protection" may be operationally defined by its adherence to specific PCI requirements regarding data privacy and encryption.

The following table encodes the `policy_equiv_jurisdiction_and_scope` axiom. It defines policies (Column `x`), the jurisdiction or regulatory body enforcing them (Column `y`, mapped to a PCI requirement ID), and the scope of governance (Column `z`). The primary key is the Policy ID.

| id | x | y | z |
|---|---|---|---|
| POL-SEC-01 | CorporateInformationSecurityPolicy | PCI-8.1.1 | AllEmployees |
| POL-SEC-02 | DataTransmissionStandard | PCI-4.1 | ExternalNetworks |
| POL-SEC-03 | EndpointProtectionStandard | PCI-5.1 | CorporateDevices |
| POL-SEC-04 | AuditLogRetentionPolicy | PCI-10.2.1 | CentralLogServer |
| POL-SEC-05 | SiteAccessControlPolicy | PCI-9.1 | DataCenters |

### 4.4.1 Scope Definition
The `z` column (Scope) must be defined with sufficient granularity to allow for automated policy mapping. Vague scopes such as "Everywhere" or "All Systems" are discouraged unless they literally apply to the entire enterprise ecosystem. In the table above, scopes like "CorporateDevices" or "ExternalNetworks" provide the necessary specificity for automated compliance engines.

## 4.5 Attestation Processes and Signatory Authority

Attestation is the process by which a qualified individual validates that the directives have been adhered to. The axiom `attestation_signed_by` classifies an Attestation (`X`) as a subclass of a Process (`bfo:0000015`) that is signed by a Person (`cco:Person`). This transforms the attestation from a static document into a temporal event with a responsible agent.

The relational schema for this axiom is straightforward, focusing on the identity of the attestation event. While the ontology links the attestation to a Person, the relational table provided focuses on the attestation entity itself. In practice, the `x` value serves as the reference key for the digital signature or the physical log entry where the signatory's credentials are recorded.

The table below illustrates the `attestation_signed_by` axiom. It lists unique attestation events (Column `x`) identified by their transaction IDs.

| id | x |
|---|---|
| ATT-2023-Q1 | Q1_Compliance_Attestation |
| ATT-2023-Q2 | Q2_Compliance_Attestation |
| ATT-2023-Q3 | Q3_Compliance_Attestation |
| ATT-2023-Q4 | Q4_Compliance_Attestation |
| ATT-2023-AD | Annual_Discovery_Attestation |

### 4.5.1 Validation Protocol
To validate the integrity of the attestation records, the system must cross-reference the `id` in this table with the signature logs maintained by the Human Resources or Identity Management system. The absence of a valid signatory timestamp for any `id` listed here constitutes a critical control failure.

## 4.6 Policy Enforcement Mechanisms

A policy without enforcement is merely advisory. The axiom `policy_min_one_enforcer` stipulates that a Policy (`X`) is a Directive that is enforced by a minimum of one Enforcer (`Y`). The "min 1" quantifier ensures that there is no policy without an assigned responsible party or automated control mechanism acting as the enforcer.

In this schema, the Enforcer (`y`) is modeled as a reference to a PCI DSS requirement. This structural choice suggests that the enforcement mechanism is grounded in the specific technical controls defined by the standard. For example, the "CorporateInformationSecurityPolicy" is enforced by the "TwoFactorAuthenticationControl" (PCI-8.1.1); the control acts as the mechanism of enforcement for the policy.

The final table embodies the `policy_min_one_enforcer` axiom. It maps Policies (Column `x`) to their specific enforcing controls (Column `y`), which are identified by PCI requirement IDs. The primary key is the Policy ID.

| id | x | y |
|---|---|---|
| POL-SEC-01 | CorporateInformationSecurityPolicy | PCI-8.1.1 |
| POL-SEC-02 | DataTransmissionStandard | PCI-4.1 |
| POL-SEC-03 | EndpointProtectionStandard | PCI-5.1 |
| POL-SEC-04 | AuditLogRetentionPolicy | PCI-10.2.1 |
| POL-SEC-05 | SiteAccessControlPolicy | PCI-9.1 |

### 4.6.1 Enforcement Verification
Compliance officers must query this table to ensure that every active Policy ID in the governance framework has a corresponding entry in `t_policy_min_one_enforcer`. Furthermore, the `y` value (the Enforcer) must be active and operational. A policy linked to an enforcing control that is currently disabled or failing constitutes a high-priority remediation finding.
