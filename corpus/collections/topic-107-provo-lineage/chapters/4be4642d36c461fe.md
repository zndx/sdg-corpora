# Chapter 4: Governance Frameworks and the Ontology of Assurance

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the structural relationships between governance artifacts, specifically focusing on the generation of evidence, the issuance of directives, and the formal act of attestation. In the context of high-assurance environments, such as financial auditing or regulatory compliance, it is insufficient to rely on unstructured assertions. Instead, a formal ontology must be applied to map the dependencies between processes (e.g., audits), information entities (e.g., directives and evidence), and claims (e.g., compliance statements). The following sections define five core axioms that govern these relationships, providing a deterministic schema for mapping audit trails to regulatory requirements. These concepts are foundational for establishing a verifiable chain of custody and logical provenance for all governance-related information content entities.

## 4.2 Audit Processes and the Production of Evidence

The fundamental unit of governance oversight is the audit process. Within the ontology, an audit is not merely an activity but a distinct class of process that is causally linked to the creation of information artifacts. The axiom **audit_produces_evidence** establishes that any entity classified as an Audit Process is a subprocess that necessarily produces a specific type of output: a Descriptive Information Content Entity (DescriptiveICE).

This relationship is critical for auditability. It posits that the existence of a descriptive record (a log, a finding, or a transaction record) is dependent upon the execution of a process. The process serves as the "bfo:0000015" (process) entity, while the output is the "cco:DescriptiveICE." This ensures that every piece of evidence in the system can be traced back to a specific generating event or workflow, satisfying the requirements of non-repudiation.

Without this classification, any output it generates would be orphaned from the process hierarchy, rendering it inadmissible in formal compliance reporting.

## 4.3 Directives: Authorship and Application

Governance is operationalized through Directives. A Directive Information Content Entity (DirectiveICE) serves as a prescriptive document—such as a policy, standard, or procedure—that dictates behavior within the system. The axiom **directive_authored_and_applies** provides a tripartite definition for these entities.

For a directive to be valid within the ontology, it must satisfy three conditions:
1.  It must be an instance of the `cco:DirectiveICE` class.
2.  It must bear an `sdg:authoredBy` relationship to a specific author class.
3.  It must bear an `sdg:appliesTo` relationship to a specific target class or scope.

This structure ensures that directives are not floating text; they are grounded in authority (authorship) and scope (application). The ontology enforces a specific constraint regarding authorship: the author (`y`) of a directive must reference an attestation process. This creates a governance loop where prescriptive documents are formally authored or validated by attestation workflows, ensuring that directives are themselves subject to governance controls.

This granular mapping allows automated governance tools to verify that a specific asset class is governed by a valid, authored directive.

## 4.4 Basic Evidence Classification

To understand the outputs of the processes described above, one must define the nature of the information itself. The axiom **evidence_basic** classifies the fundamental unit of audit data: the Descriptive Information Content Entity (DescriptiveICE).

Unlike a Directive, which is prescriptive, a DescriptiveICE is representational. It describes a state of affairs, a historical event, or a data point without issuing commands. Examples include log files, database records, audit reports, and system metrics. The axiom asserts that any entity `x` classified as evidence is a subclass of `cco:DescriptiveICE`. This distinction is vital for separating the "rules" (Directives) from the "facts" (Evidence).

## 4.5 Attestation with Supporting Evidence

The attestation process represents the formal verification of compliance. It is the mechanism by which an entity (internal or external) asserts that a set of controls is operating effectively. The axiom **attestation_with_supporting_evidence** defines the Attestation class as a process that relies on supporting evidence.

Crucially, this axiom introduces a dependency loop with the Directive axiom. An attestation (`x`) must have `sdg:withSupportingEvidence` that points to a Directive (`y`). This implies that an attestation is not merely a self-contained opinion; it is a verification that is substantiated by, or references, specific governing directives. In this ontology, the "evidence" that supports an attestation is the directive itself, effectively stating that the attestation validates adherence to the directive.

This recursive structure—where directives are authored by attestations (Axiom 2) and attestations are supported by directives (Axiom 4)—creates a closed loop of governance. This loop ensures that policies are dynamically validated by the very processes they authorize.

## 4.6 Claims and Evidence Support

Finally, the governance framework must support the assertion of specific claims. A claim is a declarative statement about the state of the system or organization, such as "the network is secure" or "data is encrypted." The axiom **claim_min_one_evidence** defines a Claim as a subclass of `cco:DescriptiveICE` that is supported by at least one piece of evidence.

This axiom enforces the "evidence-anchored" nature of the textbook's style. No claim may exist in the system without a direct link to a supporting evidence artifact. Furthermore, the evidence (`y`) referenced by the claim must be produced by an audit process (referencing `t_audit_produces_evidence`). This creates a traceability chain:
1.  **Audit Process** produces Evidence.
2.  **Claim** is supported by that Evidence.

This structure prevents unfounded assertions. Every compliance claim made in a report must be traceable back to a specific audit-generated artifact.
