# Governance Frameworks: ISO/IEC 27001 Compliance and Evidence Verification

## Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the formal ontological structures underpinning information security management systems (ISMS), specifically focusing on the integration of ISO/IEC 27001 controls, clause requirements, and the evidentiary mechanisms necessary for compliance auditing. The text establishes a rigorous mapping between abstract directive classes and their concrete instantiations within a governance framework. By defining the precise relationships between Annex A controls, organizational requirements, and the processes of verification and attestation, this chapter provides the logical architecture required to automate compliance tracking and evidence generation. The discussion is anchored in the necessity for auditable trails that withstand regulatory scrutiny, ensuring that every directive is traceable to a specific target and supported by verifiable proof.

## 1. ISO 27001 Annex A Controls as Directives

The foundational layer of the ISMS governance model is the mapping of organizational directives to the specific controls enumerated in ISO/IEC 27001 Annex A. In this ontology, we define a class `X` as a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` (Information Control Element) that satisfies the condition of being an ISO 27001 Annex A control `Y`. This axiom, `iso27001_annexa_subclass`, formalizes the relationship between a generic governance directive and the standardized control objective it fulfills. For instance, an organizational policy regarding password management is not merely a document; it is a directive instance that realizes the Annex A control A.9.3 (User authentication).

The significance of this classification lies in its ability to bridge the gap between high-level policy statements and technical compliance standards. When a directive is formally recognized as an Annex A control, it inherits the validation criteria and audit requirements associated with that specific ISO standard. This mapping allows automated governance tools to query the directive not just by name, but by its compliance classification, ensuring that all implementations align with the strictures of the international standard. The relationship is existential: for a directive to be valid within this schema, it must be associated with a recognized Annex A control class.

## 2. ISO 27001 Clause Requirements

While Annex A provides the detailed control catalog, the ISO/IEC 27001 standard itself is structured through clauses that establish the general requirements for an ISMS. The axiom `iso27001_clause_subclass` defines a class `X` as a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` that satisfies an ISO 27001 Clause `Y`. This distinction is critical for auditors: clauses represent the macro-level obligations (e.g., Clause 6.1.3 regarding legal requirements), whereas Annex A provides the micro-level controls.

In practice, a directive representing the organization's "Legal and Regulatory Compliance Policy" is classified under this axiom as satisfying Clause 6.1.3. This classification ensures that the organization’s governance framework addresses the mandatory requirements of the standard's main body, not just the annex. The ontology treats these clause-based directives with the same rigor as control-based directives, enforcing a structure where high-level compliance mandates are explicitly linked to their originating standard clauses. This facilitates top-down auditing, where compliance with clauses can be verified by examining the existence and implementation of these specific directives.

## 3. Requirement Equivalence and Target Specification

The core of the compliance logic rests on the definition of a Requirement. The axiom `requirement_equiv_specifies_target` provides a complex equivalence definition: a class `X` (Requirement) is equivalent to a `cco:DirectiveICE` that specifies a control (`Y`) and applies to a target (`Z`). This axiom serves as the nexus point of the ontology, binding the directive to the control (via Annex A) and the operational environment (via the Target).

For a requirement to be formally valid, it must possess three dimensions: it must be a directive, it must specify a control mechanism (the "what"), and it must apply to a tangible asset or system (the "where"). For example, a requirement "Encrypt Customer Database" is a directive that specifies the "Encryption Control" (Annex A) and applies to the "Customer Database" (Target). This tripartite structure eliminates ambiguity in compliance statements. It prevents vague directives such as "ensure security" by forcing the explicit definition of the control used and the asset protected. This granular definition is essential for automated compliance engines to generate accurate scoping reports and evidence collection tasks.

## 4. Verification Processes and Evidence Production

Compliance is not static; it must be verified through active processes. The axiom `verification_produces_evidence` defines a class `X` (Verification) as a subclass of `bfo:0000015` (Process) that produces evidence `Y`. In this context, verification activities—such as automated vulnerability scans, manual log reviews, or penetration tests—are formal processes whose output is not merely a report, but a distinct entity classed as Evidence.

This distinction is vital for audit trails. The ontology separates the *act* of verification from the *artifact* produced. When an auditor reviews compliance, they examine the Evidence (`Y`) generated by the Verification (`X`). By linking the Verification process to a specific Annex A control (via the Evidence), the framework ensures that all evidence is purposeful and traceable. For instance, a "Quarterly Access Review" (Process) produces an "Access Review Report" (Evidence). This report serves as the proof that the "Access Control" directive is being enforced. The formalization of this relationship allows the system to automatically flag missing evidence if the verification process has not been executed within the required timeframe.

## 5. Attestation with Supporting Evidence

The final step in the compliance lifecycle is attestation. The axiom `attestation_with_supporting_evidence` defines a class `X` (Attestation) as a subclass of `bfo:0000015` (Process) that possesses supporting evidence `Y`. Attestation represents the formal assertion by an authorized party (e.g., a CISO, Auditor, or Compliance Officer) that the controls are operating effectively. Unlike verification, which is the act of checking, attestation is the act of certifying.

This axiom requires that an attestation cannot exist in a vacuum; it must be supported by evidence. An "Annual ISO Certification Attestation" (Process) must be supported by the aggregate of evidence gathered throughout the year (e.g., scan reports, review logs). By modeling this as a process dependency, the ontology enforces the principle that sign-offs are only valid when backed by data. This prevents "rubber-stamping" and ensures that governance artifacts are rooted in reality. The linkage to the Annex A controls via the evidence ensures that the attestation covers the specific scope of the ISMS under review.
