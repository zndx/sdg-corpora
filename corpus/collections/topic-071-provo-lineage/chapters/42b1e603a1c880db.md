# Chapter 4: Formalization of ISO/IEC 27001 Governance Structures

## Scope and Preamble

This chapter establishes the formal ontological framework required to map the abstract directives of the ISO/IEC 27001 standard to tangible governance artifacts. It delineates the structural relationships between high-level policy clauses, specific control requirements derived from Annex A, and the evidentiary mechanisms used to verify compliance. The objective is to provide a rigorous, data-centric schema that supports automated auditing and continuous compliance monitoring, ensuring that every governance action is traceable to a specific regulatory axiom and verifiable through structured evidence.

## 4.1 ISO 27001 Annex A Subclassifications

The implementation of an Information Security Management System (ISMS) relies heavily on the precise categorization of controls as enumerated in Annex A of ISO/IEC 27001. Within the ontology, the concept of an Annex A subclass is defined by the axiom that a specific directive ({X}) is a subclass of the general `cco:DirectiveICE` and satisfies the condition `sdg:iso27001AnnexA` some {Y}. This formalization asserts that for a directive to be compliant with the standard, it must be explicitly typed as a realization of a specific Annex A control objective.

In practice, this means that organizational policies—such as an Access Control Policy or a Cryptography Policy—are not merely generic documents but are instances of classes that inherit properties from the standard's control set. The mapping is deterministic: the directive {X} "is something that iso27001 annex a {Y}". This relationship allows governance frameworks to query the system for all directives relevant to a specific control, ensuring that the scope of the audit covers all mandated artifacts.

Table 4.1 illustrates this mapping, demonstrating how internal governance directives are formally subsumed by the standard's control categories.

**Table 4.1: Mapping of Organizational Directives to ISO 27001 Annex A Controls**

| ID | Directive Class (X) | Annex A Control Class (Y) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| dir_ac_01 | AccessControlPolicyDirective | AnnexA_9_AccessControl |
| dir_crypto_01 | CryptographicKeyManagementDirective | AnnexA_10_Cryptography |
| dir_ops_01 | SecureDisposalPolicyDirective | AnnexA_12_OperationsSecurity |
| dir_hr_01 | ScreeningPolicyDirective | AnnexA_7_HumanResourcesSecurity |
| dir_phy_01 | PhysicalEntryDirective | AnnexA_11_PhysicalSecurity |

The primary key for this dataset is the unique directive identifier (`id`). The foreign key constraint on the `y` column ensures that the Annex A Control referenced is a valid, defined target within the broader requirements schema, preventing the association of directives with non-existent or deprecated control sets.

## 4.2 Requirement Equivalence: Specification and Targeting

While directives provide the governance layer, operational compliance is achieved through specific requirements that bridge the gap between policy and execution. The ontology defines a Requirement Class ({X}) as equivalent to the intersection of a `cco:DirectiveICE`, a specification of an Annex A control ({Y}), and an application to a specific target ({Z}). This axiom captures the tripartite nature of a compliance requirement: it is a rule that *specifies* a control, *applies to* a tangible asset or role, and functions as an executable directive.

This structure is critical for granular governance. It allows an organization to assert that a "Multi-Factor Authentication Requirement" is not just a general rule but a specific directive that applies the "Annex A.9 Access Control" control specifically to the "Remote Access Gateway" target. By formalizing the target ({Z}), the system enables precise scoping of audits, ensuring that evidence gathered is relevant to the specific asset or process under review.

Table 4.2 presents instances of this equivalence, showing how abstract controls are instantiated as requirements targeting specific organizational entities.

**Table 4.2: Requirement Equivalence Specifications**

| ID | Requirement Class (X) | Specifies Control (Y) | Applies to Target (Z) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| req_mfa_01 | MFAEnforcementRequirement | dir_ac_01 | RemoteAccessGateway |
| req_key_01 | AES256EncryptionRequirement | dir_crypto_01 | CustomerDatabase |
| req_disposal_01 | HardDriveShreddingRequirement | dir_ops_01 | EndOfLifeHardware |
| req_screen_01 | BackgroundCheckRequirement | dir_hr_01 | PrivilegedRoleCandidate |
| req_badge_01 | BiometricBadgeRequirement | dir_phy_01 | DataCenterEntrance |

The relational integrity of this table is maintained by linking the specification column (`y`) to the directive identifiers established in the Annex A subclassification table. This ensures that every operational requirement is traceable to a valid governance policy.

## 4.3 ISO 27001 Clause Subclassifications

The governance hierarchy extends beyond the operational controls of Annex A to the high-level management clauses of the ISO/IEC 27001 standard (e.g., Clauses 4 through 10). These clauses define the organizational context, leadership, planning, and performance evaluation necessary for an effective ISMS. The ontology formalizes this via the axiom that a Clause Directive ({X}) is a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` and satisfies the condition `sdg:iso27001Clause` some {Y}.

In this context, {Y} refers to the specific requirement or set of requirements that the clause governs. For instance, Clause 6.1 (Actions to address risks and opportunities) generates directives that necessitate the creation of risk treatment plans. The axiom verbalizes this as: "{X} is something that iso27001 clause {Y}". This structural definition ensures that high-level management system mandates are explicitly linked to the specific requirements they generate or govern, creating a continuous chain of authority from the standard's clauses down to operational controls.

Table 4.3 demonstrates the linkage between the standard's clauses and the specific requirements they mandate.

**Table 4.3: Clause-to-Requirement Linkages**

| ID | Clause Directive (X) | Governs Requirement (Y) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| clause_6_1_3 | Clause_6_1_3_PlanningDirective | req_mfa_01 |
| clause_8_2 | Clause_8_2_TrainingDirective | req_screen_01 |
| clause_10_1 | Clause_10_1_MonitoringDirective | req_key_01 |
| clause_14_1 | Clause_14_1_ComplianceDirective | req_disposal_01 |
| clause_7_5 | Clause_7_5_DocumentedInfoDirective | req_badge_01 |

The foreign key constraint in this schema connects the clause directive to a valid requirement ID (`y`), ensuring that the clause's authority is anchored to an existing, defined operational need.

## 4.4 Verification Processes and Evidence Generation

Compliance is not static; it must be verified through systematic processes. The ontology defines a Verification Class ({X}) as a subclass of `bfo:0000015` (a Process) that produces evidence ({Y}). This axiom distinguishes the *act* of verification from the *requirement* being verified. It posits that verification is a temporal process that results in the creation of an artifact—specifically, an evidence object linked to an Annex A control.

The verbalization "{X} is something that produces evidence {Y}" emphasizes the output-oriented nature of verification. Whether the process is an automated vulnerability scan, a manual penetration test, or a physical security inspection, the defining characteristic within this ontology is the generation of evidence records. These records serve as the factual basis for attestation and audit trails.

Table 4.4 catalogs verification processes and the specific evidence artifacts they yield, mapped back to the relevant Annex A controls.

**Table 4.4: Verification Processes and Output Evidence**

| ID | Verification Process (X) | Produces Evidence (Y) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| ver_pen_test | AnnualPenetrationTest | dir_ac_01 |
| ver_crypto_audit | KeyRotationAuditLog | dir_crypto_01 |
| ver_disposal_inspect | SanitizationCertificate | dir_ops_01 |
| ver_hr_audit | PersonnelScreeningRecord | dir_hr_01 |
| ver_cctv_review | PhysicalAccessLog | dir_phy_01 |

The schema enforces that the evidence produced (`y`) must be associated with a valid Annex A directive, ensuring that verification activities are always aligned with the control framework.

## 4.5 Attestation with Supporting Evidence

The final step in the governance chain is attestation—the formal declaration that controls are operating effectively. The ontology defines an Attestation Class ({X}) as a subclass of `bfo:0000015` (Process) that possesses supporting evidence ({Y}). This is distinct from verification; while verification *produces* raw evidence, attestation *consumes* and aggregates that evidence to support a formal statement of compliance.

The axiom "{X} is something that with supporting evidence {Y}" implies that an attestation is invalid in the absence of linked evidence. This structure is essential for audit readiness and regulatory reporting, as it prevents "empty" attestations. It forces the governance framework to validate that for every compliance claim (e.g., "We are compliant with A.9"), there exists a corroborating evidence object generated by a verification process.

Table 4.5 details the attestation records, linking the formal sign-off to the specific evidence artifacts that substantiate it.

**Table 4.5: Attestation Records and Evidence Dependencies**

| ID | Attestation Process (X) | Supporting Evidence (Y) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| att_iso_cert | ISMSCertificationAttestation | dir_ac_01 |
| att_sox_review | SOXComplianceAttestation | dir_crypto_01 |
| att_ops_audit | OperationalSecurityAttestation | dir_ops_01 |
| att_hr_compliance | HRSecurityAttestation | dir_hr_01 |
| att_phy_report | PhysicalSecurityAttestation | dir_phy_01 |

The relational structure ensures that the evidence referenced in the attestation table (`y`) corresponds to the outputs defined in the verification schema, creating a closed loop of governance: Requirement $\rightarrow$ Verification $\rightarrow$ Evidence $\rightarrow$ Attestation.
