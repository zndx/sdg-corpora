# Governance Frameworks: Compliance Mapping and Evidence Management

This chapter delineates the structural alignment between regulatory directives and technical control implementations within a formal governance ontology. It provides a detailed examination of how compliance artifacts—specifically attestations, requirements, and safeguards—are instantiated, related, and audited. The focus is on the precise mapping of Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) requirements and Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) technical safeguards to evidence-generating controls. By defining the relational schema underpinning these entities, this chapter establishes the necessary rigor for audit readiness and automated compliance verification.

## 1. Attestation with Supporting Evidence

### 1.1 Overview and Definition
In the context of formal governance, an attestation represents a definitive assertion or declaration regarding the state of a system or process. It is not merely a statement of intent but a process-derived artifact that must be substantiated by empirical data. According to the governing ontology, an attestation is defined as a process (bfo:Process) that possesses the property of "with supporting evidence." This relationship implies that the validity of the attestation is entirely contingent upon the existence and integrity of the evidence it cites.

The axiom `attestation_with_supporting_evidence` establishes that for any attestation {X}, there must exist a supporting evidence artifact {Y}. In practice, this means that when an auditor or compliance officer signs off on a control, that action (the attestation) must be linked to a specific, retrievable evidence package—such as a log file, a configuration snapshot, or a scan report—that satisfies the requirement.

### 1.2 Evidence Integrity and Audit Trails
The integrity of the attestation process relies on the immutability of the link between the assertion and the evidence. If the evidence is modified, deleted, or rendered inaccessible, the attestation becomes null and void. Therefore, governance frameworks must enforce strict versioning and retention policies for the evidence entities referenced in these attestations.

## 2. PCI DSS Requirement

### 2.1 Regulatory Framework Structure
The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) provides a prescriptive set of requirements for securing cardholder data. Within the ontology, a PCI DSS requirement is modeled as a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` (Information Control Element). It is characterized by the property `pciDssRequirement`, which links the high-level directive to a specific control or sub-requirement.

The axiom `pci_dss_requirement` posits that {X} is a directive that encompasses a specific PCI DSS requirement {Y}. This structure allows for the decomposition of complex mandates into actionable controls. For instance, the broad directive to "maintain a vulnerability management program" is decomposed into specific requirements such as "use antivirus software" or "develop secure systems."

### 2.2 Control Mapping and Dependency
A critical aspect of this ontology is the relational dependency between requirements and controls. The schema defines that a PCI DSS requirement (`x`) references a control with an evidence requirement (`y`). This creates a bidirectional flow where the requirement mandates the control, and the control generates the evidence necessary to satisfy the requirement.

## 3. Control with Evidence Requirement

### 3.1 The Control-to-Evidence Lifecycle
While requirements define *what* must be achieved, controls define *how* it is achieved. A `control_with_evidence_requirement` is a directive that explicitly mandates the generation of evidence. This distinction is vital for automated compliance auditing. A control that operates without producing an audit trail is effectively invisible to the governance framework.

The axiom `control_with_evidence_requirement` asserts that {X} is a directive that requires evidence {Y}. This evidence {Y} is often the specific artifact or log entry that an auditor examines. The ontology models this as a loop: the Control requires Evidence, and that Evidence satisfies a PCI DSS Requirement, which in turn validates the Attestation.

### 3.2 Verification and Validation
The verification of controls involves checking the evidence artifacts for completeness and accuracy. The relational schema enforces that the evidence required by a control (`y`) must correspond to a defined PCI DSS requirement (`t_pci_dss_requirement`). This ensures that controls are not generating evidence in a vacuum; every artifact produced must be traceable back to a regulatory obligation.

The foreign key constraint links `y` back to `t_pci_dss_requirement`, closing the loop between the control execution and the regulatory standard.

## 4. HIPAA Safeguard Technical

### 4.1 Healthcare Security Integration
Organizations that handle both payment card data and Protected Health Information (PHI) must navigate a complex landscape of overlapping regulations. The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) mandates specific technical safeguards to ensure the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of electronic PHI (ePHI).

The axiom `hipaa_safeguard_technical` models a HIPAA safeguard {X} as a directive (`cco:DirectiveICE`) that implements a specific safeguard {Y}. To streamline compliance efforts, this ontology maps HIPAA safeguards directly to the existing control framework defined by PCI DSS. This allows a single control implementation—such as encryption or access logging—to satisfy multiple regulatory obligations simultaneously.

### 4.2 Cross-Standard Harmonization
By mapping HIPAA safeguards to controls with evidence requirements, the framework enables unified reporting. A technical safeguard under HIPAA (e.g., Access Control) is realized through a specific control (e.g., Multi-Factor Authentication), which generates the evidence required by both HIPAA and potentially PCI DSS.

The foreign key constraint ensures that the safeguard (`y`) is mapped to a valid entry in `t_control_with_evidence_requirement`, ensuring that the safeguard is operationally active and generating evidence.

## 5. Requirement Equivalence and Target Specification

### 5.1 Directive Composition
The `requirement_equiv_specifies_target` axiom provides a granular definition of a compliance directive. It asserts that a class {X} is equivalent to a `cco:DirectiveICE` that both specifies a mechanism {Y} and applies to a target asset {Z}. This tripartite structure is essential for scoping compliance assessments. It answers not only what is required (the mechanism) but where it applies (the target).

For example, a directive might specify "Encryption" (the mechanism) and apply to "Customer Database" (the target). Without the target specification, the requirement is ambiguous and cannot be reliably audited.

### 5.2 Asset Scoping and Applicability
The inclusion of the "applies to" ({Z}) slot allows for precise scoping of controls. In large, heterogeneous environments, not all controls apply to all assets. This axiom enables the governance framework to filter requirements based on asset tags, environment types (e.g., Production vs. Development), or data sensitivity levels.
