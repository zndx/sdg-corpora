# Chapter 4: Governance Frameworks and Evidence-Based Compliance Directives

This chapter establishes the formal ontology governing the intersection of regulatory directives, specifically focusing on the structural alignment between Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) mandates and Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) technical safeguards. The discourse herein delineates the axiomatic relationships between attestation processes, evidence generation, and control requirements, providing a deterministic framework for compliance auditing. Readers are advised to refer to the embedded relational mappings for precise entity identification and cross-reference integrity.

## 1. Attestation with Supporting Evidence

### 1.1 Overview and Axiomatic Definition

In the context of governance frameworks, an attestation is formally defined not merely as a declarative statement of compliance, but as a processual entity that is ontologically dependent on the existence of supporting evidence. Drawing upon the Basic Formal Ontology (BFO), the attestation is classified as a process (`bfo:0000015`) that realizes the verification of controls. The axiom `attestation_with_supporting_evidence` posits that for an attestation ({X}) to be valid, it must possess a `withSupportingEvidence` relationship to a specific evidence class ({Y}).

This relationship ensures that governance artifacts are not created in a vacuum; rather, they are anchored to empirical data or documentation derived from compliance activities. In audit practice, this means that a "Statement of Compliance" is invalid without the concurrent submission of audit logs, scan reports, or policy reviews.

## 2. PCI DSS Requirements

### 2.1 Directive Classification

The `pci_dss_requirement` axiom defines a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` (Information and Computing Entity Directive). This classification formalizes the concept that a PCI DSS requirement ({X}) is a directive that inherently contains or references a specific PCI DSS requirement ({Y}). This distinction is crucial for governance systems that must differentiate between the *directive to comply* (the organizational mandate) and the *specific requirement text* (the regulatory standard).

In practice, this allows an organization to map internal policies (the Directive) directly to the external regulatory text (the Requirement). The axiom asserts that the directive is incomplete without the specific requirement property, ensuring that compliance efforts are traceable to the source standard.

### 2.2 Evidence and Control Dependencies

The relational schema for `t_pci_dss_requirement` introduces a complex dependency structure. The requirement ({X}) must reference a control with evidence requirement ({Y}). This creates a chain of custody for compliance: the Requirement points to a Control, and that Control must generate Evidence.

## 3. Control with Evidence Requirement

### 3.1 The Control-Evidence Loop

The axiom `control_with_evidence_requirement` further refines the governance model by asserting that a Control ({X}) is a directive that `requiresEvidence` of a specific type ({Y}). This axiom operationalizes the abstract concept of compliance; it moves the framework from "what must be done" (the Requirement) to "how it is verified" (the Evidence).

Notably, the relational schema for this table exhibits a recursive dependency in the ontology model, where the evidence required ({Y}) is often another PCI DSS requirement or a control artifact. In the schema provided, the foreign key constraint points back to `t_pci_dss_requirement`. This circularity reflects the real-world audit loop: a control is implemented to satisfy a requirement, and the evidence of that control is often dictated by another requirement (e.g., a requirement to encrypt data is satisfied by a control, and the evidence of that encryption is a log required by another standard).

## 4. HIPAA Safeguard Technical

### 4.1 Cross-Standard Interoperability

The `hipaa_safeguard_technical` axiom facilitates the mapping of HIPAA Security Rule safeguards to the existing control framework. Here, a HIPAA safeguard ({X}) is classified as a `cco:DirectiveICE` that implements a `hipaaSafeguard` property ({Y}). This allows organizations to maintain a unified control library where a single technical control (e.g., Encryption) satisfies both a PCI DSS requirement and a HIPAA technical safeguard.

The relational schema links the HIPAA safeguard to a `t_control_with_evidence_requirement`. This implies that the HIPAA safeguard is realized through the execution of a control that generates evidence. This structure is essential for unified compliance audits (e.g., a HITRUST or co-managed audit) where evidence must serve multiple masters.

## 5. Requirement Equivalence and Target Specification

### 5.1 Triadic Directive Structure

The final axiom, `requirement_equiv_specifies_target`, introduces a triadic relationship essential for granular governance. It states that a Requirement ({X}) is equivalent to a `cco:DirectiveICE` that both `specifies` a mechanism ({Y}) and `appliesTo` a target ({Z}). This axiom allows for the precise scoping of directives. It answers not just "what is required?" but "for whom?" and "which specific mechanism?".

This structure is critical for asset-based compliance. It allows the organization to state that "Requirement X applies to Database Server Z and specifies Control Y." This prevents the over-application of controls (e.g., applying PCI DSS strictures to non-cardholder data systems) and ensures audit efficiency.
