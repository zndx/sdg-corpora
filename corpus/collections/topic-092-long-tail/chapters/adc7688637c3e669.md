# Chapter 4: Regulatory Mapping and Evidence Attestation Frameworks

This chapter delineates the formal ontological structures required to map regulatory directives to evidence-based controls within a governance framework. It establishes the necessary relationships between PCI DSS requirements, HIPAA safeguards, and the attestation processes that validate them. The text provides a rigorous examination of the axioms governing these relationships, supported by relational data tables that instantiate the logical dependencies between compliance artifacts.

## 4.1 Attestation with Supporting Evidence

In the context of formal governance, an attestation is not merely a declaration but a distinct process that must be anchored to verifiable artifacts. The axiom `attestation_with_supporting_evidence` posits that an attestation (Class X) is a specific type of process—formally defined as a subclass of `bfo:0000015` (Process)—that possesses the property of `withSupportingEvidence` toward some supporting entity (Class Y). This relationship ensures that every compliance assertion is traceable to a concrete requirement or evidence artifact.

The integrity of an audit relies on this bidirectional linkage. Without the binding of the attestation process to a specific evidence class, the attestation lacks the necessary "grounding" to withstand regulatory scrutiny. The supporting evidence class (Y) serves as the ontological proof point for the process (X). In practice, this means that when an auditor signs off on a compliance report, they are effectively instantiating a process that points directly to the specific regulatory requirement or evidence collection that substantiates the sign-off.

The following table implements this axiom. The primary key (`id`) represents the unique identifier for the attestation process instance. The column `x` denotes the attestation class or process name, while `y` acts as a foreign key referencing the specific PCI DSS requirement that provides the supporting evidence. This structure enforces the constraint that no attestation can exist in isolation; it must reference a valid requirement definition.

## 4.2 PCI DSS Requirement Directives

The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) imposes a set of mandatory controls that must be formalized within an organization's governance ontology. The axiom `pci_dss_requirement` defines a class (X) as a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` (Information Control Element Directive), which holds the specific relationship `pciDssRequirement` with another class (Y). This axiom formalizes the mapping between a high-level directive and the granular requirement it seeks to enforce.

A `DirectiveICE` serves as the governance mechanism or policy document that operationalizes the requirement. For instance, a corporate policy titled "Data Transmission Security" acts as the Directive (X), while the specific PCI DSS mandate regarding the use of strong cryptography acts as the Requirement (Y). The ontology asserts that the directive *is* the implementation vehicle for the requirement. This distinction is critical for audit trails: it separates the *rule* (the PCI requirement) from the *governance action* (the internal directive).

Column `x` represents the directive class or name, and column `y` is a foreign key that references the control mechanism required to satisfy the directive. Notably, this schema establishes a dependency chain where the requirement points to a control, creating a traceable path from policy to technical enforcement.

## 4.3 Controls with Evidence Requirements

To ensure that governance directives are effective, they must be operationalized through controls that generate verifiable proof. The axiom `control_with_evidence_requirement` defines a control class (X), also a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE`, which `requiresEvidence` of type (Y). This axiom captures the "auditability" constraint: a control is only valid if it produces evidence that can be mapped back to the requirement it fulfills.

In this ontological structure, the control acts as the enforcement engine, while the evidence requirement defines the output artifact (e.g., a log file, a configuration snapshot, or a signed document). This relationship is often recursive in complex compliance frameworks; a control may require evidence to satisfy a requirement, which in turn validates the directive. The schema provided for this axiom reflects a circular dependency where the evidence requirement (`y`) references a PCI DSS requirement. This formalism allows the organization to model the scenario where a specific control (e.g., "Log Review") is required to produce evidence that satisfies a specific PCI DSS mandate (e.g., "Track and monitor all access").

This structure ensures that every control implemented is explicitly tied to a compliance obligation.

## 4.4 HIPAA Physical Safeguards

While PCI DSS focuses on payment data, the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) mandates the protection of electronic Protected Health Information (ePHI) through specific safeguards. The axiom `hipaa_safeguard_physical` addresses the physical security domain. It defines a class (X) as a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` that implements a `hipaaSafeguard` of type (Y). This maps internal physical security policies to the specific administrative or physical safeguards outlined in the HIPAA Security Rule.

Physical safeguards are measures to protect physical electronic information systems and related buildings and equipment from natural and environmental hazards, as well as unauthorized intrusion. Examples include facility access controls, workstation use policies, and device and media controls. In the ontology, the directive (X) is the corporate policy enforcing the safeguard (Y). The relational schema links the safeguard directive to a control with evidence requirements, ensuring that the physical safeguard is not merely a theoretical policy but an active control generating audit trails.

## 4.5 HIPAA Technical Safeguards

Complementing physical safeguards, technical safeguards involve the technology and the policy and procedures for its use that protect electronic health information and control access to it. The axiom `hipaa_safeguard_technical` defines a class (X) as a `cco:DirectiveICE` that implements a `hipaaSafeguard` of type (Y). This axiom covers the automated mechanisms used to protect data, such as access control, audit controls, and integrity controls.

The distinction between physical and technical safeguards in the ontology is maintained through the classification of the directive (X) and the specific nature of the safeguard (Y). However, both share the same structural imperative: they must map to controls that generate evidence. This ensures that technical configurations, such as encryption settings or user authentication protocols, are not only deployed but are also subject to continuous monitoring and verification.
