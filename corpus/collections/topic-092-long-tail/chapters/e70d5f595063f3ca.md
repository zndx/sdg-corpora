# Chapter 4: Compliance Frameworks and Evidence-Based Governance

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the structural relationships between regulatory directives, control mechanisms, and the evidentiary artifacts required to establish compliance posture. In the context of modern governance, risk management, and compliance (GRC), organizations must map high-level legal and statutory requirements to specific operational controls. This mapping is not merely administrative but serves as the foundational ontology for automated audit systems and continuous monitoring protocols. The following sections explore the formalization of these relationships, specifically examining how attestation processes rely on Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) requirements, how those requirements necessitate specific controls with evidence, and how Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) safeguards integrate into this unified framework.

## 4.2 Attestation with Supporting Evidence

An attestation represents a formal declaration by a qualified authority regarding the veracity of an organization's compliance status. Within the ontology defined for this framework, an attestation is modeled as a process that possesses supporting evidence. This relationship is critical because it decouples the act of verification from the static state of compliance. The attestation is the event (the process), while the supporting evidence is the object that substantiates the claim.

Formally, the axiom states: `{X} is something that with supporting evidence {Y}`. Here, `X` represents the attestation process itself, and `Y` represents the specific regulatory requirement or artifact being verified. In practice, this means that an auditor cannot perform an attestation in a vacuum; the process is strictly bound to the evidence it evaluates.

The following table illustrates instances of this relationship. The primary key is the unique attestation identifier, while the foreign key establishes the link to the specific PCI DSS requirement that serves as the evidence basis.

**Table 4.1: Attestation Processes and Supporting Evidence**

| id | x | y |
|---|---|---|
| ATT-2023-Q3 | ThirdPartyAuditProcess | PCI-REQ-08 |
| ATT-2023-INT | InternalReviewProcess | PCI-REQ-01 |
| ATT-2023-ROV | AnnualAttestationEvent | PCI-REQ-12 |

In the table above, the entity `ThirdPartyAuditProcess` (X) is the attestation mechanism. It is substantiated by `PCI-REQ-08` (Y), which refers to the requirement regarding identification and authentication. This structure ensures that every attestation event is anchored to a specific, auditable control requirement, preventing vague or ungrounded compliance declarations.

## 4.3 PCI DSS Requirements

The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) provides a prescriptive set of requirements for securing cardholder data. In our ontology, a PCI DSS requirement is classified as a Directive ICE (Information Control Element). It is axiomatically defined as `{X} is something that pci dss requirement {Y}`. This definition implies that the requirement itself is a directive that maps to a broader control framework.

The relational structure for this concept reveals a recursive dependency essential for granular governance. The requirement (X) must reference a control with an evidence requirement (Y). This mapping ensures that for every high-level PCI mandate, there exists a corresponding control mechanism capable of generating the necessary proof of compliance.

**Table 4.2: PCI DSS Requirements and Control Mappings**

| id | x | y |
|---|---|---|
| PCI-REQ-08 | IdentifyAndAuthenticateAccess | CTRL-EV-08 |
| PCI-REQ-01 | InstallAndMaintainFirewall | CTRL-EV-01 |
| PCI-REQ-12 | MaintainInformationSecurityPolicy | CTRL-EV-12 |

As shown in Table 4.2, the requirement `IdentifyAndAuthenticateAccess` (PCI-REQ-08) is directly linked to `CTRL-EV-08`. This foreign key relationship enforces the rule that a requirement is only valid within the system if it is associated with a tangible control implementation. This eliminates the risk of "zombie requirements"—mandates that are documented on paper but have no operational enforcement mechanism.

## 4.4 Control with Evidence Requirement

The operationalization of compliance occurs at the control level. A control is a directive that explicitly requires evidence. The axiom `{X} is something that requires evidence {Y}` captures the bidirectional nature of the compliance framework. While the previous section established that requirements map to controls, this section clarifies that controls map back to requirements, creating a closed loop of verification.

This circular reference is intentional and structurally significant. It ensures that the evidence generated by a control is always relevant to a specific requirement. The schema dictates that the control (X) references the PCI DSS requirement (Y) as the object of its evidence requirement. This mutual dependency between `t_pci_dss_requirement` and `t_control_with_evidence_requirement` creates a rigid chain of custody for compliance data.

**Table 4.3: Controls and Evidence Requirements**

| id | x | y |
|---|---|---|
| CTRL-EV-08 | UserAccessReviewControl | PCI-REQ-08 |
| CTRL-EV-01 | FirewallConfigurationControl | PCI-REQ-01 |
| CTRL-EV-12 | PolicyGovernanceControl | PCI-REQ-12 |

In this configuration, `UserAccessReviewControl` (CTRL-EV-08) is the mechanism (X) that generates evidence. The target of that evidence generation is `PCI-REQ-08` (Y). This structure confirms that the control is not operating in isolation; its specific purpose is to satisfy the evidence demands of the PCI requirement. By populating the table in this manner, we satisfy the foreign key constraint referencing `t_pci_dss_requirement`, ensuring referential integrity across the governance model.

## 4.5 HIPAA Safeguards: Physical

While PCI DSS governs payment data, the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) mandates safeguards for Protected Health Information (PHI). The HIPAA Security Rule is categorized into physical, technical, and administrative safeguards. This section addresses physical safeguards.

The axiom for physical safeguards is `{X} is something that hipaa safeguard {Y}`. Here, `X` represents the specific directive (the safeguard implementation), and `Y` represents the control with evidence requirement to which it adheres. By mapping HIPAA safeguards to the existing control framework (`t_control_with_evidence_requirement`), organizations can unify their compliance architecture. Rather than maintaining separate silos for PCI and HIPAA evidence, the physical safeguards are treated as specific instances of the broader control directives.

**Table 4.4: HIPAA Physical Safeguards**

| id | x | y |
|---|---|---|
| HIPAA-PHY-01 | FacilityAccessControl | CTRL-EV-08 |
| HIPAA-PHY-02 | WorkstationUsePolicy | CTRL-EV-12 |
| HIPAA-PHY-03 | DeviceAndMediaControl | CTRL-EV-01 |

Table 4.4 demonstrates that `FacilityAccessControl` (HIPAA-PHY-01) is a directive that functions as a HIPAA safeguard. It is linked to `CTRL-EV-08`, the User Access Review Control. This linkage suggests that the evidence gathered for reviewing user access (a common control) can simultaneously satisfy the physical access control requirements of HIPAA. This convergence reduces audit fatigue and optimizes the evidence collection process.

## 4.6 HIPAA Safeguards: Technical

Technical safeguards involve the technology and the policies and procedures for its use that protect electronic health information and control access to it. The ontological structure for technical safeguards mirrors that of physical safeguards: `{X} is something that hipaa safeguard {Y}`.

The distinction lies in the implementation. While physical safeguards involve tangible barriers (locks, badges), technical safeguards involve logical access (encryption, authentication). However, both must map back to the core control evidence requirements. This mapping ensures that technical implementations are auditable and that the evidence they produce (logs, configurations, access tokens) is captured within the central governance framework.

**Table 4.5: HIPAA Technical Safeguards**

| id | x | y |
|---|---|---|
| HIPAA-TECH-01 | AccessControlMechanism | CTRL-EV-08 |
| HIPAA-TECH-02 | AuditControlMechanism | CTRL-EV-12 |
| HIPAA-TECH-03 | IntegrityControlMechanism | CTRL-EV-01 |

In Table 4.5, the `AccessControlMechanism` (HIPAA-TECH-01) is mapped to `CTRL-EV-08`. This reinforces the concept that a single control—User Access Review—can support multiple regulatory frameworks (PCI DSS Requirement 8 and HIPAA Technical Safeguard 1). The `AuditControlMechanism` (HIPAA-TECH-02) maps to the Policy Governance Control (CTRL-EV-12), indicating that the technical act of auditing is driven by the governance policy control. By rigorously defining these relationships, the organization ensures that its technical infrastructure is not merely compliant in isolation, but is an integrated part of the enterprise-wide evidence lifecycle.
