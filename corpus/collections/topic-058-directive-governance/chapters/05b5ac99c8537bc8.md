# Chapter 4: Governance Frameworks and the Evidence Chain of Compliance

This chapter delineates the structural relationships between regulatory directives, control implementations, and the evidentiary artifacts required to demonstrate compliance. It establishes a formal ontology for mapping governance obligations—specifically within the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)—to operational controls and verification processes. The following sections define the axiomatic connections between attestation processes, requirement sets, physical safeguards, and the generation of audit evidence, providing a rigorous framework for audit readiness and regulatory alignment.

## 4.1 Attestation with Supporting Evidence

In the context of corporate governance, an attestation is not merely a declarative statement but a formal process (`bfo:0000015`) that substantiates compliance through the citation of specific, verifiable artifacts. The axiom *Attestation with Supporting Evidence* posits that for any attestation to be valid, it must be anchored to a supporting evidence base. This relationship transforms attestation from a subjective assertion into an objective, auditable event. The integrity of the attestation process relies entirely on the traceability and veracity of the evidence it cites.

When an entity asserts compliance with a governance framework, the attestation serves as the nexus between the internal control environment and external regulatory scrutiny. The supporting evidence acts as the proof source, which may include configuration snapshots, audit logs, or third-party assessment reports. Without this linkage, attestation lacks the probative value required by auditors and regulatory bodies.

The following table illustrates the mapping of specific attestation processes to the PCI DSS requirements they substantiate. Each row represents a distinct attestation event where the process (X) validates the requirement through the cited evidence (Y).

| ID | Attestation Process (X) | Supporting Evidence / Requirement (Y) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| ATT-2023-001 | Annual PCI DSS ROC Attestation Process | PCI_DSS_REQ_01 |
| ATT-2023-002 | Quarterly Physical Security Review | PCI_DSS_REQ_09 |
| ATT-2023-003 | Executive Sign-off on Access Controls | PCI_DSS_REQ_07 |
| ATT-2023-004 | Third-Party Penetration Test Attestation | PCI_DSS_REQ_11 |
| ATT-2023-005 | Wireless Network Security Survey | PCI_DSS_REQ_02 |

*Table 4.1: Attestation Process Mapping to PCI DSS Requirements*

In this schema, the primary key (`id`) uniquely identifies the attestation event, while the foreign key (`y`) ensures that every attestation is explicitly tied to a defined requirement within the compliance framework. This structure enforces the discipline that no attestation occurs in a vacuum; it must always reference a specific control objective or regulatory mandate.

## 4.2 PCI DSS Requirement Mapping

The translation of high-level regulatory standards into actionable directives is a critical function of the governance office. The axiom *PCI DSS Requirement* defines a relationship wherein a specific directive (`cco:DirectiveICE`) encompasses or addresses a particular PCI DSS requirement. This mapping is essential for breaking down complex standards into manageable components that can be assigned to responsible owners within the organization.

Directives in this context are formal mandates issued by governance bodies, such as the Information Security Policy or the Network Architecture Standard. These directives operationalize the abstract language of the PCI DSS (e.g., "Install and maintain a firewall configuration") into concrete rules (e.g., "Default deny-all firewall rules must be implemented"). The relational structure here ensures that every organizational directive has a clear lineage to a compliance obligation, thereby justifying its existence and enforcement.

Furthermore, this mapping facilitates gap analysis. By querying the relationship between directives and requirements, compliance officers can quickly identify uncovered requirements or redundant directives. The table below demonstrates how internal governance directives map to specific PCI DSS clauses, creating a defensible chain of authority.

| ID | Internal Directive (X) | PCI DSS Requirement / Control (Y) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| DIR-FW-001 | Enterprise Firewall Configuration Standard | CTRL_EVID_FW_01 |
| DIR-ENC-002 | Data-at-Rest Encryption Policy | CTRL_EVID_ENC_01 |
| DIR-LOG-003 | Centralized Log Management Directive | CTRL_EVID_LOG_01 |
| DIR-ACCESS-004 | Privileged User Access Control | CTRL_EVID_ACCESS_01 |
| DIR-VULN-005 | Vulnerability Management Procedure | CTRL_EVID_VULN_01 |

*Table 4.2: Internal Directives Mapped to Evidence-Based Controls*

The schema enforces a complex dependency where the requirement (`y`) must reference a valid control with evidence requirements. This recursive structure highlights that a requirement is often satisfied only when a specific control is implemented and evidenced. The foreign key constraint (`y`) guarantees that the organization is not merely tracking requirements but is actively linking them to the mechanisms (controls) that generate the necessary proof of compliance.

## 4.3 Control Implementation and Evidence Requirements

A control is only as effective as its ability to demonstrate its own operation. The axiom *Control with Evidence Requirement* asserts that any directive serving as a control (`cco:DirectiveICE`) must necessitate the production of evidence. This moves the organization from a "checkbox" mentality to a continuous monitoring paradigm, where the existence of a control is proven by the artifacts it produces regularly.

This axiom addresses the "long tail" of compliance, where the maintenance of controls often outweighs their initial implementation. By defining that a control requires evidence (`sdg:requiresEvidence`), the framework mandates that processes such as log reviews, access recertifications, and configuration audits are not one-time events but recurring activities with tangible outputs.

The relational schema for this concept creates a bidirectional link with the PCI requirements table, reinforcing the symbiotic relationship between the mandate (the requirement) and the execution (the control). The following table details specific controls and the evidence artifacts they must generate to satisfy the overarching PCI directives.

| ID | Control Directive (X) | Evidence Requirement / PCI Link (Y) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| CTRL_EVID_FW_01 | Next-Generation Firewall Rule Set | PCI_DSS_REQ_01 |
| CTRL_EVID_ENC_01 | AES-256 Database Encryption Module | PCI_DSS_REQ_02 |
| CTRL_EVID_LOG_01 | SIEM Log Aggregation and Retention | PCI_DSS_REQ_03 |
| CTRL_EVID_ACCESS_01 | Active Directory Group Policy Management | PCI_DSS_REQ_04 |
| CTRL_EVID_VULN_01 | Automated Vulnerability Scanning Service | PCI_DSS_REQ_05 |

*Table 4.3: Control Directives and Associated Evidence Artifacts*

In this structure, the primary key identifies the specific control instance, while the foreign key (`y`) links back to the PCI requirement identifier. This circular dependency—where the requirement points to the control and the control points back to the requirement—ensures that the control implementation is strictly scoped to satisfy the specific language of the regulation. It prevents scope creep and ensures that evidence generation is targeted and relevant.

## 4.4 HIPAA Physical Safeguards

While logical controls dominate the cybersecurity landscape, physical security remains a foundational component of regulatory compliance, particularly under HIPAA. The axiom *HIPAA Safeguard Physical* defines the relationship between a directive and a physical safeguard measure. This addresses the specific requirements of the HIPAA Security Rule, which mandates facility access controls, workstation use policies, and device security.

Physical safeguards differ from logical controls in that they involve tangible assets and environmental controls. The mapping of directives to these safeguards ensures that policies regarding "Facility Access" or "Workstation Security" are codified into enforceable standards. These directives must integrate with the broader evidence framework, meaning that physical security events (e.g., badge access logs, visitor records) must be captured as evidence just as digital logs are.

The table below maps specific HIPAA-related directives to the control evidence requirements. This mapping is crucial for organizations that are subject to multiple regulatory frameworks (e.g., a hospital processing credit card payments), as it allows for the consolidation of control assessments.

| ID | HIPAA Physical Safeguard Directive (X) | Control Evidence Requirement (Y) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| HIPAA_PHYS_01 | Facility Access Control and Visitor Log Policy | CTRL_EVID_ACCESS_01 |
| HIPAA_PHYS_02 | Workstation Location and Screen Privacy Protocol | CTRL_EVID_PHYS_01 |
| HIPAA_PHYS_03 | Device and Media Control and Disposal Policy | CTRL_EVID_ENC_01 |
| HIPAA_PHYS_04 | Emergency Mode Operation Plan | CTRL_EVID_LOG_01 |
| HIPAA_PHYS_05 | Security Awareness Training (Physical) | CTRL_EVID_ACCESS_01 |

*Table 4.4: HIPAA Physical Safeguard Mapping to Control Evidence*

The foreign key constraint in this schema (`y`) links the HIPAA safeguard to the generic control evidence table. This design facilitates a unified compliance view, allowing an auditor to verify that a single control (e.g., access logging) satisfies evidence requirements for both PCI DSS (logical access) and HIPAA (physical access). This convergence is essential for efficient governance, reducing the audit burden by demonstrating that a single evidence artifact supports multiple compliance obligations.

## 4.5 Verification and Evidence Production

The final link in the compliance chain is the verification process. The axiom *Verification Produces Evidence* posits that verification is a distinct process (`bfo:0000015`) that actively generates the evidence required by controls. This distinguishes verification from passive monitoring; it is an active, often manual or semi-automated, review process that results in a definitive artifact (e.g., an audit report, a scan result, a manager's sign-off).

Verification processes are the mechanism by which the organization "tests" the controls. Without verification, evidence may be auto-generated and potentially manipulated or erroneous. The verification process adds a layer of human or independent system oversight to ensure the integrity of the evidence. This axiom formalizes the role of the auditor or compliance officer as a producer of governance data.

The relational structure ties the verification process directly to the PCI requirement it fulfills. This ensures that verification activities are not performed arbitrarily but are scheduled and executed to satisfy specific compliance deadlines. The table below outlines key verification processes and the evidence they produce to validate the PCI DSS requirements.

| ID | Verification Process (X) | Evidence Produced / Requirement (Y) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| VER-001 | Quarterly External Vulnerability Scan | PCI_DSS_REQ_11 |
| VER-002 | Annual Penetration Test (Internal/External) | PCI_DSS_REQ_11 |
| VER-003 | Firewall Configuration Review | PCI_DSS_REQ_01 |
| VER-004 | Critical File Change Detection Monitoring | PCI_DSS_REQ_10 |
| VER-005 | Wireless Analyzer Survey | PCI_DSS_REQ_02 |

*Table 4.5: Verification Processes and Resulting Evidence*

The schema enforces that every verification process (`x`) must result in evidence that satisfies a requirement (`y`). This creates a closed loop: the requirement mandates a control; the control requires evidence; the verification process produces that evidence; and the attestation process cites that evidence. This rigorous chain of custody and logic provides the high assurance demanded by modern regulatory frameworks.
