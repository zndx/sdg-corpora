# Governance Frameworks and Evidence-Based Compliance Mechanisms

This chapter delineates the structural and functional relationships between regulatory directives, control implementations, and the evidentiary artifacts required to demonstrate compliance within a formal governance framework. The scope encompasses the integration of Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) requirements, Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) physical safeguards, and the verification processes that bind these elements into a coherent audit trail. The discussion is grounded in a formal ontology, mapping specific classes of directives to their evidence requirements and verification mechanisms to ensure rigorous adherence to regulatory standards.

## 1. Attestation with Supporting Evidence

In the context of formal governance, attestation is defined not merely as a declaration of compliance, but as a processual entity that substantiates its claims through direct reference to supporting evidence. Ontologically, attestation is classified as a `Process` (BFO:0000015), which implies it occurs over time and has a distinct beginning and end. However, the validity of this process is contingent upon its relationship to specific evidentiary artifacts. The axiom `attestation_with_supporting_evidence` posits that for an attestation to be valid, it must possess supporting evidence that maps directly to a specific requirement.

This relationship is critical in audit scenarios where an independent assessor must verify that a declared state of compliance is backed by empirical data. For instance, a Chief Information Security Officer (CISO) issuing an attestation regarding network security must be able to point to specific logs, configuration files, or audit reports that substantiate that claim. Without this linkage, the attestation is considered hollow and fails to meet the burden of proof required by standards such as SOC 2 or ISO 27001.

The structural integrity of this relationship is maintained by ensuring that the evidence cited (Y) is not arbitrary but is a recognized instance of a requirement class. In the relational data model, this creates a dependency where the attestation record must reference a valid identifier from the PCI DSS requirement tables, ensuring traceability from the high-level declaration down to the granular control implementation.

## 2. PCI DSS Requirements and Directives

The PCI DSS framework is constructed upon a hierarchy of directives. These directives are formalized within the ontology as subclasses of `cco:DirectiveICE` (Information Control Element). The axiom `pci_dss_requirement` establishes that a specific class (X)—often representing a policy, procedure, or technical standard—is a directive that fulfills a specific PCI DSS requirement (Y).

This mapping is essential for translating the high-level, prescriptive language of the PCI DSS standard into actionable governance artifacts within an organization. For example, the requirement to "change vendor-supplied defaults" is not merely a suggestion; it is a directive that must be instantiated as a specific configuration standard or hardening guide. The ontology captures this by linking the abstract requirement to the concrete directive class.

Furthermore, the relational schema indicates a complex dependency where the PCI DSS requirement (Y) references a `control_with_evidence_requirement`. This recursive structure implies that a requirement is rarely satisfied in isolation. Instead, the satisfaction of a PCI requirement is dependent upon the implementation of a control that itself generates evidence. This creates a chain of custody: the Directive mandates the Requirement, which is satisfied by a Control, which produces Evidence.

## 3. Control Implementation and Evidence Requirements

Controls are the operational mechanisms put in place to mitigate risk and ensure compliance with directives. The axiom `control_with_evidence_requirement` defines a control (X) as a directive that specifically requires evidence (Y). This distinction is crucial: a control exists in a compliant state only if it can demonstrate its operation through artifacts.

In audit practice, a control without evidence is considered ineffective. For instance, a "Password Policy" (the control) is ineffective if there are no system logs or access reviews (the evidence) demonstrating that the policy is enforced. The ontology formalizes this by classifying controls as `cco:DirectiveICE` that possess the property of `sdg:requiresEvidence`.

The relational schema for this axiom presents a circular reference to `t_pci_dss_requirement`. This circularity is not an error but a reflection of the compliance lifecycle: a Control requires Evidence to prove that a PCI Requirement is met, and that PCI Requirement validates the existence of the Control. This feedback loop ensures that controls are not implemented in a vacuum but are always aligned with the specific regulatory obligations they are designed to satisfy.

## 4. HIPAA Physical Safeguards

While PCI DSS focuses heavily on technical and operational controls related to cardholder data, the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) mandates specific safeguards regarding the physical access to electronic Protected Health Information (ePHI). The axiom `hipaa_safeguard_physical` defines a class (X) as a directive that implements a HIPAA safeguard (Y).

This section of the ontology addresses the physical security domain, encompassing facility access controls, workstation usage, and device management. Like PCI DSS requirements, HIPAA safeguards are modeled as directives (`cco:DirectiveICE`). However, they are distinct in their domain of application, focusing on the "physical" layer of the security stack as opposed to the logical or network layers.

The relational structure links the HIPAA safeguard (Y) to a `control_with_evidence_requirement`. This implies that physical safeguards must be backed by the same rigor of evidence as technical controls. For example, a policy requiring "restricted access to data centers" (the safeguard) must be supported by a control such as "badge reader logs" (the evidence requirement). This mapping ensures that physical security is not treated as a soft, procedural exception but is integrated into the broader evidence-based governance framework.

## 5. Verification Processes and Evidence Production

The final component of the governance framework is the verification process. While controls operate continuously to maintain security, verification is the distinct activity of observing, testing, and documenting the state of those controls. The axiom `verification_produces_evidence` classifies a verification process (X) as a `Process` (BFO:0000015) that produces evidence (Y).

Verification activities include penetration testing, vulnerability scanning, log reviews, and access audits. These activities are generative; they create the artifacts that attestations rely upon. Without the verification process, there is no evidence to support the control, and consequently, no basis for the attestation.

In the relational model, the evidence produced (Y) by the verification process references a `t_pci_dss_requirement`. This establishes the purpose of the verification: it is not an arbitrary exercise, but a targeted activity designed to generate proof for a specific requirement. For example, a "Quarterly Firewall Rule Review" (Verification) produces a "Rule Cleanup Report" (Evidence), which satisfies the PCI DSS requirement for reviewing firewall rules at least once every six months.
