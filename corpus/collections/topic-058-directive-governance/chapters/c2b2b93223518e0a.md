# Chapter 4: Governance Frameworks and Evidence-Based Compliance

This chapter delineates the structural relationships between regulatory directives, technical controls, and the evidentiary artifacts required to demonstrate compliance within a formal governance framework. It establishes the ontological bindings between high-level policy mandates—such as those found in the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)—and the operational mechanisms that fulfill them. The discussion proceeds from the abstract definition of attestation to the concrete implementation of physical safeguards, emphasizing the bidirectional dependencies between requirements and the controls that satisfy them.

## 4.1 Attestation with Supporting Evidence

In the context of formal audit and compliance, an attestation is not merely a declaration of adherence to policy; it is a process entity (bfo:0000015) that is ontologically dependent on the existence of specific, verifiable artifacts. The axiom "Attestation with Supporting Evidence" posits that for any attestation to be valid, it must reference a corpus of evidence that substantiates the claim. This relationship prevents "empty" attestations—assertions of compliance that lack grounding in observed reality—by enforcing a structural dependency where the attestation instance (X) holds a property `withSupportingEvidence` that necessitates a corresponding requirement or evidence class (Y).

From a governance perspective, this construct mandates that auditors and compliance officers cannot validate a control framework without first identifying the specific evidence objects that satisfy the regulatory query. The attestation serves as the logical wrapper, often represented by a signed report or an executive certification, which aggregates these underlying evidence pointers. In practice, this means that when an organization asserts compliance with a specific control objective, the assertion must be intrinsically linked to the data objects—logs, configurations, or scan results—that prove the control is operating effectively.

## 4.2 PCI DSS Requirements

The PCI DSS Requirements function as the specific directive classes (cco:DirectiveICE) that operationalize broader security policies within the payment card industry. The axiom defining this relationship asserts that a directive (X) encompasses a specific PCI DSS requirement (Y). This is the mechanism by which the abstract goal of "data security" is translated into actionable mandates, such as maintaining a firewall configuration or encrypting transmission of cardholder data.

These requirements are not isolated text strings but are nodes within a larger graph of compliance obligations. They serve as the bridge between the governance layer (attestations) and the operational layer (controls). By defining a PCI DSS requirement as a subclass of a DirectiveICE, the ontology allows these requirements to inherit properties common to all directives—such as versioning, effective dates, and ownership—while retaining the specific attributes necessary for PCI compliance validation. The critical structural feature here is that the requirement itself points to a control implementation, creating a chain of custody from the written standard to the technical enforcement.

## 4.3 Controls with Evidence Requirements

To satisfy the directives outlined in the previous section, organizations implement specific controls. However, the existence of a control is insufficient for compliance; the control must generate evidence of its operation. The axiom "Control with Evidence Requirement" formalizes this by stating that a directive or control (X) `requiresEvidence` (Y). This creates a rigorous feedback loop: the control implements the requirement, and the evidence validates the control.

This relationship is foundational for continuous monitoring and automated compliance. By explicitly defining what evidence a control must produce, the framework allows for automated ingestion of logs, state snapshots, or other telemetry to verify status in real-time. For instance, a "Password Rotation Control" is not merely a policy; it is a directive that requires the evidence of "Timestamp of Last Password Change" for all user accounts. The ontology treats the evidence requirement as a first-class object, ensuring that the data structures needed for audit are preserved alongside the operational logic of the control.

The `t_control_with_evidence_requirement` table demonstrates this dependency. The `x` column identifies the specific control instance, while `y` identifies the PCI DSS requirement that serves as the evidence standard or the specific evidence artifact class required. Note the referential integrity: the evidence requirement (`y`) must correspond to a defined PCI requirement, linking the technical implementation back to the regulatory text.

## 4.4 HIPAA Physical Safeguards

While PCI DSS often focuses on technical and operational controls, the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) imposes stringent requirements for physical access to electronic Protected Health Information (ePHI). The axiom "HIPAA Safeguard Physical" describes a directive (X) that implements a HIPAA safeguard (Y). This maps the legal requirements of the Security Rule (specifically the Physical Safeguards section) to tangible facility controls such as door locks, security cameras, and badge readers.

In a unified governance framework, physical safeguards are treated with the same ontological rigor as digital controls. A "Facility Access Log" is not merely a list of names; it is the instantiation of a HIPAA safeguard directive (Contingency Operations or Access Control). By modeling this as a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE`, the framework ensures that physical security is auditable within the same system as network security. This allows for a holistic risk assessment where a failure in physical access control can be correlated with a potential compromise of digital assets, satisfying the "addressable" and "required" implementation specifications of the HIPAA Security Rule.

The `t_hipaa_safeguard_physical` table maps specific HIPAA safeguard directives to the underlying control mechanisms that generate evidence. The foreign key (`y`) links the safeguard to a control with an evidence requirement, ensuring that the physical safeguard is actively monitored and recorded, rather than passively existing.

## 4.5 Verification Producing Evidence

The final concept in this chapter addresses the dynamic process of verification. Verification is distinct from the control itself; it is the act of checking the control's state or output. The axiom "Verification Produces Evidence" asserts that a verification process (X), which is a temporal occurrence (bfo:0000015), `producesEvidence` (Y). This evidence is the output artifact—such as an audit report, a scan result file, or a compliance checklist—that is subsequently consumed by the attestation layer.

This distinction is crucial for maintaining the integrity of the compliance chain. If a control runs but is not verified, no evidence is generated for the attestation. If a verification is performed but does not produce a persistent artifact (Y), the compliance chain is broken. This axiom ensures that verification activities are not merely theoretical exercises but are concrete data-generation events. It supports the "continuous compliance" model by treating verification as an industrial process with inputs (control states) and outputs (evidence artifacts).

The `t_verification_produces_evidence` table captures these events. The `x` column represents the specific verification activity (e.g., a scheduled audit or a manual review), while `y` references the PCI DSS requirement that the produced evidence satisfies. This links the procedural act of verification directly to the regulatory requirement it fulfills.
