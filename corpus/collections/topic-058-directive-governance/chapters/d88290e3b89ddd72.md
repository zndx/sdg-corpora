# Chapter 7: Regulatory Compliance and Evidence Verification Frameworks

## 7.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the structural and functional relationships between regulatory directives, specifically within the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), and the evidentiary mechanisms required to substantiate compliance. The text establishes a formal ontology for mapping governance controls to verification processes, emphasizing the necessity of auditable data trails. By defining the axiomatic links between attestation, requirement specifications, and evidence generation, this framework provides a reproducible methodology for auditing complex information security environments. The following sections decompose these relationships into discrete ontological components, supported by relational data schemas that enforce referential integrity across compliance artifacts.

## 7.2 Attestation with Supporting Evidence

In the context of formal governance, attestation is not merely a declarative statement but a distinct process (BFO:Process) that must be anchored in objective reality. The ontology defines attestation as a class of activity that possesses the characteristic of "with supporting evidence." This implies that an attestation event is ontologically dependent on the existence of artifacts that verify the claims made therein. Without these artifacts, the attestation is considered null or void from an audit perspective.

The relationship between the attestation process and the evidence is causal and dependency-based. The attestation entity serves as the claimant, while the evidence entity serves as the substrate for validation. In practice, this means that when an auditor signs off on a compliance report, that action (the attestation) must be traceable to specific data points, logs, or configuration states that satisfy the criteria of the standard being audited.

## 7.3 PCI DSS Requirements

The PCI DSS framework is operationalized through a series of directives (cco:DirectiveICE). These directives are not standalone text blocks but are ontologically structured as classes that possess specific requirement properties. The axiom "PCI DSS requirement" establishes that a directive is defined by its mapping to a specific control or sub-requirement within the standard. This mapping allows for the decomposition of high-level mandates into actionable, verifiable components.

From a data modeling perspective, this structure facilitates granular querying of compliance obligations. By treating requirements as distinct entities linked to broader directives, organizations can automate the mapping of technical controls to regulatory text. This is critical for maintaining "continuous compliance" where changes in the IT infrastructure are immediately cross-referenced against the applicable requirement set to detect gaps.

## 7.4 Control with Evidence Requirement

To satisfy the directives outlined in the previous section, an organization must implement controls. However, the mere existence of a control is insufficient for governance purposes; the control must be instrumented to produce evidence. The axiom "Control with Evidence Requirement" posits that a directive (cco:DirectiveICE) is characterized by the necessity to produce evidence. This creates a bidirectional flow where the requirement demands the control, and the control generates the evidence required by the attestation layer.

This ontological structure highlights the "evidence lifecycle." A control is instantiated (e.g., a firewall rule), it operates within the environment, and it generates logs or states (evidence). This evidence is then consumed by the verification process. The schema defined here often exhibits a recursive or mutual dependency with PCI requirements, as the evidence required by one control may serve to satisfy multiple requirements, or a single requirement may necessitate evidence from multiple controls.

## 7.5 HIPAA Safeguard Physical

While PCI DSS governs payment card data, HIPAA mandates the protection of Protected Health Information (PHI). The "HIPAA Safeguard Physical" axiom addresses the physical security requirements of the HIPAA Security Rule. This axiom classifies physical safeguards as directives (cco:DirectiveICE) that implement specific safeguards. These safeguards are distinct from logical or administrative controls, focusing on the physical access to facilities, hardware, and workstations.

In a unified governance framework, physical safeguards must be integrated into the broader evidence ecosystem. This means that physical access logs, facility maintenance records, and hardware inventories must be treated as formal evidence artifacts. The relational schema links these physical safeguards to the control evidence requirements, ensuring that physical security is not managed in a silo but is part of the enterprise-wide compliance posture.

This mapping illustrates the cross-walking of regulatory frameworks, where a single control mechanism (such as access revocation or inventory control) serves to satisfy both PCI DSS and HIPAA obligations.*

## 7.6 Verification Produces Evidence

The final component of the compliance ontology is the verification process. While controls operate continuously, verification is the distinct act of observing, testing, or validating those controls. The axiom "Verification Produces Evidence" defines verification as a process (bfo:0000015) that yields evidence. This is the mechanism by which raw data (logs, states) is converted into auditable evidence.

Verification activities include automated vulnerability scans, manual penetration tests, log reviews, and physical inspections. The output of these processes is not merely a pass/fail status but a tangible artifact—a scan report, a signed checklist, or a capture file. This axiom closes the loop: the verification process produces the evidence that supports the control, which satisfies the requirement, which enables the attestation.
