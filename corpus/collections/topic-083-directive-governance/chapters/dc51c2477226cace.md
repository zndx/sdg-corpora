# Chapter 4: Governance Directives and Evidence Correlation

## 4.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter delineates the structural relationships between governance directives, procedural verifications, and the evidentiary artifacts required to establish compliance within a regulated framework. The text establishes a formal ontology for mapping the lifecycle of control objectives, from their jurisdictional enactment through to the generation of supporting audit trails. It is intended for compliance architects, internal auditors, and information security governance officers who require a deterministic method for linking policy requirements to specific evidence artifacts. The following sections define the axiomatic relationships between policy classes, verification processes, and evidence types, supported by relational data schemas that enforce referential integrity across the governance domain.

## 4.2 Policy Supersedence

### 4.2.1 Conceptual Definition

In the context of dynamic regulatory environments, governance directives are rarely static; they evolve to address emerging threats or legislative changes. The axiom of **Policy Supersedence** defines a directed relationship between two classes of directives where the successor class (X) explicitly overrides and replaces the predecessor class (Y). This relationship is critical for maintaining the currency of compliance frameworks. When a new directive is enacted, it must be formally mapped to the legacy directive it renders obsolete. This mapping ensures that control repositories are purged of obsolete requirements and that audit efforts are focused solely on active mandates.

### 4.2.2 Application and Hierarchy

The application of supersedence is not merely a versioning exercise but a legal assertion of authority. A superseding directive inherits the scope of the predecessor but modifies the obligations. From an ontological perspective, both the successor and predecessor are subclasses of `cco:DirectiveICE` (Information Control Element). The relationship `sdg:supersedes` establishes a hard link that prevents conflicting interpretations during an audit. For instance, a data privacy regulation updated to reflect cross-border data transfer laws will supersede the previous version that restricted all transfers. The governance framework must explicitly record this transition to validate that historical evidence was generated under the correct version of the directive at the time of execution.

The columns `x` and `y` represent the successor and predecessor directives, respectively. ## 4.3 Verification and Evidence Production

### 4.3.1 Conceptual Definition

Verification is a fundamental process within the governance lifecycle, defined formally as a subclass of `bfo:Process`. The axiom **Verification Produces Evidence** posits that the execution of a verification activity necessitates the creation of a distinct artifact (Y). This artifact serves as the objective proof that the process occurred. Unlike a control, which is a directive or rule, a verification is an active event—such as a penetration test, an audit interview, or an automated configuration scan—that yields a tangible output.

### 4.3.2 Evidence Generation Mechanisms

The integrity of the governance framework relies on the immutable link between the verification process and its output. If a verification process occurs without producing evidence, it is effectively non-existent from an audit perspective. The evidence produced (Y) may take various forms, including logs, reports, or certificates. The ontology classifies this relationship as `sdg:producesEvidence`, creating a dependency where the existence of the evidence validates the completion of the process. For example, the verification process "Quarterly Vulnerability Scan" must produce the evidence artifact "Q3_Vulnerability_Report.pdf" to satisfy the requirement.

## 4.4 Attestation and Supporting Evidence

### 4.4.1 Conceptual Definition

While verification produces raw data, **Attestation** is the formal mechanism by which a responsible party asserts that a state of affairs is true, supported by that data. The axiom **Attestation with Supporting Evidence** defines a relationship where an attestation process (X) relies upon specific evidence artifacts (Y) to substantiate the claim. Attestation is distinct from verification; it is a declarative act, often signed or authorized, that consumes evidence rather than producing it. This class is also a subclass of `bfo:Process`, emphasizing that attestation is an activity occurring at a specific point in time.

### 4.4.2 The Chain of Custody

The strength of an attestation is directly proportional to the validity of its supporting evidence. In a mature governance framework, an attestation cannot exist in a vacuum; it must reference the specific artifacts generated by verification processes. This relationship creates a chain of custody: Verification produces Evidence, which in turn supports Attestation. For example, a Chief Information Security Officer (CISO) attesting to "System Hardening Compliance" must reference the specific "Hardening Check Reports" as supporting evidence. The ontology captures this via the `sdg:withSupportingEvidence` property.

## 4.5 Control Evidence Requirements

### 4.5.1 Conceptual Definition

Controls are the specific directives or rules implemented within an organization to mitigate risk. The axiom **Control with Evidence Requirement** mandates that for a control (X) to be considered effective, it must require the generation or preservation of specific evidence (Y). This is a prescriptive relationship: the control definition itself contains a clause specifying the evidence that must be retained. Unlike verification, which is an event, a control is a standing directive (subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE`).

### 4.5.2 Defining Control Objectives

The explicit linkage between a control and its required evidence is the cornerstone of audit readiness. When a control is defined—such as "Require Multi-Factor Authentication for Remote Access"—it must simultaneously define the evidence requirement, such as "MFA Authentication Logs." This relationship (`sdg:requiresEvidence`) ensures that control owners understand precisely what documentation is necessary to prove the control is operating effectively. It shifts the paradigm from passive compliance to active evidence generation.

## 4.6 Policy Jurisdiction

### 4.6.1 Conceptual Definition

Governance directives do not apply universally; their scope is constrained by legal, geographic, or functional boundaries. The axiom **Policy with Jurisdiction** formalizes this by linking a directive (X) to a specific jurisdiction (Y). This relationship (`sdg:hasJurisdiction`) is essential for multinational organizations that must navigate a complex web of conflicting regulations. A policy defined for the European Union may not be appropriate for the Asia-Pacific region. The jurisdiction acts as a scoping constraint for the directive.

### 4.6.2 Scope and Authority

Jurisdiction can be defined geographically (e.g., "California," "European Union") or functionally (e.g., "Federal Agencies," "Payment Card Industry"). By explicitly mapping a policy to its jurisdiction, the governance framework can automatically filter relevant controls during an audit. This prevents the misapplication of regulatory requirements. For instance, a "CCPA Data Handling Policy" has the jurisdiction of "California Residents," whereas a "GDPR Processing Policy" has the jurisdiction of "EEA Data Subjects." The ontology treats the policy as a `cco:DirectiveICE` and the jurisdiction as the domain of applicability.
