# Chapter 12: Artifact Taxonomy and Brand Attribution in Municipal Asset Management

## 12.1 Scope and Preamble

This chapter establishes the formal ontological framework for the classification, identification, and brand attribution of physical assets within the municipal infrastructure inventory. In accordance with the compliance standards set forth in the 2024 Municipal Governance Handbook, all tangible assets subject to depreciation, maintenance scheduling, or safety inspection must be rigorously categorized as Artifacts and mapped to their respective manufacturing origins. The primary objective of this taxonomy is to ensure data integrity across the City’s Asset Management System (AMS), facilitating precise audit trails and liability assignment. The following sections detail the implementation of the `cco:Artifact` class and the `sdg:hasBrand` object property, providing the necessary semantic structure to support regulatory reporting and inter-departmental resource allocation.

## 12.2 The Artifact Class (cco:Artifact)

### 12.2.1 Definition and Ontological Status

Within the context of the city’s digital twin and inventory registry, an Artifact is formally defined as a distinct, enduring entity that is the product of a fabrication process. Under the Common Core Ontology (cco), the class `cco:Artifact` serves as the superclass for all discrete physical objects that have been intentionally created by one or more agents. This definition excludes naturally occurring objects—such as geological formations or unmodified biological matter—unless they have been significantly altered through human intervention to serve a specific function.

The classification of an entity as an `cco:Artifact` is the prerequisite for its entry into the fixed asset ledger. For the purposes of the Department of Public Works (DPW) and the Department of Information Technology (DoIT), this categorization triggers specific compliance workflows, including lifecycle tracking, depreciation calculation, and mandatory safety inspection scheduling. It is imperative that asset managers distinguish between the Artifact itself (the physical object) and the Information Carrier (the record of the object), a distinction that maintains logical consistency in the relational database schema.

### 12.2.2 Operational Examples

To illustrate the application of this class, consider the assets typically encountered during a municipal facility audit. A "Traffic Control Cabinet" installed at an intersection is an `cco:Artifact` because it is a manufactured object designed to house electronic controllers. Similarly, a "Hydraulic Lift" utilized in the Central Maintenance Garage is an `cco:Artifact`. These entities are not merely "equipment"; they are specific instantiations of artifact classes that possess defined properties and capabilities.

The ontological commitment to classifying these items as artifacts allows the city to enforce constraints on their management. For instance, an artifact cannot exist without a creation event, and it must be spatially located at a specific site. This rigor prevents the entry of vague or abstract concepts—such as "safety protocols" or "budget allocations"—into the physical asset tracking system, thereby preserving the sanctity of the inventory data.

## 12.3 Brand Attribution (sdg:hasBrand)

### 12.3.1 The Relational Property

The second foundational concept for asset governance is the attribution of a Brand to an Artifact. This relationship is captured by the object property `sdg:hasBrand`. In the Manchester Syntax of the Web Ontology Language (OWL), this relationship is axiomatized as a restriction: a specific class of Artifact must have a Brand that is a member of a specific class of Organization or Commercial Entity.

The verbalization of this axiom is: "{X} is something that has brand {Y}". In this context, {X} represents the specific class of the Artifact (e.g., a specific model or type of equipment), and {Y} represents the Brand class (e.g., the manufacturer or the parent company). This relationship is not merely descriptive; it is functional. The brand attribution determines the warranty terms, the availability of replacement parts, and the applicable safety recalls. Consequently, the `sdg:hasBrand` property is a mandatory data field for all high-value and safety-critical artifacts in the system.

### 12.3.2 Compliance and Verification

Accurate brand attribution is a critical component of compliance auditing. During a "City Inspections" review, officials verify that the installed artifacts match the brands specified in the procurement contracts. Discrepancies—such as the installation of an artifact associated with a non-approved brand—constitute a violation of procurement compliance and may void service level agreements (SLAs).

For example, if the procurement specification dictates that "Fire Alarm Control Panels" must be of the brand "SafeTechAlerts," but the installation log indicates a brand of "GenericSecurityInc," the asset is flagged for immediate review. The `sdg:hasBrand` property thus serves as a control mechanism to ensure that the physical reality of the city's infrastructure aligns with the contractual and regulatory framework governing its acquisition.

## 12.4 Relational Mapping of Product-Brand Associations

### 12.4.1 Schema Implementation

To operationalize the axioms described in Sections 12.2 and 12.3, the data architecture team has deployed a relational table designated as `t_schemaorg_product_brand`. This table embodies the axiom `schemaorg_product_brand`, which asserts that a Product (our Artifact) has a Brand. The table structure is designed to map the specific class of the artifact (`x`) to the specific class of the brand (`y`).

The primary key of this table is the `id` column, a unique identifier (VARCHAR) for each specific product-brand assertion. The column `x` holds the name of the Artifact class, while the column `y` holds the name of the Brand class. The constraint `y VARCHAR(255) NOT NULL` enforces the ontological requirement that every valid Artifact in this context must be associated with a Brand. There are no foreign key constraints within this specific table definition, implying that `x` and `y` function as references to external class taxonomies rather than pointers to instance tables within this specific schema fragment.

## 12.5 Audit Verification and Data Governance

### 12.5.1 Verification Protocols

The integrity of the `t_schemaorg_product_brand` table is subject to quarterly audit protocols. Auditors from the City Comptroller’s office perform cross-referencing checks between the physical asset tags attached to artifacts and the digital records stored in the AMS. The verification process confirms that the brand listed in column `y` matches the brand nameplate physically affixed to the artifact identified in column `x`.

Discrepancies identified during these audits—such as a `StreetlightLuminaireLED` physically branded as "Current" (a division of GE) but recorded as `PhilipsLighting`—trigger a remediation workflow. This workflow involves updating the digital record to reflect the physical reality or, if the physical installation was in error, initiating a replacement order to align the installation with the approved brand standard. This rigorous verification process is essential for maintaining the "single source of truth" required for effective governance.

### 12.5.3 Governance Framework Implications

The consistent application of the `schemaorg_product_brand` axiom across these diverse domains demonstrates the flexibility of the ontological approach. By standardizing the relationship between the Artifact (`x`) and the Brand (`y`), the municipality creates a unified data model that supports high-level analytics. City managers can query the database to identify all assets associated with a specific brand—a capability that proves invaluable during large-scale product recalls or when negotiating enterprise-wide maintenance contracts.

Furthermore, this structured data supports the generation of the "Ministerial Diary" and "Committee Reports" referenced in administrative documents. When the Committee on Housing and Public Works requests an inventory of all assets requiring safety inspection, the query engine leverages the `t_schemaorg_product_brand` table to filter results not just by location, but by the manufacturer's safety compliance history. This evidence-anchored approach to asset management transforms raw inventory data into actionable governance intelligence.
