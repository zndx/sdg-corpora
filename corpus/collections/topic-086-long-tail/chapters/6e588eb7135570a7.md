# Chapter 4: Structural Governance of Compliance Artifacts and Directives

This chapter delineates the formal ontological structures governing the definition, composition, and constraint modeling of compliance artifacts within an enterprise information system. It provides a rigorous examination of the intersection between physical or digital artifacts and the regulatory directives they encapsulate, establishing the logical schema necessary for automated compliance auditing. The content herein defines the axiomatic relationships between artifacts, their constituent parts, and the governance directives that specify their operational parameters, including the cardinality constraints that enforce segregation of duties and redundancy requirements.

## 4.1 Artifact Composition and Part-Whole Intersection

The foundational unit of governance is the artifact, defined not merely as a standalone object but as an entity that gains its semantic meaning through its composition. In this framework, an artifact is formally defined as the intersection of the class `cco:Artifact` and the object property `bfo:0000051` (has part). This intersection implies that for an entity to be classified as a specific type of governed artifact, it must necessarily contain specific component parts. This is not a mere correlation; it is an existential dependency. If the component part is absent, the entity ceases to satisfy the conditions of the defined class.

This axiom is critical for distinguishing between generic objects and governed instruments. For example, a "Policy Document" is only valid as such if it contains specific "Control Clauses." The structure ensures that decomposition is built into the identity of the object. When modeling these relationships, the primary key serves as the unique identifier for the composite artifact, while the foreign key establishes a mandatory dependency on the directive or part entity it encapsulates.

This mechanism prevents the creation of hollow governance documents that lack enforceable content.

## 4.2 Directive Specification and Target Application

While artifacts provide the structural container, directives provide the operative logic. A directive is modeled as a subclass of `cco:DirectiveICE` (Information Control Element) that is characterized by two essential properties: it must `specify` a requirement and it must `applyTo` a target. This tripartite structure—Directive, Specification, and Target—forms the semantic bridge between abstract policy and concrete implementation.

The axiom governing this relationship asserts that a directive is equivalent to the intersection of a directive class, a specification relationship, and an application relationship. This means a directive cannot exist in a vacuum; it must explicitly articulate what it specifies (often a control procedure or another artifact) and the scope of its application (the target system, role, or data entity). This formalism allows for precise traceability: from the high-level directive down to the specific artifact it governs, and outward to the operational target it affects.

This structure ensures that directives are not merely textual statements but are active, bound relationships within the ontology.

## 4.3 Maximum Cardinality Constraints

In governance frameworks, limiting the scope of authority or the number of assigned actors is often as critical as defining the authority itself. The axiom for maximum cardinality constraints establishes a ceiling on the number of relationships a specific class may participate in regarding a particular property. Formally, this is expressed as a subclass restriction where Class X is constrained to have a maximum of 3 relationships of property p with Class Y.

This constraint is typically employed to enforce segregation of duties or to limit the blast radius of a specific configuration. By capping the relationship count at three, the framework prevents the concentration of governance capabilities in a single node or ensures that a specific directive does not become over-applied across too many disparate targets, which would dilute its effectiveness or auditability. In the context of relational integrity, this constraint is validated at the application layer to ensure that no more than three rows exist in the linking table that associate a specific instance of X with instances of Y via property p.

Consider the entry `CON-MAX-01`. It dictates that the `SystemAdministratorRole` (x) can have a maximum of three associations with the `AccessControlDirective` (y). Practically, this might translate to a rule that no more than three individual administrators may be assigned the "super-user" privileges defined by `DIR-ACC-001` at any given time. This is a direct implementation of the principle of least privilege and control distribution. The foreign key to `t_requirement_equiv_specifies_target` ensures that the constraint is tied to a valid, defined directive within the system.

## 4.4 Minimum Cardinality Constraints

Conversely to maximum limits, minimum cardinality constraints ensure redundancy, reliability, and consensus. This axiom stipulates that a Class X must have a minimum of two relationships of property p with Class Y. This is a subclass restriction, meaning that if an entity is a member of Class X, it *must* satisfy the condition of having at least two links to Class Y.

This pattern is ubiquitous in high-availability and compliance architectures requiring "four-eyes principles" or dual control. For instance, a critical safety protocol might require verification by at least two distinct officers. Similarly, a data backup strategy might require that a specific directive applies to at least two distinct storage locations to ensure survivability. The ontology enforces this by requiring that the instantiation of Class X is invalid unless the count of relationships to Class Y meets the threshold of two.
